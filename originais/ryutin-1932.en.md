>
> 1. The testimony of M.N. Ryutin himself. He never, anywhere, under any circumstances, denied his authorship. On the contrary, during the investigation of the "Leninist Union" case in 1932 and later, during interrogations in 1936, he claimed to have prepared the main documents himself.

Marx , in his letter to Kugelmann, says: "History would have a very mystical character if 'accidents' played no role. Accidents, of course, are themselves an integral part of the general course of development, balanced by other accidents. But acceleration and deceleration depend to a large extent on these 'accidents', among which also figure such a 'chance' as the character of the people initially at the head of the movement" [ 1 ].

In our circumstances, such a fortuitous occurrence as the character of the man at the head of the movement, the party, and the working class—Stalin's character—plays a truly fateful role. Under a proletarian dictatorship that has concentrated all the levers of the economy in its hands, possessing an apparatus dozens of times more powerful and extensive than that of any bourgeois state, under the undivided dominance of a single party in the country and the gigantic centralization of the entire party leadership—the role of the General Secretary is enormous. His personal qualities acquire exceptional political significance.

This is precisely why Lenin, in his "Testament," attached such exceptional importance to the personal qualities of the General Secretary; this is precisely why Lenin, knowing Stalin's personal qualities, persistently emphasized in his "Testament" the need to remove Stalin from the post of General Secretary and replace him with a person more suitable for this role.

Lenin wrote in his "Testament":

> "Stalin is too rude, and this shortcoming, quite tolerable in the environment and (in) relations between us communists, becomes intolerable in the position of General Secretary. Therefore, I propose that comrades consider a way to remove Stalin from this position and appoint another person to this position, one who is different from the comrades in every respect. Stalin has only one advantage: he is more tolerant, more loyal, more polite and more attentive to comrades, less capricious, etc."[ 2 ]
>
> “Comrade Stalin, having become General Secretary, has concentrated immense power in his hands, and I am not sure whether he will always be able to use this power with sufficient caution” [ 3 ], Lenin continues.

Stalin, commenting on the "Testament," as usual with sophism, reduces the whole matter to rudeness, diverting attention from his other qualities, which Lenin had mentioned. Yet it is precisely these qualities that are decisive.

Lenin doubted that Stalin would be able to wield the General Secretary's immense power with sufficient caution. Lenin demanded that the General Secretary be more "loyal." So, Stalin wasn't loyal enough even then. And loyal means faithful, devoted, and honestly fulfilling his "duties."

Stalin is insufficiently loyal to the interests of the Party, insufficiently devoted, and insufficiently honest in fulfilling his duties. This is precisely the essence of Lenin's characterization of Stalin. And if Stalin concealed, disguised, and suppressed these "qualities" under Lenin, then after Lenin, he gave them full rein. If under Lenin, Stalin was insufficiently loyal, faithful to the interests of the Party, and honest in fulfilling his duties, now he became a true traitor to the Party, casting aside Party integrity and honesty, subordinating everything to the interests of his own ambition and lust for power.

Lenin further notes Stalin's intolerance of other people's opinions. This quality, combined with the first—disloyalty and dishonesty—led to his inability to tolerate people with independent, self-reliant party opinions, people spiritually, ideologically, and theoretically superior to him, relying on the party apparatus and the GPU. He ousted them from their leadership positions, slandered them, exaggerated their past mistakes, and "invented" dozens of new ones. He deceived the party, terrorized the party masses, and, in place of the party leaders he had discredited, installed people who were theoretically limited, ignorant, and unprincipled, but tame, submissive toadies and flatterers, ready to "recognize" any of his "theories" as Leninist, and any of his anti-Leninist articles as "historical."

As for Stalin’s politeness towards his comrades, which Lenin demanded of the General Secretary, his “historical” letter to the “Proletarian Revolution”[ 4 ] about Slutsky and Volosevich[ 5 ] can serve as an example, where the strength of the evidence is inversely proportional to the strength of the shout of the arrogant, presumptuous, insolent leader, who feels himself in the party and the country as if it were his own fiefdom, where he is free to execute and pardon anyone.

## 2. Stalin as an unprincipled politician

In theoretical terms, Stalin had shown himself to be a complete nonentity in recent years, but as an intriguer and political schemer, he displayed brilliant "talents." After Lenin's death, he grew more brazen with each passing year.

At first cautiously, then increasingly boldly, casting off the mask of the "modest" Old Bolshevik, whom the Party had "forced" to bear the heavy burden of General Secretary, he increasingly demonstrated his desire to climb into the pantheon of great men, shunning no means. He had already turned his fiftieth birthday into a veritable "coronation." Thousands of the most vile, vile, servile resolutions and greetings from the "masses," concocted by the disciplined Party, trade union, and Soviet apparatus, addressed to the "dear leader," "Lenin's finest student," and "brilliant theoretician"; dozens of articles in Pravda, in which many authors declared themselves Stalin's disciples, such as Voroshilov, proclaiming Stalin a major theoretician, and so on—such was the main backdrop to the anniversary. For every Bolshevik who had not yet completely lost his sense of shame and had not forgotten the old party traditions, this whole comedy of “coronation” evoked feelings of disgust and shame for the party.

Finally, Stalin's "historical" article in "Proletarian Revolution" definitively and cynically reveals his true intentions. To rewrite history so that Stalin would occupy his "befitting" place as a great man—that was the hidden meaning of Stalin's article. Now, after 15 years of proletarian dictatorship, all textbooks on the history of the Party have been proven worthless, containing "Trotskyist contraband."

From now on, the party's history will be written, or rather, fabricated, anew. Yaroslavsky, in his repentant speech at the Moscow Regional Party Conference, openly and cynically blurted out a "secret." He declared:

> "I must emphasize that in some textbooks on the history of the party, and this applies primarily to me, Comrade Stalin's role in the development of Bolshevism, especially in the pre-war years, is insufficiently illuminated."

This is the root of all the cries about "Trotskyist contraband," about slandering the party, about rotten liberalism, and so on. The answer is simple! Falsifying the history of the party under the guise of defending it, exaggerating some facts, ignoring others, concocting still others, elevating mediocrity to the pedestal of a "historical figure"—this is the essence of revising the history textbooks of the party. From now on, Yemelyan Yaroslavsky is definitively transformed into Yemelyan Ilovaisky[ 6 ]. And these people, without blushing, declare that the history of the party must be illuminated objectively.

Stalin will undoubtedly go down in history, but his "celebrity" will be that of Herostratus. Narrow-minded and cunning, power-hungry and vindictive, treacherous and envious, hypocritical and insolent, boastful and stubborn—Khlestakov and Arakcheyev, Nero and Count Cagliostro—such is Stalin's ideological, political, and spiritual physiognomy.

Now it is absolutely clear to everyone that Stalin had already planned to carry out his “18th Brumaire”[ 7 ] in 1924-25. Just as Louis Bonaparte swore allegiance to the constitution before the Chamber and simultaneously prepared to proclaim himself emperor, so Stalin, in his struggle with Trotsky, and then with Zinoviev and Kamenev, declared that he was fighting for the collective leadership of the party, that “it is impossible to lead the party outside the board,” that “it is impossible to lead the party without Rykov, Bukharin, and Tomsky,” that “we will not give you Bukharin’s blood,” that “the policy of cutting off is repugnant to us”[ 8 ] – and at the same time he was preparing a “bloodless” 18th Brumaire, cutting off one group after another and selecting people personally loyal to him for the apparatus of the Central Committee and for the secretaries of the provincial and regional committees.

If during Louis Bonaparte's coup d'état, the population of Paris heard the roar of cannons for several days, then during Joseph Stalin's coup d'état, the party heard the "gunfire" of slander and deception for several years. Stalin, like Louis Bonaparte, achieved results: the coup was accomplished, his personal dictatorship, the most naked and deceptive, was realized.

The main cohort of Lenin's comrades has been removed from their leadership positions, and one part of it is sitting in prisons and exile, another, having capitulated, demoralized and spat upon, is dragging out a miserable existence in the ranks of the party, and a third, having completely decomposed, has turned into loyal servants of the "leader"-dictator.

Over the past four or five years, Stalin has broken all records for political hypocrisy and unprincipled politicking. Even bourgeois politicians don't change their principles with the same shamelessness as Stalin.

What is the essence of unprincipled politicking? It lies in holding one view on one issue today, and the next (under the same circumstances and conditions, or under changed ones that don't truly justify such a change in political behavior—in the interests of an individual or clique)—the exact opposite. One point is made today, and the next day, on the same issue, under the same conditions, another. Yet, in both cases, the unprincipled politician considers himself right and consistent. He exploits the fact that the masses today are forgetting what they were told and promised yesterday, and that tomorrow they will forget what they were told today. If the masses notice the trick, the unprincipled politician tries to justify his shift by claiming that the political and economic situation, the balance of class forces, has now radically changed, and therefore a different policy, tactics, strategy, etc., is needed.

A Marxist-Leninist derives changes in policy, tactics, and strategy from actual changes in the socio-economic situation and the balance of class forces. An unprincipled politician, even if he conceals his politicking with Marxist-Leninist phraseology, on the contrary, subordinates his analysis and elucidation of the socio-class situation to changes in his personal behavior or the behavior of his clique, group, or party. Such is Kautsky[ 9 ], such is the entire clique of leaders and theoreticians of the Second International, such are the "theoreticians" of the various "left" groups and cliques, such is Stalin. The "methodological" essence of unprincipledness is the same in both cases.

Thousands of facts could be cited to illustrate Stalin's hypocrisy and unprincipled politicking, for his leadership in recent years has turned into nothing but unprincipled politicking and deception of the masses, but we will limit ourselves to only the most striking and major facts.

1. Stalin accused the "Trotskyists" of demanding:

a) increasing the pace of industrialization; b) intensifying the fight against the kulaks; c) introducing an emergency tax on the kulaks; d) raising prices; and d) confiscating 1,500 million rubles from the cooperatives.

He declared the "Trotskyists" counterrevolutionaries and "Trotskyism" a social democratic deviation. However, immediately after the 15th Congress, whose decisions were entirely directed against the "Trotskyists," when he decided to deal with the "rightists" after the "Trotskyists," and when he saw that attacking Bukharin and his group from the right was tactically disadvantageous, he began to pursue a policy in the areas of industrialization and rural policy that was directly opposed to the congress's decisions.

Having robbed Trotsky and his group blind, Stalin asserted that his super-industrialization, the pressure not only on the kulak but also on the middle peasant, the extraordinary tax, the confiscation of one and a half billion rubles from the cooperatives, and subsequently price hikes, rationing, and queues—all this was something entirely different from the proposals of the "Trotskyists." To do this, however, he had to turn everything upside down: what he had previously called Leninism in the area of ​​industrialization and rural policy, he declared to be right-wing opportunism, and what he had previously declared to be Trotskyism, he now called Leninism.

2. Stalin spoke specifically about the pace of work during the reconstruction period[ 10 ] at the 14th Congress:

> "The main thing about industry is that it has already reached the limit of pre-war prices, and that further steps in industry mean its development on a new technical basis, i.e., new equipment, new construction of factories. This is a very difficult matter. To step over (this) threshold, to move from a policy of maximum utilization of everything that we had in industry to a policy of building a new industry on a new technical basis, a basis for the construction of new factories, to cross this threshold requires large amounts of capital. But since we have a significant shortage of capital, in the future the development of our industry will, in all likelihood, not proceed at the same rapid pace as it has done so far" [ 11 ].

So, Stalin here clearly speaks of a slowdown in the reconstruction period. His views, expressed in the above quotation, are undoubtedly correct, but let alone anyone who dared to express them in 1930-31. By directive of that same Stalin, from 1928 onward, any talk of a slowdown in the reconstruction period was declared bourgeois, sabotage, and so on. However, one of two things is certain: either Stalin was mistaken earlier at the 14th Congress, or he was mistaken later with his new theory of increasing the pace. But Stalin is now silent about both the old and the new "theories."

3. At the October Plenum of the Central Committee and the Central Control Commission in 1927, Stalin said: “What is the pacification of the village? This is one of the new conditions for the construction of socialism. It is impossible to build socialism while having bandit uprisings and uprisings among the peasants”[ 12 ]. But already in July 1928, Stalin put forward a completely opposite “theory” and declared: “As we move forward along the path to socialism, the resistance of the capitalist elements will increase and the class struggle will intensify”[ 13 ]. In 1927, in his opinion, it was impossible to build socialism because we had bandit uprisings in the country, and even rebellions in Georgia[ 14 ]. In 1929-30-31, we experienced hundreds of uprisings, many of which were larger than those in Georgia. All of this turned out to be a natural result of the "gigantic successes in building socialism." The further we advance along the path to socialism, the more such uprisings there will be in the countryside, for the "class struggle will intensify." Back then, "pacifying" the countryside was "one of the fundamental conditions for building socialism," but now, the intensification of class struggle in the countryside, the incitement of this struggle, is one of the fundamental conditions for this construction.

4. In the same speech at the October Plenum, Stalin declared: “At the 14th Congress of our Party, the opposition, headed by Zinoviev and Kamenev, attempted to undermine this maneuver of the Party, proposing to replace it, in essence, with a policy of dispossession, a policy of restoring the Poor Peasants’ Committees. This was, in essence, a policy of restoring civil war in the villages.”[ 15 ] However, two years later, he himself put forward the slogan of dispossession, the liquidation of the kulaks as a class, organized a grandiose civil war in the villages, before which the entire policy of the Poor Peasants’ Committees, the food tax system, and the uprisings in the villages during the period of War Communism pales, and yet he remains a “consistent Leninist.”

5. Specifically on the question of fomenting class struggle in the countryside, Stalin also previously spoke in a manner directly opposite to what he is doing today. In his "Questions and Answers" at Sverdlovsk University in 1925, when he had not yet had to lie as much as he does now, Stalin said: "Does it follow from this that we must foment class struggle on this front? No, it does not. On the contrary! It only follows that we must in every possible way moderate the struggle on this front, regulating it through agreements and mutual concessions and under no circumstances allowing it to 'reach sharp forms, to clashes.'" And even specifically on the fight against the kulaks, he emphasized: "It may seem that the slogan of fomenting class struggle is entirely applicable to the conditions of the struggle on this front. But this is wrong, absolutely wrong. For here, too, we are not interested in fomenting class struggle. For we can and must do without inciting conflict and complications here”[ 16 ].

So, in 1925, Stalin declared that we "can and must do without inciting class struggle" against the kulaks, and he called the slogan of intensifying class struggle against the kulaks counterrevolutionary. And in 1928, he declared: "As we advance along the path to socialism, the resistance of capitalist elements will increase and the class struggle will intensify." But if intensifying class struggle against the kulaks is an inevitable "law" for a proletarian dictatorship, then all talk about "we can and must do without inciting class struggle on this front" is merely a well-meaning petty-bourgeois, reformist, and reactionary wish. If, indeed, "we can and must dispense with inciting struggle" against the kulaks, then the assertion that "as we advance along the path to socialism, the resistance of capitalist elements will increase and the class struggle will intensify" cannot be viewed as anything other than a justification and encouragement for inciting class struggle. This is indeed the case. His new theory of "intensifying class struggle" is precisely a justification for the policy of inciting class struggle not only against the kulaks but also against the middle peasants, and a justification for arbitrary treatment of rural workers. The confused "leader" and his "learned" flatterers might say that Stalin's statements apply only to the restoration period, while entirely different laws apply to the reconstruction period. But this would be a typical Stalinist evasion. Firstly, on this issue he does not make any reservations about the fact that his “theory” concerns only the recovery period; secondly, the entire nature and meaning of the thoughts he expressed here in itself speaks against such a limited understanding of the given quote.

6. At the Moscow Provincial Conference of September 23–27, Stalin specifically emphasized in his speech that industrialization was unthinkable without a correct policy in the countryside. He said: “To pursue a policy of discord with the majority of the peasantry means to start a civil war in the countryside, to hinder the supply of peasant raw materials (cotton, beets, flax, leather, wool, etc.) to our industry, to disorganize the supply of agricultural products to the working class, to undermine the very foundations of our industry, to disrupt all our construction work, (to disrupt) our entire plan for the industrialization of the country” [ 17 ].

This is precisely what has happened today. Cotton, flax, and beets, though sown haphazardly under the scourge of terror and repression, yielded poor harvests due to poorly cultivated soil and poor care, and much of what did grow remained unharvested, lost to the snow.

Tens of millions of centners of beets were left unharvested, and tens of millions of already-harvested beets were lost in piles under the snow, as the farmers' lack of transportation and their own self-interest prevented them from being transported to factories, which were expected to be idle during the busiest period. Tens of thousands of hectares of unharvested cotton also perished. The same thing happened to flax. The textile industry is still operating at half capacity, relying on substitutes. Leather, wool, fat, and other agricultural products needed by industry are unavailable. Workers are on starvation rations, not only receiving insufficient meat and fat, but even potatoes and cabbage. The workers are exhausted, and the purchasing and paying power of the working masses in both city and countryside has been fundamentally undermined. All the foundations of industrialization have been undermined. Stalin's predictions have been brilliantly "justified" here. But he is forced to remain silent about it.

7. On the issue of raising prices, Stalin said in 1927: "I could, further, cite a number of documents from the opposition in favor of raising prices for industrial goods, which increase cannot but lead to the decline of our industry, to the strengthening of the kulaks, to the ruin of the middle peasants, and to the enslavement of the poor by the kulaks" [ 18 ]. At present, prices have risen four to five times compared to the level at which they stood four years ago, and real wages have fallen colossally. But, according to Stalin, this is now leading to the rapid growth of industry and to a steady improvement in the material conditions of the working class and the toiling masses of the village.

8. Stalin, as is well known, once mocked Kamenev when he tried to blame the failure of grain procurements on the kulak: "The kulak regulated" – this provoked a "decisive rebuff" from Stalin [ 19 ]. Now, under the magician's wand, the kulak has become an omnipotent and omnipresent force: everywhere and everywhere, the kulak is plotting intrigues against socialist construction. And here Stalin has gone no further than political plagiarism, employed with a zeal worthy of better application.

9. The situation is no better with his campaign against the theory of the "degradation of agriculture" [ 20 ]. Stalin denounced this theory as "right-wing opportunist" and argued that individual peasant farming in its majority was developing and capable of development. However, only a year had passed since the beginning of Stalin's "historical" campaign, and the campaign was still ongoing, when Stalin came forward at a conference of Marxist agrarians with a new theoretical formula. "Our small-peasant farming," he declared, "not only does not realize the annual expansion of reproduction in its majority, but, on the contrary, does not always have the ability to realize even simple reproduction" [ 21 ]. Translated into simple language, this means that the majority of peasant farming (i.e., middle-peasant farming) not only does not develop, but is also marking time, i.e., is experiencing stagnation, or even sliding downwards.

In the future, we will demonstrate the theoretical illiteracy of Stalin's latest "theory" from the point of view of Marxism-Leninism; for now, we note this as an example of the "consistency" and "principledness" of this "steel" Bolshevik, who changes his views and principles like gloves.

## 3. Stalin as a sophist

To prove and "reconcile" all these irreconcilable contradictions, Stalin has sophistry at his disposal. An unprincipled politician is by nature a sophist, a political magician and actor. He dons various masks depending on his goals. And since dialectics and sophistry share a certain superficial similarity, for both types of thinking are based on the recognition of the principle of contradiction, it is all the easier for Stalin to pass off sophistry as dialectics.

Sophistry is a seeming dialectic. Under certain conditions, dialectic imperceptibly morphs into sophistry, and Stalin skillfully exploits this. The sophist, at will, chooses any proposition and proves its "truth." He knows he will always find a group of people who will allow themselves to be deceived.

What is the essence of sophistry and how does it differ from dialectics? The essence of sophistry is that one or another one-sided and abstract definition is advanced in isolation, without connection to the surrounding world, individual cases, facts, and examples are plucked out, and "conclusions" and "proofs" are built on these. And since "given the enormous complexity of the phenomena of social life, one can always find any number of examples or individual data to confirm any proposition" [ 22 ] (Lenin), Stalin, therefore, always proves whatever he wants, especially since there is nowhere to prove the opposite—the entire press is at Stalin's personal disposal, and with the terror reigning over the party, the working class, and the toiling peasantry, no one would dare to prove the opposite, for this would inevitably entail various punishments. "The power of proof" is here simply replaced by "proof of force"—argumentum baculini. The stick-like proof was Stalin's primary method of "proof" and "argumentation" when dealing with party members and party organizations. And we're not even talking about workers and the bulk of the village population!

But Stalin, as a sophist, uses not only the proverbial rod, but also all the basic techniques and methods of sophistry.

First, Stalin accepts as proven what needs to be proven, and bases all his other conclusions on this predetermined proposition. These are his decreed propositions: "Trotskyism is the vanguard of the counterrevolutionary bourgeoisie," "The Right is an agent of the kulak," "The bulk of the middle peasantry has turned toward collectivization," and so on.

Secondly, where it suits him, he bases his conclusions on the principle: after this, therefore, because of this, and connected with this. The saboteurs sought to slow the pace of industrialization, while the right, as Stalin decreed, was also against rapid progress; therefore, the right is an agent of the class enemy.

Using this argumentation, one could just as successfully prove that the German Communist Party was "aligned" with the fascists, since both were seeking the overthrow of the Brüning government[ 23 ], that the Russian Bolsheviks in the State Duma were "aligned" with the right-wing parties, since both often voted together against a number of Duma bills, that the Cadets and some other right-wing parties were "agents" of the Bolsheviks, since both saw the bankruptcy of the autocracy on the eve of the 1914 war and predicted the inevitability of its collapse, and in February 1917 they together overthrew it; thus, one could "prove" that at the present time the majority of the conscious part of the party is "aligned" with Kautsky, the Mensheviks, the Socialist Revolutionaries, and the Cadets, and is an agent of capitalism, since both see the bankruptcy of Stalin's policy. By this method one can prove anything. These facts once again demonstrate Stalin's "brilliant" qualities as a "leader"; they show that we have before us not a "hard-boiled Bolshevik," but a Bolshevik who, at crucial moments in the life of the party, allowed himself to make gross opportunistic mistakes and hesitations, and who has now turned into an unprincipled politician and adventurer.

## 5. Leninism and the fight against opportunism

We focus first and foremost on the fundamental question of Marx-Lenin's teachings—the struggle against opportunism. Defending the purity of the theoretical principles of Marxism-Leninism is the fundamental duty of a Bolshevik and a communist. An irreconcilable struggle against opportunism, which expresses bourgeois influence on the party of the proletariat, is a decisive condition for the triumph of the proletarian revolution and the victorious construction of socialism.

All this is absolutely indisputable. But anyone who has only assimilated this requirement in Lenin’s teaching has not yet become a Bolshevik. We must not forget that every truth, every great teaching can be distorted, vulgarized, turned into a caricature. Lenin, in his brilliant work, “Left-Wing” Communism: An Infantile Disorder, writes on this issue: “The surest way to discredit a new political (and not only political) idea and to damage it is to reduce it to absurdity in the name of defending it. For every truth, if made “excessive”… if exaggerated, if extended beyond the limits of its actual applicability, can be reduced to absurdity, and even, under the given conditions, it inevitably turns into absurdity” [ 35 ]. This is precisely what has happened today with Lenin’s teaching on the struggle against opportunism. Stalin vulgarized Lenin's doctrine of combating opportunism to its utmost limits, reduced it to absurdity, and turned it into a caricature and an object of ridicule and mockery for party members themselves, for workers and other working people in general, and for the enemies of the proletarian dictatorship. Stalin simply turned the fight against opportunism into a weapon for terrorizing the party in defense of his illiterate anti-Leninist theories and policies, turning it into a scourge to whip party members into all sorts of campaigns.

What is opportunism?

Engels gives the following classical definition of opportunism:

> "This forgetting of great, fundamental considerations because of the momentary interests (of the day), this pursuit of momentary successes in the struggle for them without taking into account the subsequent consequences, this sacrifice of the future movement to the present - perhaps this also occurs because of "honest" motives. But (this) is opportunism and will remain opportunism, and "honest" opportunism is perhaps the most dangerous of all others"[ 36 ].

Lenin gives exactly the same definition of opportunism. He says:

> “Opportunism consists of sacrificing fundamental interests in order to gain temporary partial benefits. That is the crux of the matter, if we take the theoretical definition of opportunism”[ 37 ].

If we decipher these formulations of Engels and Lenin, if we make them more concrete, we can say that opportunism for parties that are only just struggling for power consists in the rejection of the struggle for the armed overthrow of the bourgeoisie, in capitulation to the difficulties and dangers of this struggle, in substituting the struggle for the world proletarian revolution with the struggle for reforms, in obscuring the irreconcilability of the class contradictions between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat, in striving to reconcile these contradictions instead of aggravating them, in undermining the international solidarity of the proletariat, in renouncing it, in sowing illusions in the ranks of the working class that within the framework of capitalism the proletariat can achieve an improvement in its position, in bowing to the spontaneity of the workers' movement, in forgetting its duty to defend the Soviet Union in every possible way, in striving to reconcile the worldview of the revolutionary proletariat - Marxism-Leninism, to dissolve it, To cripple and disfigure in relation to one or another variety of the bourgeois worldview. As for parties and the proletariat that have already won state power, the concept of opportunism significantly expands and simultaneously changes.

For a Communist Party where the proletariat has already won political power, the entire old meaning of the concept of opportunism also remains, insofar as it concerns the correct application of Leninist principles in the struggle to overthrow capitalist rule in countries where the bourgeoisie holds sway. When we move on to the country of the proletarian dictatorship itself, the meaning of the concept of opportunism undergoes a slight modification. Here, too, we cannot tolerate any glossing over of class contradictions, either between the proletariat and the capitalist elements of the city and village, or between the proletariat and the middle peasantry. A sober, clear, honest, straightforward, and consistent Marxist-Leninist assessment, a sharp Marxist-Leninist analysis, is absolutely essential here, as elsewhere.

But under the conditions of a proletarian dictatorship, we are no longer interested in inciting class struggle, in intensifying it not only between the proletariat and the middle peasantry, but also between the proletariat and capitalist elements.

The policy of fomenting class struggle, as the experience of the last three years has shown, is enormously disruptive and fundamentally undermines socialist construction. The proletarian state, however, has every opportunity to avoid fomenting class struggle without compromising an inch of its position.

If even the bourgeoisie, albeit temporarily and within narrow limits, without ceding its position, with the aid of the bourgeois state apparatus, the press, and the church, manages to moderate the class struggle, soften it, give it "legitimate," so to speak, normal forms, and regulate it, then the proletarian state has at its disposal dozens of times more powerful means for regulating the class struggle. Stalin, as we saw above, once understood these elementary Marxist truths. Now, completely confused and lying, he has even discovered the "law" of the inevitable intensification of the class struggle "as we advance along the path to socialism."

The second characteristic: capitulation to the difficulties of socialist construction, ignoring and neglecting the tasks of socialist construction, bowing to spontaneity and the inevitable, abandoning the necessary rapid pace of industrialization. Opportunism prior to the conquest of political power naturally lacks this characteristic. Stalin has created a particularly thick fog here, mixing one part truth with nine parts lies, and it will take the greatest effort to separate the grains of truth from the vast mass of nonsense. Capitulation to the difficulties of socialist construction in one economically backward country is undeniable opportunism. However, simply stating this fact does not resolve the issue; it merely raises it.

Anyone who truly capitulates to difficulties is an opportunist, but cries only about capitulation. He is not yet a Bolshevik, but perhaps only a demagogue, a phrase-monger, a chatterbox, pursuing not the overcoming of difficulties but his own personal, ambitious goals. For the essence of the question lies in the methods for overcoming difficulties. If the leadership's political line, while calling for a struggle against difficulties, ensures their overcoming and the victory of socialist construction, then such a line is truly Leninist. If, however, the leadership's political line not only fails to ensure the overcoming of these difficulties but, on the contrary, increases them, piling them one upon another, driving the Party and the country into a dead end, ruining socialist construction, discrediting it at its very roots, then it is anti-Leninist, although it cannot be called opportunist. Arakcheev's policies, which refused to capitulate to difficulties and had only one slogan—"Drive!"—certainly do not contain opportunism, but they also lack a grain of Leninism. Admiring spontaneity and the natural course of events is opportunism, but ignoring the specific situation, overlooking the entire material, cultural, social, and political conditions within which one must work, and engaging in economic and political skepticism—all this is no less adventurism.

Stalin has now provided a genuine "theoretical basis" for this Khlestakov-style adventurism. In his speech "New Situation—New Tasks of Economic Development," he writes:

> "Finally, a few words about our economic plan for 1931. There are some near-party philistines who insist that our production program is unrealistic, impossible to fulfill. They are something like Shchedrin's "wise minnows," always ready to spread around themselves the "emptiness of thoughtlessness." Is our production program realistic? Absolutely! It is realistic, if only because its fulfillment now depends solely on ourselves, on our ability and our desire to utilize the vast resources at our disposal. How else can we explain the fact that a number of enterprises and industries have already exceeded their plan? It would be foolish to think that the production plan is reduced to a list of figures and targets. In reality, the production plan is the living and practical activity of millions of people. The reality of our production plan lies in the millions of workers creating a new life. "The reality of our production program is living people, it is you and me, our will to work, our readiness to work in a new way, our determination to fulfill the plan. Do we have this determination? Yes, we do. Therefore, our production program can and must be implemented" [ 38 ].

Such is Stalin's "philosophy" of fighting for plan fulfillment, for high rates. In its theoretical Marxist-Leninist illiteracy, its complete rupture with materialist dialectics, its emptiness, its absolute absence of even the slightest attempt at a genuine analysis of the conditions on which plan fulfillment depends, its naked, undisguised desire to gloss over the bankruptcy of adventurist rates and planning methods with empty chatter and the plucking of isolated "facts," this speech is a true gem.

Anyone who hasn't yet forgotten the basics of Marxism-Leninism, who hasn't yet lost the ability to think like Lenin under the influence of the daily distortion of the teachings of Marx, Engels, and Lenin, will immediately say that Stalin's speech doesn't smell of Leninism. In its objective content, it is a philosophy of economic and political adventurism, it is pure subjective idealism, it is a kind of Socialist Revolutionary ideology in a new edition.

Let's look at the arguments Stalin uses to defend the plan's feasibility. First, the plan's feasibility, according to Stalin, is proven by the fact that a number of enterprises and industries have already exceeded the plan. Second, by the fact that fulfilling the plan depends solely on us. The feasibility of the plan lies in our determination to fulfill it? That's all Stalin could offer in defense of the plan's feasibility. But these arguments actually prove nothing, and instead reveal once again the pathetic sophistical devices Stalin is forced to resort to to justify his policies.

Does the fulfillment of the plan by individual enterprises, or even entire branches of industry, prove the reality of the plan for all industry? Absolutely not. This is precisely the kind of cherry-picking of isolated "facts" that Lenin so mercilessly castigated. From 50,000 enterprises, you can always single out 500-600 that fulfill the plan, but this says absolutely nothing about the reality of the plan as a whole. Some of these enterprises fulfilled the plan because they were fully supplied with raw materials at the expense of other enterprises; others because they were better financed at the expense of other enterprises; others because they were provided with the best, most qualified specialists; others simply because this plan, compared to the previous year, not only failed to produce any increase in output, but even a decrease; others because of a colossal deterioration in product quality; and still others, finally, simply provided inflated information about plan fulfillment, engaging in window dressing, imitating Stalin. And on such a "theoretical" basis, Stalin builds his conclusions!

The situation is roughly the same with individual branches of industry. Stalin singles out the automobile industry, electrical engineering, mechanical engineering, and a couple of similar industries, and bases his conclusion on them. But these are young industries: their production volumes are still insignificant and they are only just mastering technology. Naturally, they produce at a high rate, but these industries account for no more than 10% of the total industrial workforce in the USSR; they are placed in exceptionally favorable conditions relative to the rest of industry (in terms of supplies of raw materials, finance, foreign currency, building materials, etc.); they were supplied with all of this at the expense of the bulk of industry; these industries concentrated a huge amount of surplus labor in their enterprises; the workers of these enterprises, at the expense of all other workers, were better fed in canteens, better supplied with food, clothing, etc., from closed distribution centers, and better served with special clothing. Herein lies the secret of these industries' plan fulfillment, if we ignore the falsification of statistical data. But here, too, Stalin reasons according to the principle: if there can be an exception, then there can also be a rule. If some people are liars, then everyone can be liars.

A remarkable "Marxist-Leninist" argument! Stalin is afraid to even touch this question with a concrete analysis, for such an analysis would expose the springs of his tempo-based balancing act. Therefore, he limits himself to decrees instead of proof.

As for Stalin's second proof, it demonstrates even more clearly how confused and deceitful Stalin was. Blaming everything on "objective conditions," looking for them where they don't exist, is, of course, opportunism. But denying them where they exist, refusing the strictest, most concrete, bold, and honest Marxist analysis and consideration of these conditions, means leading the party and the country to certain defeat; it means leading blindly...

Stalin declares that the fulfillment of the plan now depends solely on us. This is pure nonsense and ignorance. The fulfillment of the plan depends on how correctly it is drawn up. A hollow plan, which takes into account only our determination to fulfill it, is doomed to bankruptcy sooner or later. A correct plan must satisfy at least the following conditions: 1) a correct and strict accounting of available material resources; 2) correct accounting, observing the measure of those changes and shifts that must be made in the relationships between individual elements of the national economy based on the systematic, rapid growth of productive forces and an increase in the relative importance of the socialist sector in a given period and in a given space; 3) the obligatory and steady real growth of the material and cultural standards of the working class and the toiling masses.

The fulfillment of the plan depends not only not solely on ourselves, but to a huge extent on: a) the raw material base, b) the supply of food and clothing for the workers, c) the availability of sufficient financial resources, d) sufficient rail, water, and horse-drawn transport, and e) the personal interest of the workers and all working people in fulfilling the plan. Without these conditions, any talk of fulfilling the plan, of the will to work, of "our determination" is mere phrases, chatter, and dust in the eyes of the workers. But it is precisely these conditions that Stalin obscures, glosses over, and distracts the workers' attention from, because they are absent. Indeed: the raw material base has been fundamentally undermined, the workers are starving (even potatoes have become a scarce commodity), the workers are barefoot and naked, there is nothing to finance industry with, because taxes are weak and the population's solvency has been completely undermined, horse-drawn transport has been almost completely destroyed, the personal interest of the workers and peasants has been destroyed...

As a result, Stalin's plan's reality remains a blank. Instead of plan fulfillment, there are just empty phrases about plan fulfillment. The results of the 1931 economic year confirmed this.

Let us now turn to "rapid growth." We have already noted above that Stalin juggles these words as he pleases, investing them with a wide variety of meanings, depending on what suits him. What is a "rapid growth"? If we adhere to the foundations of Marxism and if we do not alter the meaning of the concept "rapid growth" depending on Stalin's "economic situation," then it can have only one meaning: a more rapid growth of socialist productive forces than capitalism experienced during its heyday. While capitalist countries during their heyday achieved an annual increase in output of 6 to 10%, the Soviet Union, having abolished private land ownership, expropriated factories, plants, and transportation, holding all the decisive economic commanding heights in its hands, conducting a planned economy, and relying on the support, activity, and creativity of the masses, has every opportunity to achieve a higher growth rate. Anyone who denies this possibility denies the advantages of a socialist economy—that is, an opportunist.

And then, within the framework of this accepted fundamental principle, precise calculations and computations begin. Whether this annual growth will be 12, 13, 15, 18, or 20% must be proven, firstly, by precise calculations and computations, and secondly, by the actual experience of socialist construction. This must always be kept in mind, however, that rapid growth rates must be ensured not for three or four years, but over a long historical period, and that they must be based on the crisis-free development of the socialist economy, as well as on the actual growth of the material well-being of the masses. And anyone who claims that a 12% annual growth rate is possible is no more an opportunist on this basis than someone who claims that a 20 or 30% annual growth rate is possible.

Anyone who today proclaims 15% growth to be opportunistic, announces 25% tomorrow, and 30% next year, and then begins to carry out this entire operation in reverse order, is mocking Lenin’s teaching on the fight against opportunism, is turning the fight against opportunism into a caricature, into a laughing stock, and thereby is not contributing to the fight against opportunism, but is only strengthening it.

Now a few words about the fight against opportunism in practice. Here, Stalin broke all records for the vulgarization of Leninism. Opportunism in grain procurement, opportunism in self-procurement, opportunism in wood procurement, opportunism in seed procurement, opportunism in timber rafting, opportunism in wool and flax procurement, opportunism in horse care, opportunism in cleaning out barnyards and pits, opportunism in the work of rat-catchers. Opportunism everywhere!

Everything is painted in the same Stalinist gray! Everyone has been turned into opportunists! But where everyone is an opportunist, there are no opportunists; the struggle against genuine opportunists becomes impossible, or at least enormously difficult, for they are lost in the rest of the mass of specially manufactured opportunists.

Moreover, when this sharp Leninist weapon of combating opportunism begins to be applied indiscriminately to everyone, for the purpose of slandering and terrorizing the masses, it naturally becomes dulled, loses its former significance, and begins to be regarded simply as a means of reprisal against dissenters and a means of intimidating the masses, which is, in fact, what the struggle against opportunism has essentially degenerated into at the present moment. On the other hand, it is precisely the genuine opportunists, the former Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, who have been particularly adept at adapting to Stalin's contemporary policies and now act as Stalin's reliable support. "All opportunism is distinguished by adaptability, although not all adaptability is opportunism" [ 39 ] (Lenin).

The most characteristic feature of opportunists is that they are always and everywhere perfectly able to adapt themselves to the ruling regime: in England they have adapted themselves to the constitutional monarchy and are ministers of “his royal majesty” – the King of Great Britain; in Germany they are the faithful servants of Hindenburg[ 40 ]; in Italy, after the first shout from Mussolini, they went over to the side of fascism and for 12 years have conscientiously served the regime of bloody fascist terror; in Bulgaria they are the defenders of the Tsankovshchina and its heirs[ 41 ]; in Japan they support the semi-feudal monarchy and all its policies.

Our opportunists also managed to adapt to Stalin's regime and adopted a camouflage color scheme. And all the true, most seasoned opportunists today must be sought precisely within the ranks of Stalin's ruling clique!

What does the failure to fulfill grain procurement plans for a given region, or even the Union, have to do with opportunism? Failure to fulfill the plan can be explained by a variety of reasons: a shortage of grain and a poor harvest, bad weather hindering threshing and hauling, the inability of procurement officials to organize the operation, the resistance of the bulk of the villagers to sell grain at low prices, which, moreover, cannot be exchanged for any goods, and so on. But for Stalin, none of these reasons exist. For him, only the resistance of the kulaks and opportunism exist. And Stalin is forced to cling to all this nonsense, for he cannot tell the truth or provide a sober Marxist analysis; he is forced to replace arguments with empty, loud, and terrifying phrases.

So, how does it follow that squeezing the last pood of grain and potatoes from a village, expropriating the last cow or sheep from a middle or poor peasant, depriving a peasant of his last pound of flax or wool, chicken or egg—is Leninism? After all, such policies fundamentally undermine the raw material base for socialist construction. If yesterday you stripped the village bare in the interests of socialist industry, then today you will get nothing from it in the name of those same interests, as we are witnessing.

Whence, then, does it follow that violence and abuse of the rural working masses (not the kulak) in order to fulfill all sorts of plans, a frenzied terror—is Leninism? Whence, finally, does it follow that a worker who is unable to procure grain is an opportunist, while one who can perform this function is a Leninist, a seasoned Bolshevik?

One can be an excellent procurer of grain, wool, firewood, potatoes, and cabbage, a specialist in exterminating rats, bedbugs, and cockroaches, and yet have absolutely nothing in common with Leninism. Conversely, one can be an excellent proletarian revolutionary, a conscious and honest communist, a consistent Marxist-Leninist, and yet a completely useless wool and potato procurer, a poor groom, or a cafeteria manager. Stalin, however, lumps them together. A good Bolshevik must necessarily be a good rat exterminator, and a rat exterminator must necessarily be a good Bolshevik.

If Romain Rolland in his time invented bureaucratic imperialism and the imperialism of revolutionary workers' syndicates, if the famous Italian writer Mario Martisso wrote an entire book "on artistic capitalism", and Ernest Sayer in his four-volume work "The Philosophy of Imperialism" studies in detail "racial imperialism", "utilitarian imperialism", "democratic imperialism", "irrational imperialism", if, finally, Sayer's student was even agreed upon as ant, bee and tree imperialism, then Stalin invented a whole series of opportunists: "Opportunism of grain procurement workers", "wool opportunism", "egg opportunism", "opportunism in the extermination of fleas and cockroaches".

The aforementioned bourgeois scholars and writers lumped together the most diverse phenomena under the single word "imperialism," while Stalin lumped together the most diverse phenomena under the single word "opportunism." Bourgeois scholars took one purely external characteristic and declared that any manifestation of the desire for expansion, inherent in all living beings, constituted imperialism. According to Stalin and the Stalinist press, any failure to fulfill a plan or any doubt about its reality constituted opportunism and was qualified as opportunism.

Lenin’s saying that “every truth can be reduced to absurdity if it is exaggerated, if it is taken beyond the limits of its actual applicability” [ 43 ] is thus remarkably clearly confirmed in Stalin’s struggle against opportunism.

Why, indeed, was there no practical struggle against opportunism under Lenin? Or was there no practical struggle at all? Or did Lenin fail to recognize opportunism? Only because Lenin was a brilliant dialectician, not a sophist. He knew how to distinguish opportunism from fundamentally different phenomena. Stalin, however, goes through one door and ends up through another: he hurls thunder and lightning against opportunism, sabotage, and counterrevolution, yet he drags them along on his back—such is the dialectic and irony of history. Today, this assertion sounds paradoxical. But after a while, it will be clear to every Marxist-Leninist.

Every action taken to extremes turns into its own opposite, said old Hegel. Stalin's struggle against opportunism also turned into its own opposite.

## 6. Leninism and socialist society

At the 16th Party Congress, Stalin declared that we had already entered the period of socialism, since the socialist sector now holds in its hands all the economic levers of the entire national economy, although the construction of a socialist economy and the elimination of class differences were still a long way off.

At the 17th Party Conference, a year and a half after the 16th Congress, Molotov went even further and stated with complete certainty that we had already entered the first phase of communism, i.e., we were currently living in a socialist society, albeit in its initial stage.

In the resolution of the 17th conference on the reports of Molotov and Kuibyshev, this idea is concretized as follows: “As a result of the implementation of the Bolshevik pace of socialist construction and the liquidation of mainly parasitic classes, the foundations and source of exploitation of man by man are eliminated already in the first five years, the national income grows at a rate unattainable for capitalist countries, unemployment and poverty (pauperism) are eliminated, the ‘price scissors’ and the opposition between the city and the village are eliminated, the well-being and cultural level of workers and toiling peasants grows from year to year, mortality falls and the population of the USSR rapidly increases.

All these achievements are the result of a colossal growth of revolutionary activity among the broadest masses of the working class and toiling peasants, the result of a tremendous rise in socialist competition and shock work, and finally, the result of the Leninist policy of our party, which consistently carried out a full-scale offensive against capitalist elements on the entire front”[ 44 ].

The conference resolution further emphasizes that the main political task of the second five-year plan is the final liquidation of capitalist elements and classes in general, the complete elimination of the causes that give rise to class differences and exploitation, and the overcoming of the remnants of capitalism in the economy and consciousness of people, turning the entire working population of the country into conscious and active builders of a classless socialist society.

Formally, this resolution does not, by and large, contradict the teachings of Marx and Lenin on the first phase of communism (socialist society). If, in reality, our socialist construction had proceeded as the 17th Conference resolution asserts, we would indeed have entered a socialist society.

However, the essence of Stalin’s “general line” is that in this most important issue it formally appears to rely on the teachings of Marx and Lenin, but in essence it represents a mockery of Marxism and Leninism, the most vile and base discrediting of the teachings of our teachers.

The worst enemy of communism, the most brilliant provocateur could not have come up with anything better than Stalin, who proclaimed through the mouth of Molotov that we were already living in a socialist society, albeit in its initial stage.

The validity of every theoretical proposition and political assertion must be verified and supported by facts, practice, and reality. Let us now proceed to such verification.

1. The resolution asserts that we have achieved gigantic "successes in socialist construction." In reality, despite the construction of dozens of large factories using the latest technology and the presence of 100,000 tractors in the countryside, we are witnessing an undermining of the very foundations of socialist construction. First, the Soviet Union's primary productive force—the working class itself and the toiling masses of the countryside—has been undermined: they are exhausted, they work half-starved, they are barefoot and naked. Second, their purchasing power has been fundamentally undermined, as a result of which the entire industrialization project now hangs in the balance. Third, the entire raw materials and agricultural base of industry has been undermined.

2. The resolution asserts that our national income is growing at a rate unattainable for capitalist countries. In reality, our national income has been falling over the past four years, and the country's capital stock is not increasing, but declining. The construction of new factories and plants costing 8-10 billion rubles at one extreme is associated with the destruction of capital stock worth 20-30 billion rubles at the other extreme. Overall, national labor productivity has not increased, but fallen.

The construction of new factories and plants is not being carried out at the expense of increasing the national income, but at the expense of expropriating a large part of the workers' wages - through all kinds of loans, taxes, membership fees, deprivation of workers' benefits, special clothing, a wild increase in prices and at the expense of expropriating the bulk of the village population.

3. The resolution claims that the "price scissors" are being abolished. In reality, the "price scissors" have grown enormously. The peasant receives a paltry pittance for his products at the standardized procurement prices of 1 ruble 50 kopecks – 2 rubles per pood of grain, and pays 1 ruble 50 kopecks per meter of calico.

4. The resolution asserts that we have eliminated unemployment and poverty. In reality, we have temporarily eliminated unemployment, but have gigantically increased poverty. Currently, with the exception of a tiny ruling clique and a tiny percentage of highly paid workers and specialists (only 1-2 million people), the remaining 158 million people in the Soviet Union are paupers or semi-paupers.

5. The resolution asserts that the antithesis between city and village is already being eliminated in the first five-year plan. In reality, due to the general impoverishment of the masses in both cities and villages, the antithesis between city and village is not only not diminishing, but actually increasing.

Bread, meat, wool, leather, flax, chickens, eggs, and other goods are confiscated from the villages for next to nothing, all of which is hauled off to the starving cities and exported abroad at half price. The village has been transformed into the worst kind of colony. There are no goods in the village; at the same time, there is nothing to make homespun clothing and shoes from, because the flax, wool, and leather have been confiscated, and the livestock is slaughtered and dying from poor care and lack of feed.

Bast shoes have become a desperately short supply. As a result, the entire village is clothed in the squalor of the poor. A collective farmer's workday is paid an average of 15-20 kopecks, which, converted into a gold ruble, amounts to two or three kopecks. The countryside is now a vast cemetery. The growing antagonism between city and village, driven by the general deterioration of the conditions of workers and the majority of the village population, is, incidentally, brilliantly expressed in the depopulation of the countryside and the exodus of the entire healthy and able-bodied population to the cities. Such is Stalin's abolition of the antagonism between city and village. As for the abolition of unemployment, firstly, as we have already emphasized, unemployment was abolished due to the enormous increase in poverty among the overwhelming majority of the Soviet Union's population. Secondly, there are currently at least 300-400 thousand unemployed in the Soviet Union, and a huge increase in unemployment is expected in the near future. Stalin is hiding the onset of unemployment, and he will, of course, continue to hide it in the future.

6. The resolution asserts that the well-being of workers and toiling peasants is growing year after year. In reality, their well-being has deteriorated dramatically over the past four years. The average worker's real wage currently amounts to no more than 25% of the 1927 real wage; the average peasant's (collective farmer's) budget expenditure on family needs, in commercial rubles, is currently 3-4 times lower than it was in 1926-1927. Stalin's adventurist, anti-Leninist policies have led to this. Workers go weeks without a gram of meat, butter, or milk; they are forced to stand in line for hours for a yard of calico; there is nowhere to buy a fork, a glass, or a spoon. Stalin counters this by promoting rest homes and increasing the number of workers in a family, but rest homes existed in 1927 and were more efficient than they are now. As for the additional involvement of workers in production, it in no way compensates for the enormous decline in real wages and all the suffering workers have endured in recent years. Only those who have lost all shame can speak of a steady increase in the well-being of workers and those in labor; only to mock the workers can one claim that they are better off now than in 1926-1927.

7. The resolution asserts that "a radical change has occurred in agriculture, expressed in the final turn toward socialism among the poor and middle peasant masses in the village." In reality, peasants are driven into collective farms through terror, direct and indirect coercion, and violence. Collective farms are maintained solely by repression and by the fact that the peasants have been forced into an environment where they have nowhere else to go. Consequently, the productivity of collective farmers is incomparably lower than that of individual farmers, and the quality of their work is even worse.

Thanks to Stalin's policy of systematically fleecing the village, peasants on collective farms are not only no better off than they were on individual farms, but far worse off. As a result, not only will a hundred thousand tractors fail to convince the villagers of the advantages of communal farming, but even their numbers will be many times greater.

Technology must be supported by the right policy. Only with the right policy can technology solve everything! Technology without the right policy solves nothing.

8. The resolution asserts that we have seen a colossal increase in activity and upsurge among workers and toiling peasants. In reality, however, in recent years we have seen a huge decline in activity among the proletariat and the rural working masses, an extraordinary sense of harassment, oppression, intimidation, and oppression.

The apparatus passes off its own hustle, bustle, chatter, and paperwork for shock workers as mass activity. With terror reigning over the working masses, there can be no talk of any genuine growth in mass activity.

Thus, the entire conclusion that we have already entered the first phase of communism is based, from beginning to end, on false premises and assertions. All premises prove to be fictitious, fabricated, and false. As a result, Stalin's "socialist society" is entirely devoid of socialist content.

In reality, we are now incomparably further from a socialist society than we were in 1926-1927. For every Marxist-Leninist who knows how to distinguish form from content, there can be no doubt about this.

## 7. Classes and the intensification of class struggle

We have already noted above that Stalin, in a conversation with the Sverdlovsk people in 1925, put forward one theory of class struggle and in 1928 – a completely opposite one, without, however, renouncing his first theory, but simply keeping silent about it in order to hide his inconsistency and his politicking.

The first theory is, in principle, undoubtedly correct, and it would be dishonest, as Stalin does with his political opponents, to consider them anti-Marxist, anti-Leninist, opportunistic just because Stalin expressed these views.

The second, on the contrary, breaks with Marxism-Leninism, breaks with materialist dialectics, and is a sophistic counterfeit of Leninism. The essence of the second theory, as is well known, is expressed in Stalin's words: "As we advance towards socialism, the resistance of the capitalist elements will increase, the class struggle will intensify" [ 45 ]. Stalin justifies this theory simply: no moribund class has ever voluntarily left the historical arena. If we undermine the very roots of the capitalist elements, if we attack them, they will naturally offer furious resistance. This is the entire "theoretical basis" for the intensification of the class struggle. In its methodology, the "theory of intensification of the class struggle" is just as mechanistic and anti-dialectical as Bukharin's theory with its peaceful integration of "kulak cooperative nests" into the socialist system [ 46 ].

Wherein lies the falsity of Bukharin's theory? It lies in the fact that he mechanically conflates the kulak, capitalist economy with the socialist one, forgetting the antagonistic nature of the kulak economy in relation to the socialist economy, ignoring this fundamental and decisive difference, and, in his own theoretical speculations, forcing one hostile economic system (structure) into another. A kulak economy remains kulak as long as it is, in one form or another, based on the exploitation of the labor of others. Its internal, so to speak, organically inherent tendency is to displace, destroy, and disintegrate all other opposing economic structures (primarily the socialist one) and to completely dominate the field of struggle.

But the same tendency, the same dynamic of development, also applies to the socialist economy: it is torn apart in such a way that it displaces, defeats, and undermines elements of the capitalist economy and transforms simple small peasant farming along socialist lines; it also strives to occupy and does occupy, by virtue of its advantages, the entire field of struggle.

Between the kulak and socialist economies, under the correct Leninist policy in the countryside, as it was before 1928 and as it was bound to be in the future, there exist, of course, not simple forms of class struggle, but extremely complex, unique, and particular ones. On the one hand, the Party and the proletariat have no interest in inciting class struggle against the kulaks, for this would disrupt socialist construction; on the other hand, we cannot gloss over the contradictions between the kulaks and the proletarian dictatorship in the slightest, for this would inevitably lead to the degeneration of the proletarian dictatorship, to its demise.

Between the proletarian state and the kulaks there is a persistent class struggle going on all the time, but in this class struggle there are undoubtedly elements (albeit unequal) of mutual concessions, compromises, and a kind of “cooperation”.

The proletariat sets itself the task of eliminating (but, of course, not in the current Stalinist forms) capitalist elements, but at the same time, up to a certain limit and until a certain point, it is forced to "preserve" these elements; otherwise, their very elimination proves impossible, unthinkable. "Preserving" in order to eliminate them—such are the contradictions of socialist construction.

Bukharin saw and noted elements of the well-known, forced compromise on the part of the kulaks vis-à-vis the proletarian dictatorship, but he forgot the fundamental contradiction between the proletariat and the capitalist elements of the village. He forgot the struggle between them, overlooking the fact that the fundamental, decisive factor in the relationship between the workers' state and the kulaks was not compromise, but class struggle, albeit in its own unique forms.

What is the falsity and inconsistency of Stalin's theory? It is that, in its methodology, it is just as mechanistic and anti-dialectical as Bukharin's theory. It is that it sees only those factors that intensify the class struggle, while ignoring, concealing, and obscuring other, incomparably more important, decisive factors that act in the opposite direction, weakening (given the correct Leninist Party policy) the kulaks' resistance, isolating them from the masses, fragmenting and demoralizing them, causing passivity and a decline in their energy.

In one of his speeches, Stalin declared that there had never been an example in history of a dying class yielding its position without a fight. It will undoubtedly fight, defending every inch of its interests with a fight.[ 47 ] This is untrue. But such a general, naked formula proves absolutely nothing; it is, as usual, an empty and "loud" Stalinist slip of the tongue. If Stalin wanted to appeal to history to prove the correctness of his theory, he would have to show that history contains examples proving that, after the political dominance of a new class has been established following a revolution, the resistance of the dying classes, defeated in the struggle against the revolution, begins to grow and the struggle against it begins to intensify. Such an example would be quite convincing and convincing, but Stalin cannot cite precisely such examples, because they do not exist in history. On the contrary, the history of bourgeois revolutions in a number of countries offers classic examples of the exact opposite: after the bourgeoisie has triumphed and consolidated its rule, the resistance of the defeated nobility systematically weakens, and its class struggle with the new ruling class gradually softens. This is precisely what the history of the classical bourgeois revolution in France attests to, as does the history of the English revolutions.

L.M. Kaganovich, so beloved by Kharkov workers and party members, even attempted to exploit Marx's name three years ago at a meeting in Sverdlovsk to justify Stalinist theory. He claimed that Marx himself had emphasized, "The forces of revolution rally the forces of counterrevolution," while our opportunists, he claimed, fail to understand this and speak of a fading curve of class struggle. It would take a certain audacity, however, to exploit Marx's expression in such a deceptive manner. Is Marx's assertion correct? Undoubtedly so. But when Marx spoke of "class struggle," he was referring to the period of direct struggle for power between the two main contending forces.

While the armed struggle for power between the forces of revolution and counterrevolution continues, and victory is not yet definitively decided, the forces of revolution actually rally the forces of counterrevolution. When the enemy is defeated in open battle, however, the opposite process begins: if the counterrevolution wins, the forces of revolution are weakened and demoralized for a long period. Such are the consequences of the June defeat of the Paris workers[ 48 ], such are the consequences of the Paris Commune, such are the consequences of the defeat of the 1905 revolution in Russia. And, conversely, if the proletariat ultimately wins in open battle, then a period of weakening, disintegration, fragmentation, and disintegration of the forces of counterrevolution begins. Such are the consequences of the victory of the October Revolution. Moreover, if, after its defeat, the proletariat, after a period of enriched experience, rises to the struggle with renewed, tenfold strength—for the growth of capitalism inevitably entails the development, growth, and strengthening of the proletariat—then, after the defeat of the bourgeoisie, it is no longer capable of rising to the struggle even with its former strength, for the growth of the proletariat by no means signifies the previous growth of the bourgeoisie after a proletarian revolution. A certain growth of the new bourgeoisie during the recovery period, as occurred, for example, in the Soviet Union, is inevitable, which, of course, also leads to some intensification of its political and economic activity. However, with the transition to the reconstruction period, this process also reverses its direction.

Kaganovich doesn't care about any of this; he's only interested in using Marx's authority to support his "patron's" anti-Marxist theory and to deceive party members and workers.

Let's now consider how Stalin's theory harmonizes with his other statements and the decisions of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) over the past three to four years. Stalin asserted that as we advance along the path to socialism, the resistance of capitalist elements will increase and the class struggle will intensify. But at the same time, according to Stalin, in recent years we have: a) liquidated the kulaks as a class and uprooted capitalism; b) strengthened the alliance between the working class and the middle peasants, which has found expression, among other things, in the fact that the middle peasants have finally turned toward socialism; and c) experienced a rise in the material well-being and cultural level of political consciousness among the middle and poor peasant masses in the villages.

Stalin sees no contradiction between his theory and the assertions about the elimination of the kulaks as a class, the improvement of the material situation of the middle and poor peasant masses, and the fact that the middle peasants have irrevocably turned toward socialism. In fact, they are mutually exclusive.

What is the strength of the kulak? First and foremost, it lies in its economic might, which enables it to economically and politically subjugate certain groups of the poor and middle peasants. Secondly, it lies in the economic insecurity, lack of culture, and political backwardness of the masses. The kulak's strength in the village is not in itself. Even in 1927-1928, it represented a tiny fraction of the village population in the Soviet Union—no more than 5-6 percent. Its strength lies in the influence it exerts on the middle and poor peasant masses. But the displacement and subsequent elimination of the kulak class means undermining its economic, and at the same time, political, strength, weakening this strength. At the same time, the growth of the material well-being of the poor and middle peasant masses means a narrowing of the kulak's economic base and sphere of influence (and their cultural and political growth, a narrowing of its political one). The rise in the material standard of living and the political consciousness of the poor and middle peasant masses, their decisive shift toward collectivization while simultaneously undermining the economic roots of the kulaks, marks the kulaks' political isolation, i.e., the weakening of their capitalist influence and resistance. According to Stalin's theory, however, everything is the other way around. The result is an incredible mess, one thing contradicting the other. The right hand doesn't know what the left is doing, and this is passed off as Leninism! This is the case with the "strengthening resistance of the kulaks."

Things are no better with the "intensification of class struggle as we advance toward socialism." First of all, the intensification of class struggle against the kulaks and the strengthening of their resistance are far from coinciding. Stalin doesn't understand this either.

What is the intensification of class struggle? It is the transition from the lower forms of class struggle to the higher, from sabotage to sabotage, from sabotage to rebellion by counterrevolutionary elements under the dictatorship of the proletariat, from an economic strike to a political strike, from a political strike to a general mass political strike; from a strike to an insurrection. Insurrection is the highest and most acute form of class struggle.

History shows us numerous examples of how the strengthening of the resistance of one struggling class to another is always associated with an intensification of the class struggle on the part of one or another class. Quite often, in history, there have been periods when the resistance of that class weakened, and when the transition to higher, more acute forms of class struggle was not an expression of the strengthening of its resistance, but rather of its weakening, the "last gasp" of that resistance. Such were some of the uprisings of the petty nobility in Western Europe during the final consolidation of royal power; such were the last counterrevolutionary uprisings in France during the Great French Revolution [ 49 ]; such was our Georgian uprising in 1924.

At certain moments, perishing classes may thus shift to more acute forms of class struggle. This is possible for a number of reasons. For example, international complications, intervention, or conspiracy by individual kulak groups, even under correct policies and the rule of the proletariat. Can this, however, be used to establish a law of inevitable intensification of class struggle as we advance along the path to socialism? Absolutely not!

Given a correct party policy, given a genuine strengthening of the alliance between the working class and the middle peasants and reliance on the poor, given a genuine turn of the middle peasants towards socialism, given a genuine improvement in the situation of the working masses in the villages, given a growth in the political consciousness of the main masses of the villages, given the undermining and narrowing of the economic base of the kulaks and their political isolation – the fundamental law of class struggle for the Soviet Union must be formulated in exactly the opposite way: **as we advance along the path to socialism, the resistance of the capitalist elements will weaken, and the class struggle will soften and gradually die down**.

And no matter how much Stalin and his clique of "theoreticians" shout against this proposition, no matter how many arbitrarily they pick out individual quotations from Lenin and interpret them at random, they cannot bring forward a single serious objection to this proposition. Stalin's theory, despite its apparent external "revolutionary nature," has nothing in common with Marxism-Leninism, nor does it have anything in common with the program of the Comintern. The Comintern program on this issue states the following: "Thus, already being the decisive economic force, which basically determines the entire development of the economy in the USSR, socialism is thereby taking further major steps in its development, systematically overcoming the difficulties arising from the petty-bourgeois character of the country and associated with periods of temporary exacerbation of class contradictions."[ 50 ]

Firstly, the Comintern Program doesn't mention a word about increasing resistance on the part of capitalist elements, and secondly, it speaks only of periods of "temporary aggravation of class contradictions," not of an intensification of class struggle as we advance along the path to socialism. There is a clear, fundamental, and fundamental discrepancy between the Comintern Program and Stalin's "theory." The Comintern Program speaks of the same "temporary aggravation of class struggle" that we also discuss, while Stalin established the law of "increasing resistance on the part of capitalist elements and intensification of class struggle" for the entire duration of our advance toward a socialist society. Moreover, Stalin and Co. have become so confused and deceitful that they believe that even in a socialist society, an intensification of class struggle is inevitable. According to Molotov (and therefore also to Stalin, for Molotov is only a shadow of Stalin), as we have seen, the USSR is already living in a socialist society, albeit in its initial stages. During the Second Five-Year Plan, the construction of a socialist society, according to the resolution of the 17th Party Conference, will make gigantic progress. The primary political task of the Second Five-Year Plan, states the resolution, is "the final elimination of capitalist elements and classes in general." Classes, therefore, will be finally eliminated during the Second Five-Year Plan; there will be no classes. At the same time, as the resolution emphasizes, an intensification of the class struggle is inevitable (at certain moments, and especially in certain regions and in certain sections of the socialist construction project). Classes "will be finally eliminated," and "the class struggle will intensify at certain moments." Could there be a more vulgar, slanderous caricature of Marx and Lenin's teachings on socialist society? Could there be a more shameful counterfeit of Marx and Lenin's brilliant and dialectical analysis of the contradictory features inherent in the first stage of communism (socialist society)?

It is undeniable that even a genuine socialist society, and not Stalin's vile, slanderous caricature and mockery of it, will, as Marx says, in every respect—economically, morally, and intellectually—still bear the imprint of the old society from whose depths it emerged. In a socialist society (the first phase of communism), complete equality will not yet exist; bourgeois law is not yet completely abolished, but only partially, etc. Lenin writes on this subject that life reveals to us at every step the remnants of the old in the new—both in nature and in society. And Marx did not arbitrarily insert a bit of "bourgeois law" into communism, but took what is economically and politically inevitable in a society emerging from the depths of capitalism.

It's perfectly natural that classes will disappear, but certain traces, echoes, and reflections of class struggle, gradually fading as we approach socialism, will be felt for a certain period in socialist society. But to confuse traces, echoes, and reflections of class struggle with the intensification of class struggle is to be either Marxist-illiterate, a political crook, or both.

Some jurors, the official defenders of the "general line," will try to cite our Soviet reality in defense of the theory of intensifying class struggle. In reality, isn't the class struggle intensifying? Doesn't that justify Stalin's point of view?

It would be absurd to deny the enormous intensification of class struggle in the Soviet Union. This intensification has reached unprecedented proportions. The country has been seething with uprisings for the past three years. Here and there, despite the incredible terror inflicted on the working class, strikes are breaking out; hunger and poverty among the masses have reached horrific proportions; masses of party members, workers, and the main strata of the rural population are burning with indignation and hatred toward Stalin and his clique.

The intensification of the class struggle is evident. But this intensification is not due to our advancement toward socialism, but (despite the construction of a number of major factories, as we have already shown elsewhere) to a movement backward and away from socialist society. Not to an improvement in the material conditions of the masses, but to an unprecedented deterioration. Not to an increase in the activity and creativity of the masses, not to the development of party and soviet democracy, but to the suppression of their activity and the complete strangulation of party and soviet democracy. In short, the intensification of the class struggle in recent years is not the result of correct policies, but, on the contrary, the result of incorrect policies. It testifies not to Leninist leadership of the country, but to an anti-Leninist, adventurist policy. Even in their heyday, Eastern despotisms were often shaken to their very foundations, and sometimes even suffered complete collapse. The adventurist policies of individual Roman emperors repeatedly set the Roman Empire back for decades, even during periods of its heyday. Adventurist policies led to failures and defeats for some bourgeois states. Adventurist policies have already undermined and can destroy the first proletarian state. The adventurism of many statesmen, their stupidity, incompetence, fraud, and so on, ultimately form part of the general pattern of historical development, but adventurism does not cease to be adventurism, nor fraud to be fraud. Just as the leaders of bourgeois states can pursue a more or less sober, balanced policy from the standpoint of their class, or they can pursue a disastrous, harmful, and adventurist policy, so too the leaders of a proletarian state, if they abandon the positions of Leninism, must inevitably take the path of opportunism or adventurism. The latter is what happened to Stalin.

Let us now, in conclusion, examine the development of the theory of "intensification of class struggle" and its implementation. The key here is Stalin's slogan, "Liquidation of the kulaks as a class through complete collectivization"—that is, the transition from a policy of restricting and displacing capitalist elements in the countryside to a policy of eliminating the kulaks as a class.

The overwhelming majority of genuine kulaks, as is well known, immediately after the emergency measures began to liquidate their businesses and flee to the cities. By the end of 1928, they had already been largely liquidated as a class; a small number were shot and imprisoned, while the majority found various jobs as workers in factories, railroads, construction sites, warehouse guards, stokers, janitors, grooms, cooks, clerks, accountants, bartenders, and so on. A significant number found work simply as dependents on their city relatives and close acquaintances. But the slogan of "liquidating the kulaks as a class" began to be implemented with particular force precisely from the beginning of 1930. Naturally, its overwhelming target was the middle and poor peasants. The circle of those classified as kulaks thus gradually expanded, and by the beginning of 1932, the number of dispossessed kulaks in some areas, relative to the total rural population of these areas, reached 30-35%. If we include all those individually taxed and those subject to firm assignments ("tverdyshi"), then in some areas it even reaches 40-50%. The total number of the entire rural population of the Soviet Union subjected to various forms of repression by party and Soviet organs over the past four years (dekulakization, arrests, executions, exile, firm assignments, fines, searches, threats, etc.) in any case constitutes no less than 40-50% of the entire rural population. Under such conditions, it is clear that "the resistance of capitalist elements will increase, the class struggle will intensify," for, essentially, from 1930 onward, the struggle in the villages was no longer against the kulak, but against the middle and poor peasants, the bulk of the village population. It was precisely the middle and poor peasants who primarily found themselves imprisoned in "kulak" concentration camps; from 1930 onward, the middle and poor peasants constituted the primary targets of executions and all manner of punitive expeditions employing artillery and aircraft; the middle and poor peasants constituted the bulk of those exiled to the northern regions, Central Asia, Kazakhstan, and other parts of the Soviet Union.

To facilitate the "enumeration" of the middle and poor peasants into kulaks, Stalin invented new terms—"the kulak-wealthy upper crust of the village," "podkulachniki," and so on—that had not been used in any Party lexicon before 1928. While prior to 1928, Party decisions, resolutions of Party committees, conferences, and congresses (including the 15th Congress) had dealt with the precise term and concept of "kulak," now this term was quietly replaced by the extremely elastic and vague concept of "the kulak-wealthy upper crust." The political purpose of the term “kulak-wealthy elite” was to classify the “required” number of middle peasants as kulaks, and the political purpose of the term “podkulachnik” was to ensure that every protest of the middle and poor peasants against the criminal policy of violence and abuse of the main masses of the village could be qualified as a protest of a “podkulachnik,” a “class enemy,” and that the protester could be dealt with as an “agent” of the kulak.

The fabrication of kulaks from the middle and poor peasants is simultaneously associated with the most vile persecution, accusations of opportunism and the expulsion from the party of local workers who are unable to find the necessary percentage of kulaks or who do not find them at all.

As a result of this policy, those considered poor or middle peasants in 1929 became kulaks in 1930, or were elevated from poor to middle peasants, or even, in some cases, straight to kulaks. In 1930 and 1931, all these operations were repeated, the only difference being that with each passing year they assumed increasingly gigantic proportions.

Stalin, as is typical of a dishonest, unprincipled politician, here too portrays the inclusion of the middle peasants as kulaks as isolated excesses “on the ground,” as minor shortcomings in a large mechanism, and shifts the blame onto others.

In reality, these are not "isolated" errors, not "local excesses," but the essence and result of a general policy and line, the result of an entire system of measures, one of the concrete manifestations of violence, tyranny, and abuse of the rural working masses, the result of Stalin's complete break with the principles of Leninism in general and with the principles of Leninism on the peasant question in particular. Even today, Stalin trumpets the resistance of the kulaks in his press day after day. In fact, kulaks, as a distinct socio-economic category engaged in one form or another in the exploitation of the labor of others, have not existed in the villages, at least not since the end of 1929. Even former kulaks (with the exception of a tiny handful who found work on state farms, in logging camps, wandered around in rebel detachments, or simply begged in the villages) no longer exist. But Stalin cannot acknowledge this indisputable fact. He, like a fish in water, needs cries about kulak resistance and "opportunists." For him, these cries now serve a function similar to that of the cries about "Yids," "criminal agitators," and "internal enemies" for the autocracy.

Unable to tell the truth about the fact that the bulk of the village is in a state of merciless and cruel struggle against Stalin's policies, he is forced to cover up this struggle with the fig leaf of "kulak resistance" to the successes of building socialism.

Finally, Stalin's slogan of "liquidation of the kulaks as a class" is anti-Leninist not only because its main thrust is directed against the middle and poor peasants. ## 8. Simple Reproduction and Marxism

Confused on one issue and unable to admit his mistakes, Stalin is forced to carry this confusion and distortion of Leninism through the entire chain of political and theoretical issues.

We also find distortions of Marx's and Lenin's teachings in his discussion of extended and simple reproduction in small peasant farming. We briefly touched on this issue, having previously demonstrated Stalin's lack of principles. Now we will illustrate his illiteracy on this issue once again. Let us recall Stalin's "theoretical discovery," made at a conference of Marxist agrarians. In his speech, he declared: "Can it be said that our small peasant farming is developing according to the principle of extended reproduction? No, it cannot be said. Not only does our small peasant farming fail to realize extended reproduction annually, but, on the contrary, it does not always have the capacity to even realize simple reproduction. Is it possible to further accelerate our socialist industry with such an agricultural base as small peasant farming, which is incapable of extended reproduction and which, moreover, represents the predominant force in our national economy?" “No, it’s impossible.”[ 52 ].

So, it's as clear as day: the bulk of our small peasant farming, that is, the middle peasant farming, is not developing according to the principle of expanded reproduction. Small peasant farming in the Soviet Union is incapable of expanded reproduction. It is either stagnant or declining, degrading.

Did Karl Marx raise the question of simple reproduction for capitalism? “Simple reproduction,” he says, “reproduction on an unchanging scale is an abstraction in the sense that, on the one hand, the absence of any accumulation, or reproduction on an extended scale, is an implausible assumption given the existence of a capitalist basis, and, on the other hand, the relations in which production takes place do not remain absolutely unchanged in different years” [ 53 ]. According to Marx, therefore, even with the existence of a capitalist basis, simple reproduction is “an implausible assumption.” According to Stalin, even with a socialist basis, simple reproduction is typical for the bulk of agricultural producers. Marx, Engels, and Lenin emphasize that in agriculture under the dominance of capitalism, the same laws of development generally apply as in industry: large-scale production has all the advantages over small-scale production, and large-scale production overcomes and displaces small-scale production. In agriculture, although more slowly (due to the presence of rent and some other secondary reasons), the same process of concentration and centralization of capital, the process of expropriation of small producers, occurs as in industry. “To the same extent,” writes Marx, “in which agriculture becomes a simple branch of industry, capitalist production is established in the village” [ 54 ].

The main tendency of capitalism “consists in the displacement of small-scale production by large-scale production, both in industry and in agriculture”[ 55 ].

All these propositions were and remain universally accepted among Marxist-Leninists. They are all indisputable. They have been confirmed by the entire course of capitalist development. However, until now, not a single literate Marxist-Leninist has ever thought to assert that small peasant farming, even under capitalism, is incapable of expanded reproduction. Such an assertion, firstly, would reveal theoretical ignorance, and secondly, it would contradict the facts.

The displacement of small-scale production by large-scale production and the advantages of large-scale production over small-scale production in agriculture are one thing, but the inability to expand reproduction is quite another. Stalin, due to his theoretical limitations, believes that since small-scale production is being displaced by large-scale production, it is incapable of expanded reproduction. In reality, it is being displaced, despite the fact that it is engaged in expanded production. It goes without saying that the rate of expanded production in small-scale peasant farming is incomparably slower than in large-scale farming: small-scale peasant farming cannot utilize many of the large-scale agricultural machinery and technical improvements used in large-scale agriculture. It can utilize other machinery and improvements, but to a very limited extent. It can also utilize advances in agronomy and other sciences related to agriculture to a much lesser extent than large-scale farming. Nevertheless (albeit to a limited extent compared to large-scale capitalist farming), small-scale peasant farming benefits from both the advances of agronomy and the development of capitalist technology. These decisive circumstances create the fundamental conditions for expanded reproduction in small-scale peasant farming. The use of certain household and simple agricultural machinery, the transition from a three-field system to a multiple-field system and to a large-scale farm, from grain to industrial crops, from beef to dairy and butter farming, to commercial pig and poultry farming, plus backbreaking labor—this is the foundation, a very narrow foundation compared to large-scale capitalist agriculture, upon which expanded reproduction in small-scale peasant farming relies.

The Marxists' dispute with David, Hertz[ 56 ], and the Narodniks was not at all about the inability of small-scale peasant farming to expand "reproduction," but about the fact that the same laws of capitalist development operate in agriculture as in industry, that large-scale agriculture has every advantage over small-scale agriculture, and that small-scale peasant farming, in the struggle against large-scale capitalist agriculture, survives only through the backbreaking labor and exhaustion of the peasant. Large-scale production displaces small-scale production, and small-scale production in agriculture is ultimately doomed to disappear and perish. But this is not at all what Stalin told us!

Neither in Marx, nor in Engels, nor in Lenin, nor in Kautsky, nor in Plekhanov - nowhere in the entire world Marxist literature will you find even a hint of the assertion that small peasant farming on a capitalist basis is not capable of expanded reproduction.

On the contrary, as we have seen, Marx asserts the exact opposite. He says that “the absence of any accumulation, or reproduction on an extended scale, is an implausible assumption given the existence of a capitalist basis”[ 57 ].

For Marx, simple reproduction, even under capitalism, is an abstraction, but according to Stalin, even under conditions of extensive socialist construction, simple reproduction dominates the economy of the overwhelming majority of the rural population!

Let's see what follows from this. Under capitalism, according to Marx, development even in small peasant farming occurs according to the principle of expanded reproduction. Under proletarian dictatorship, however, as Stalin asserts, the bulk of peasant farming either stagnates or reduces its production. Translated into simple terms, this means the impoverishment of the bulk of the rural population.

Party decisions and Stalin himself, however, have consistently asserted the exact opposite. Specifically, that we are seeing a steady improvement in the situation of the bulk of the rural population. How can one reconcile the two? Further. If, under capitalism, extended reproduction is achieved in the individual farming of the main middle peasant masses of the village, while under a proletarian dictatorship and socialist construction, simple reproduction or even negative extended reproduction is achieved, then how is a steady strengthening of the alliance between the working class and the peasantry possible on such a basis? And Stalin presents such nonsense to the masses as the final word of Marxism-Leninism! People can only utter such illiterate nonsense when they know in advance that no one will dare contradict them or find a platform to do so, for all platforms are at the personal disposal of the "leader" and under reliable guard.

It's curious that back in April 1928, just a year and a half before the conference of Marxist agrarians, he was preaching the exact opposite to the Institute of Red Professors. He said: "All the data indicate that peasant farm yields could be increased by 15-20 percent within a few years. We currently have at least 5 million wooden plows in use. Replacing them with plows alone could significantly increase grain production in the country. I'm not even talking about supplying peasant farms with a certain minimum of fertilizers, cleaned seeds, small-scale machinery, etc. The contracting method, the method of concluding agreements with entire villages and hamlets for the supply of seeds, etc., with the obligatory condition of receiving from them an appropriate quantity of grain products—this method is the best way to increase peasant farm yields and involve peasants in cooperatives. I think that with serious work in this direction, in 3-4 years we could have (additionally) at least 100 million poods of new marketable grain from small and medium-sized individual peasant farms”[ 58 ].

It is absolutely clear that Stalin here asserts the existence of opportunities for individual peasant farming (not only middle peasant farming, but also poor peasant farming) for expanded reproduction.

A year and a half later, however, he asserts the opposite. And both theories continue to coexist peacefully under the leaky roof of Stalin's consistency and erudition. Theoretical illiteracy, coupled with unscrupulousness, politicking, and eclecticism—this is the true name for Stalin's theory of "simple reproduction" in small-scale peasant farming.

The anti-Marxist, anti-Leninist content of Stalin's theory has a single political purpose. It lies in providing a theoretical basis for forced forms of collectivization. Indeed, if the individual peasant farming of the middle peasant class has no prospects for expanded reproduction without being transformed into a capitalist kulak economy, then industrialization reaches a dead end; it lacks an agricultural foundation. Therefore, a choice must be made: either force collectivization by all means, refusing to shy away from brutal measures of direct and indirect coercion and violence (maybe it will work out), or put an end to industrialization and socialist construction. Adventurist policies require theoretical justification. It was for this justification that Stalin concocted the theory described above.

## 9. Evaluation of the intra-party struggle in light of the lessons of past years

Over the past five years, we have witnessed Stalin and his apparatus struggle with two main oppositions: the Trotskyist opposition led by Trotsky and the opposition led by Bukharin. Now, in light of the lessons of the past years, it is more necessary than ever to make a calm, objective assessment of where each opposition went wrong, if any, and where, if anything, they were right.

Experience has shown that the Trotskyist opposition was undoubtedly mistaken on the decisive economic issues of the balance of class forces in the country. The Trotskyist platform on industrialization policy, wage policy, tax policy toward the poor on the one hand and the kulaks on the other, price policy, and the role and relative importance of the kulaks in the countryside and private capital in the city, was clearly demagogic. Instead of a concrete, sober Marxist-Leninist analysis, we had the undeniable dominance of leftist phrases, flirting with the masses, and unfulfilled promises.

In early 1928, Stalin resolutely began implementing the economic component of the Trotskyist platform, something the Trotskyists themselves openly acknowledged, citing as one of the decisive reasons for returning to the party and supporting the new policy. The negative consequences of this policy, however, were not long in becoming apparent. With the onset of this policy, an economic crisis began to develop: a reduction in crops on peasant farms, a reduction and destruction of livestock, rising prices, a rapid decline in the chervonets, a decline in real wages, disorganization in planning, a decline in the interest of the bulk of the rural population in developing their economy, and so on.

Stalin, of course, took the Trotskyist economic platform to its logical conclusion, to the point of absurdity, but this was far from accidental: "Once the claw gets stuck, the whole bird is lost." In Stalin's "New Course," every measure, with its inexorable, ironclad economic necessity, led to a second, a third, and so on. Here, however, it wasn't Stalin who directed events, but events who directed Stalin. The descent down this steep slope could only be halted by decisively and irrevocably abandoning the erroneous, demagogic assumptions and making a sharp turn. Stalin is completely incapable of this.

L.D. Trotsky and the Trotskyists, one must assume, would have proven more honest and dedicated to the cause of the proletarian revolution: they would have been able to recognize their "debatable errors" in time and turn around. The assessment of Stalin's role and the situation within the party is entirely different. On this crucial and crucial issue for the fate of the proletarian revolution, Trotsky and the Trotskyists were, on the contrary, largely correct. Trotsky, earlier than others, saw the processes within the party that had already begun to develop in 1923. Trotsky, earlier than others, also saw Stalin's desire to establish his personal dictatorship within the party.

When Trotsky, back in 1924, wrote in his "New Course" that the party lived on two floors: "in the upper level they decide, while in the lower level they only learn about decisions"—when he said that the party apparatus, despite the ideological "growth of the party," continued to stubbornly think and decide for it—party workers responded that Trotsky's assertions were sheer slander. Who, however, can honestly say today that Trotsky was mistaken? But back then, compared to today, the pulse of intra-party life was still beating normally; back then, at least, the "two-story" party life was recognized as an abnormal phenomenon. The only debate was whether this abnormality existed or not. Now, however, the two-story life, where "in the upper level they decide, while in the lower level they only learn about decisions," has become not only "normal." Everyone has become so accustomed to this that they don’t even notice the complete break with the principles of intra-party democracy and democratic centralism in such a “two-story” existence.

Trotsky further wrote in his "New Course": "The main danger of the old course, as it arose as a result of both major historical causes and our own mistakes, is that it reveals a tendency toward increasingly pitting the few thousand comrades who constitute the leading cadres against the rest of the party mass as objects of influence. If this regime were stubbornly maintained, it would undoubtedly threaten to ultimately cause the degeneration of the party—and simultaneously at both its poles, that is, both in the party youth and in the leading cadres."

And this prediction of Trotsky's has now also been fully vindicated. The degeneration of the party is proceeding at such rapid rates, it has already gone so far, that only decisive and bold operational measures can save the situation.

In this timely and correct Leninist uncovering of the embryos of the imperceptible degeneration of the party that had begun, in Trotsky's passionate desire to return the party to the path of internal party democracy and healthy democratic centralism lies Trotsky's enormous historical and revolutionary merit, which no slander or past mistakes can take away from him.

## 11. The Crisis of the Comintern

After characterizing Stalin, his methods of struggle and his theoretical positions, we can move on to the international situation and our internal situation.

The objective economic preconditions and conditions for the overthrow of capitalism throughout the world have long been present. Imperialism is on the eve of a global socialist revolution. The capitalist system is experiencing a general crisis. The current global economic crisis, of unprecedented depth and breadth, is developing within the framework of the general crisis of capitalism and, in its specific features, is a reflection and expression of the general crisis of capitalism. The existence of a proletarian dictatorship on one-sixth of the globe, despite the crisis that the proletarian dictatorship itself is experiencing, stemming from purely subjective causes and incorrect anti-Leninist leadership, is the most striking expression and manifestation of the incipient disintegration and collapse of capitalist society.

The material prerequisites for the victory of socialism are present in all capitalist countries. What is lacking are the subjective prerequisites, and above all, the mass influence of these parties' leadership among the proletarian and petty-bourgeois working masses of the city and countryside. There are not enough cadres, not enough theoretically mature, seasoned, and consistent activists. Finally, there are not enough authoritative cadres with a broad Marxist outlook and a solid theoretical background, capable of independently posing and resolving both general theoretical questions and questions of strategy and tactics in the class struggle of leaders.

Under these conditions, proper leadership of the Comintern is crucial to the fate of the world proletarian revolution. Meanwhile, in reality, we have a picture of the exact opposite. The leadership of the Comintern in recent years has been of such a nature and has taken such forms that strengthening its position is out of the question. On the contrary, the Comintern is currently experiencing an undeniable crisis. This crisis is expressed in the following:

1. The Comintern, from the headquarters of the world proletarian revolution, which it actually was under the leadership of Lenin, Zinoviev, and to some extent even under the leadership of Bukharin, was transformed, after the defeat of all oppositions and comrades of Lenin, after the establishment of Stalin's personal dictatorship in the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and the Comintern, into simply Stalin's chancery for the affairs of the communist parties.

If Louis XIV said, “France is me,” then Stalin now says with the same confidence: “The Comintern is me.”

2. Under Lenin and Zinoviev, the Comintern's core leadership was filled with genuine leaders, each of whom boldly and independently raised fundamental theoretical and political questions, unafraid of false accusations of deviation, unafraid of being vilified and spit upon for their mistakes, but knowing in advance that only their comrades would correct them. A vibrant, creative Bolshevik mind constantly throbbed within the Comintern's core leadership. No one was afraid to contradict Lenin or Zinoviev, for they fought errors with the force of argument, not with force. Today, on the contrary, the Comintern lacks leaders, but only executive officials, afraid to utter an unnecessary word, to express an independent, bold thought, constantly looking over their shoulder at a vindictive boss who lacks understanding of the means of struggle. Even some former genuine leaders, having completed a course of Stalinist studies in recent years, have turned into simple Stalinist serfs, into heroes of “whatever you please.”

There is not a single work, either philosophical, political economics, or any other social science, worthy of mention. All the theoretical wisdom of recent times has been embodied for the Comintern in Stalin's illiterate, stupid, and mendacious article – a letter to the editors of Proletarian Revolution [ 61 ]. All dialectical materialism has been forced into the Procrustean bed of the "six conditions" [ 62 ].

Stalin is now officially the Comintern's infallible pope. ## 12. The crisis of the proletarian dictatorship

### _I. Economic crisis_

It finds its expression and manifestation: first, in the economic crisis, encompassing both the socialist economy and individual peasant farming; second, in the gigantic crisis of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks); and third, in the crisis of the entire mechanism of the proletarian dictatorship and its transmission belts (the Soviets, trade unions, cooperatives, the press, etc.).

Under normal, ordinary conditions, statistics serve as a vital tool for understanding economic processes. For socialist construction, statistics are an indispensable planning tool. Without a conscientious and rigorous approach to compiling statistical reports and planned statistical appendices, there can be no planned socialist construction, nor can there be normal, largely crisis-free, socialist development. Socialism, said Lenin, is accounting.

But currently, only a hopeless idiot would trust Stalin's statistics. "Stalin's statistics" serve not to substantiate the truth, but to conceal it, to deceive the masses. In the future, Marxist statisticians and historians will undoubtedly write entire scholarly works on how Stalin used his "statistics" to deceive the masses of party members and workers about his own growth rates.

There's an English joke that statistics can prove anything. For Stalin, however, this joke became a true maxim, a guiding principle for highlighting the "successes" of social construction with statistical data.

District workers deceive in statistical data on the area sown to cotton, flax, and wheat; they deceive in statistical data on the filling of seed funds; factories deceive in the fulfillment of industrial and financial plans. Deception and deception are everywhere! And once again, district workers and directors are to blame. Truly scapegoats and whipping boys! Why, however, was this deception absent five or six years ago? Why has it become a widespread, typical, characteristic phenomenon only in recent years? Because Stalin's entire policy is nothing but deception, a complete deception of the masses. A policy of inflated tempos and inflated plans with mandatory demands for their fulfillment and overfulfillment, a policy of blatant and false reports of victories, in blatant contradiction with reality, is inevitably linked to false statistics. Returning to statistics, it can be said, at least with certainty, that Stalin's statistics can only be used with extreme caution, and then only as a supplementary source of information to establish the true picture of the economy.

We will have to draw conclusions only on the basis of numerous observations and eyewitness testimony and their corresponding processing, which can, to a certain extent, replace the method of selective statistical survey.

The main and characteristic feature of the economic crisis is, firstly, the decline in the country's fixed capital, despite the construction of a dozen major factories. According to Gosplan, the country's fixed capital in 1927 (when statistics were still accurate), amounted to 61 billion rubles. Of this, industry and transportation accounted for 21 billion rubles; urban housing construction – 14 billion rubles; all rural buildings – 14 billion rubles; all livestock – 9 billion rubles; and other agricultural equipment – ​​3 billion rubles.

During this period, capital investments amount to approximately 9 billion rubles in 1927 prices, of which no more than 7 billion are fixed assets, while the remainder are under construction or mothballed in projects begun but suspended. Industrial fixed assets therefore now amount to 28 billion rubles. Since 1927 (in 1927 prices), approximately 2 billion rubles have been invested in new urban housing construction; however, at the same time, the old housing stock in thousands of county and provincial cities and dozens of large towns (with the exception of a small number of cities such as Moscow, Leningrad, and Kharkov) has not been repaired at all; on the contrary, it has been deteriorating at an unusually rapid rate. Considering that this stock consists, to a significant extent, of wooden structures requiring frequent and thorough repairs, depreciation costs must amount to no less than 3–3.5% annually, which amounts to the same 2 billion rubles over four years (1928–1931). The urban housing stock remains unchanged in value terms. Meanwhile, the rural capital stock has shrunk dramatically. At least 30% of the rural housing stock and other buildings have been destroyed over the past four years. This amounts to 4.2 billion rubles out of 14 billion. Meanwhile, collective farm construction costs, at best, amount to 200 million rubles. Agricultural implements—wagons, sleds, harnesses, and plows—are at least 50% destroyed, broken, scattered, and torn. This amounts to 1.5 billion rubles. Finally, according to the general observations of all conscientious observers, the number of livestock in the countryside has decreased as a result of forced collectivization over the entire period of the adventuristic, anti-Leninist policy: horses by 70%, cows by 10%, pigs and sheep by 85-90%, and chickens by 70-80%. On average, we have seen a reduction in livestock production of at least 70%. This amounts to 6.3 billion rubles. We can only counter this with 100,000 tractors, which, with a tractor costing 2,000 rubles, gives us only 200 million. Even if we add another 300 million rubles for the remaining agricultural machinery acquired by the village in recent years (and their purchase has almost ceased), the total will still amount to 500 million rubles. This still leaves us with a minus of 5.5 billion rubles. As a result, the fixed capital of the village currently amounts to only 14 billion rubles instead of 26 billion rubles (9.8 billion for buildings, 1.5 billion for inventory, plus 2.7 billion for livestock). The fixed capital of the country, therefore, currently amounts to: 28 billion rubles for industry and transport, 14 billion rubles for urban housing, and 14 billion rubles for agriculture—a total of 56.5 billion rubles. Against 61 billion rubles in 1927, i.e. Overall, we have seen a reduction in the country's fixed capital over the last 4 years by 5 billion rubles, or 8.5%.

The fixed capital of industry has increased by 30% over the past 4 years, but the fixed capital of agriculture has decreased by almost 45%.

But alongside the country's shrinking fixed capital, its working capital has also shrunk enormously: the decline in gold reserves, food, clothing, furniture, industrial raw materials, and so on, amounts to at least 10 billion rubles. We are currently living literally without any reserves, without any reserves from day to day! Like a losing gambler rummages, searching his pocket for the last nickel to place his final bet, so Stalin and his clique are squeezing the last valuables, the last gold crosses and rings, out of the population. They are selling precious paintings, carpets, and valuable antique trinkets abroad for pennies to avoid bankruptcy today, only to face the same fateful question tomorrow.

The country's fixed and working capital thus declined by no less than 17-20 billion rubles, the agricultural industrial base was fundamentally undermined, and industrialization was in limbo. These are the true, tangible results of Stalin's leadership of socialist construction.

Our industry, despite its capital stock increasing by 30%, is also experiencing a profound crisis due to the erosion of the raw materials and financial tax base for industrialization. The resolution of the 17th All-Union Party Conference states that the output of all socialist industry in 1931 amounted to 27 billion rubles, representing a 21% increase over 1930. Let's examine whether the figure of 27 billion rubles and these percentages are accurate.

Let us compare these figures with the data Molotov presented at the 15th Party Congress.

Molotov, in his report in March 1931 at the 6th Congress of Soviets, said: “The growth of industry at the Supreme Council of the National Economy over two years ensured an increase in gross output from 9.5 to 15.6 billion rubles, i.e. by 64% against the 41% envisaged by the five-year plan”[ 65 ].

Thus, in the second year of the five-year plan, the industrial output of the Supreme Council of the National Economy (SCNE) in 1926–27 prices amounted to 15.6 billion rubles. In the third (1931), the "decisive" year of the five-year plan, according to the resolution of the 17th Party Conference, we see a 21% increase in output compared to the previous year.

Twenty-one percent of 15.6 billion rubles equals 3.3 billion rubles. By the end of 1931, we had 18.9 billion rubles in 1926-27 prices. Where did the 27 billion rubles come from? It's perfectly clear that the calculations were based not on 1926-27 prices, but on 1931 prices. But to use 1931 prices when the value of the chervonets is plummeting and not mention it in the resolution is, firstly, to overwhelm with figures, to engage in deception and window dressing, something the Stalin clique accuses of the lower-level workers; secondly, to calculate costs using the steadily rising prices of 1931 is to provide no real definition of the magnitude of production growth. This statistical trick by Stalin and company alone suggests that it would have been necessary at all costs to increase the rate to at least 20%.

So, we have obvious statistical fraud from above. But it is also compounded by the receipt of falsified, exaggerated data directly from the factories, as those who failed to fulfill plans were dismissed, brought to trial, accused of opportunism, and so on. It's no coincidence that Stalin's newspapers are now screaming about the fraudulent practices of many enterprises and directors. Add to this the enormous deterioration in product quality, coupled with rising prices. If we apply the entirety of modern production, even to the quality of 1926-1927, this would represent a reduction in the actual cost of production of at least 30-40%. Then we must add the enormous increase in production costs, thanks to the enormous industrial downtime, which is growing year after year due to a shortage of raw materials and inflated planning. Finally, Stalin's statistics ignore the fact that four years ago, handicraft industry also accounted for a significant percentage of the industrial output that contributed to the country's overall industrial and trade turnover. At the present moment, we have a reduction in the production of handicraft industry by at least 35%.

### II. Party Crisis

The Marxist-Leninist understanding of the most important theoretical and, at the same time, political issues—the struggle against opportunism, mass struggle, class struggle, the nature of socialist society, the objective and subjective factors in socialist construction, the principles of socialist industrialization, the Party's policy in the countryside, and collectivization—and the practical application of this understanding have been replaced by empty, false, and blatant "leftist phrases" that blatantly contradict facts and reality. The theoretical, and at the same time, practical, formulation of the crucial question of the struggle against opportunism for Bolshevism has been trivialized, vulgarized to the utmost degree, transformed into a caricature and simply a means to justify Stalin's policies, terrorize dissenters, and serve as a whip for the driver during all sorts of campaigns.

The party is dominated by incredible theoretical confusion and fear not only of posing any new theoretical problem, which is now absolutely impossible, but also of any more or less independent thought.

On the other hand, towering over these organs is an absolute dictator, effectively irreplaceable by anyone, effectively uncontrolled by anyone, effectively unaccountable to anyone, concentrating in his hands tens of times greater power, exercising tens of times more personal tyranny, abuse, and violence against the masses and the country than any former monarch of any absolutist state.

Second, by its ability to link up, to a certain extent, if you will, to merge with the broadest masses of workers, primarily the proletarian, but also the non-proletarian working masses. Third, by the correctness of the political leadership exercised by this vanguard, the correctness of its political strategy and tactics, provided that the broadest masses are convinced of this correctness by their own experience. Without these conditions, discipline in a revolutionary party truly capable of being the party of the advanced class, whose goal is to overthrow the bourgeoisie and transform the whole of society, is impossible." Without these conditions, attempts to create discipline inevitably turn into empty talk, into phrases, into grimaces”[ 72 ].

At the present time we do not have any of these conditions for the existence of genuine Bolshevik discipline in the party.

The existence and seriousness of the processes of degeneration at the top of the party and Soviet apparatus are undeniable. Moreover, it is characteristic that this time in history, this degeneration is occurring according to all the classic examples of degeneration: the true followers of a doctrine are declared persecuted "heretics," while its falsifiers and distorters are declared its true followers. This is how the degeneration of primitive Christianity occurred, this is how the degeneration of the top brass of German Social Democracy and the Second International occurred, and this is how it is happening here too. It goes without saying that even the Stalinist clique itself, while deliberately distorting Leninism and the principles of socialist construction, fails to recognize the true nature and extent of its degeneration. But this has always been the case throughout history: those who degenerate never see their own degeneration or greatly downplay it. Only the opposing side has always seen it. Although experiencing a profound crisis and finding itself at the top, seriously engulfed in the process of degeneration, the party, however, is undoubtedly healthy in its entirety. Only the source of the crisis and the process of degeneration that has begun must be addressed for the entire party to get back on its feet.

The task is for the entire party mass to unite, organize itself, understand the current situation, set itself a clear political goal, put an end to the dictatorship of Stalin and his clique, get back on the path of correct, Leninist theory and politics, and thereby ensure the victory of communism.

This is precisely the primary duty of every honest Bolshevik today. This is precisely what the thoughts of all the best elements of the party and the working class must be directed toward.

### III. The Crisis of the Soviets and the Drive Belts of the Proletarian Dictatorship

The crisis of the party is in itself a crisis of the proletarian dictatorship, for the party is the soul of the proletarian dictatorship, its guiding force. Without the Leninist Communist Party, a proletarian dictatorship is unthinkable. The experience of all revolutionary struggles of the working class in all countries has fully confirmed this conclusion.

The depth of the crisis of the proletarian dictatorship is already determined to a large extent by the depth of the crisis of the party. But the crisis of the proletarian dictatorship is currently expressed not only in the crisis of the party, but also in the crisis of Soviet power as a form of proletarian dictatorship, as well as in the crisis of all its transmission belts: the Komsomol, trade unions, cooperatives, voluntary societies, the press, and so on.

The crisis of Soviet power, in the narrow sense of the word, is expressed above all in the crisis of Soviet democracy. Soviet democracy has been supplanted and replaced by Stalin's one-man dictatorship. The Soviets, once organs expressing the will, sentiments, and genuine desires of the broadest party and non-party working masses and the poor and middle peasantry, where they could criticize not only the cook, the switchman, the foreman, or the factory director, but also the fundamental measures of the Party and government (criticism, of course, remaining within the framework of recognition of the leading role of the Party and the Soviet Constitution), have been transformed into organs for suppressing the will of these masses, organs of violence and terror against the masses, organs expressing the will of the same insignificant handful of party apparatus and clique of leaders, but not of the broad working masses. Previously, the Soviets were where non-party members verified the correctness of the Party's policies and its leadership of the masses and the country. Now the Soviets have been transformed into simple, powerless and helpless appendages of the party apparatus, where one is only allowed to admire Stalin’s six conditions and accept solemn greetings to the “dear leader,” despite the greatest hatred of the workers and peasants towards him.

The election of the Soviets has also been replaced, to a vast, overwhelming degree, by appointment. The Soviets are now filled with people who listen not to the voice of the masses, but only to the voice of their superiors; they look up, not down; they are ready, at the behest of their superiors, to commit any arbitrary and violent act against the masses, hiding behind the name and will of these masses. The situation is not altered in the least by the fact that a large number of Party and non-Party workers are currently working in the Soviets, in sections and elsewhere. A centralized, extraordinarily ramified apparatus, under threat, pressure, terror, and so on, forces these workers and peasants to act as soulless cogs in a vast machine, oppressing and terrorizing others against their will.

The endless deterioration of the situation of the workers and peasant masses, the policy of terror towards them, has gigantically undermined their trust in the proletarian dictatorship and its main organ - the Soviets.

The Soviet apparatus, for the most part, is operating completely idle; all decrees and measures of the Party and Soviet authorities are the subject of continuous mockery and ridicule on the part of the Soviet apparatus, because the absurdity, adventurism, deception of the masses, and the Khlestakov-like nature of Stalin's "general line" are especially striking to the Soviet apparatus, which is forced to implement this line.

Trade unions are also experiencing a crisis. This crisis manifests itself in the fact that they have transformed from a school of communism into a school of deceiving the masses, a school of the most shameless disregard for the will and sentiments of the masses, a mere appendage of the same party and economic apparatus.

According to Lenin, the task of trade unions should be, firstly, to educate the working masses in a spirit of conscious commitment to socialist production and, secondly, to protect their interests from the bureaucratic distortions of a workers' state. Currently, trade unions not only fail to protect workers' interests but, on the contrary, are an obedient tool in Stalin's hands to reduce real wages and their material standard of living.

The cultivation of a conscious attitude toward socialist production among workers has also been replaced by the slanderous vilification of backward workers as kulaks, saboteurs, agents of the class enemy, and so on. While Trotsky once demanded the nationalization of trade unions, something Lenin resolutely opposed, this nationalization has now been implemented, but in the crudest, most caricatured, bureaucratic form. Trade unions, in turn, have lost almost all authority and trust in the eyes of the workers.

Cooperatives also suffer from the same general malady of the Soviet Union: a large part of the cooperative network is deficient. Under current pricing policies and the lack of goods, any kind of cultured trade is out of the question. There are essentially no elections to cooperative bodies. For the masses, cooperatives are now merely collectors of differential shares, i.e., disguised taxes.

The Komsomol, in a mirrored and modified form, is experiencing roughly the same phenomena as the Party. These phenomena essentially boil down to the following: a break with Leninism; the transformation of dialectics into sophistry; the transformation of the apparatus into a self-sufficient force, alien to the masses; the transformation of the struggle against opportunism into a caricature; a crisis of worldview; disillusionment with the Party and socialist construction among the thinking section of the Komsomol; unprincipled politicking, careerism, and hypocrisy, as a product of apparatus terror and the Party's incorrect, anti-Leninist policies.

The Red Army and the GPU, organs previously used exclusively to suppress the resistance of exploiters and to combat the enemies of the proletarian dictatorship, are increasingly being used each year to suppress the discontent of workers and the poor and middle peasant masses in the countryside. (Mass arrests of party members and non-party workers, executions, and the merciless suppression of spontaneous uprisings by the poor and middle peasant masses in the countryside, driven to despair and starvation by Stalin's policies.)

The press, from being a tool for educating the masses, from a powerful lever that facilitated socialist construction and its observation from all sides, has been transformed into a gigantic factory of lies and the introduction of confusion and demoralization into the consciousness of the masses.

Stalin currently uses newspapers not to inform the masses of the true state of affairs in the Soviet Union, but precisely to keep them ignorant; not to reveal the truth, but to mislead. Individual facts, arbitrarily extracted from the general context or even fabricated, falsely combined reports, the exaggeration of certain phenomena and events while hushing up others that are ten times more important—this is the content and work of our newspapers. Most newspaper workers cynically scoff at their own fabrication of lies, but even this small cog, despite its awareness, is forced to "turn" along with the rest of the machine at the push of a button from Stalin's office. It goes without saying that all the processes we've listed are still in progress. In every organization, in every phenomenon, the old is strangely intertwined with the new, the sick with the healthy, the proletarian dictatorship with its denial, pieces, fragments of Leninism with its falsification and distortion, but the main direction of the evolution of the proletarian dictatorship under Stalin's leadership is exactly as we have described it.

The most vicious enemy of the party and the proletarian dictatorship, the most vicious counter-revolutionary and provocateur could not have done a better job of destroying the party and building socialism than Stalin did.

Leninism and the proletarian revolution cannot be permanently killed at the hands of the enemy—every time after defeat, they rise with renewed, tenfold strength. Stalin is killing Leninism under the banner of Leninism, the proletarian revolution under the banner of the proletarian revolution, and socialist construction under the banner of socialist construction!

Stalin is objectively fulfilling the role of traitor to the socialist revolution. But this would be the most horrific assassination history has ever seen! It would set back the entire course of history by decades.

Everything that is best, honest, truly Bolshevik, Leninist in the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and the Comintern must at all costs prevent this before the time is lost.

### IV. Ways out of the crisis and the tasks of honest, consistent Leninists

The Party, in its vast majority, is resolutely opposed to the policies of Stalin and his clique.

Even more unanimously opposed to this policy are workers and employees.

As for the village, this course has absolutely no supporters, not even people who are neutral towards it.

The entire village is driven to despair and seething with indignation. The ongoing mass uprisings in the village are the best indicator of its political mood.

The Red Army also largely reflects the political sentiments of the proletariat and peasant masses. Even the party apparatus, for the most part, is hypocritical and deeply disbelieves in the successful outcome of Stalin's adventure. Stalin and his clique, therefore, are not sustained by the trust, sympathy, and support of the masses, but by some other foundation, by some other means. What are these means?

A regime of unprecedented terror and colossal espionage, carried out through an extraordinarily centralized yet vastly ramified apparatus that concentrated all the country's material resources in its hands and made the physical existence of tens of millions of people directly dependent on itself—this is the fundamental foundation of Stalin's dictatorship. The entire system of the state apparatus, including the Party, terrorizing others while simultaneously living under the constant sword of Damocles, is, contrary to the consciousness of every single cell, forced, like a machine, to perform its movements, derived from the primary source, and carry out the will of the chief "mechanic."

But, having reached a dead end and established a reign of terror in a variety of forms throughout the country, Stalin cut off all avenues of retreat and an evolutionary solution to the crisis. He has elevated himself to the pedestal of an infallible pope and cannot admit not only the criminality of his policies, but even the slightest mistake. A dictator cannot make mistakes—only his subordinates do. The removal of Stalin and his clique by normal democratic means, guaranteed by the Party Charter and the Soviet Constitution, is therefore completely out of the question.

They will defend their dominance against the party and the country using all sorts of pretexts, with weapons in their hands, cannons and machine guns.

It would be unforgivably childish to delude oneself into believing that this clique, which through deception and slander usurped the rights of the Party and the working class, could voluntarily surrender them. This is all the more impossible since Stalin understands perfectly well that the Party and the working class cannot forgive him for his horrific crimes against the proletarian revolution and socialism. In this state of affairs, the Party is left with two choices: either continue to meekly endure the mockery of Leninism and the terror, and calmly await the final demise of the proletarian dictatorship, or remove this clique by force and save the cause of communism.

Is such a formulation of the question permissible from the standpoint of Marxism-Leninism? Not only is it permissible, but it is indisputably correct. It is criminal, harmful, and disastrous for the proletariat and its party to forcibly remove their leading party and soviet bodies if they pursue correct, Leninist policies and express the will of the party and non-party masses. Conversely, it should be considered the direct duty of every honest Bolshevik and non-party worker to fight for the forcible removal of these bodies when they pursue anti-Leninist policies that are disastrous for the proletarian dictatorship, when they have become a clique, fail to express the will of the masses, and at the same time, relying on the apparatus, prevent their replacement by the normal methods provided for in the Party Charter and the Soviet Constitution.

The experience of the proletarian revolution has shown us something completely unforeseen and unexpected. To turn away from these new facts, to fail to see them, is to become like ostriches and try to bury our heads in the sand. We have grown accustomed to the notion that under a proletarian dictatorship, the leadership of the party and the country will always express the will of the masses. In reality, however, this leadership has degenerated during the course of intra-party struggle into a personal dictatorship, destroying Soviet power and the party, hated by the masses, relying primarily on terror and provocation. This is a new, unique, and completely unexpected phenomenon for the party. Painful and difficult as it may be, this must be acknowledged by all who do not want to remain captive to illusions and face the fact of the complete collapse of the proletarian revolution.

People who lack Marxist thinking believe that Stalin's removal would also be the overthrow of Soviet power. Stalin cultivates and disseminates this view in every possible way. But it is absolutely wrong.

In reality, we are not talking about the destruction of the proletarian dictatorship, but about its restoration, for while it remains in form, its content has been largely lost. We are not talking about violating the principles of Leninism, but rather about defending them. Just as the removal of one bourgeois government and its replacement by another does not yet signify the overthrow of bourgeois rule, although it may ultimately entail such a state of affairs, for every political upheaval unleashes the forces of classes hostile to a given political system, so too the removal of one "proletarian" government and its replacement by another proletarian government does not yet signify the overthrow of the proletarian dictatorship, although such a struggle is fraught with grave dangers for the proletarian dictatorship itself.

At the present time, in the era of the world proletarian revolution, in the era of the open struggle for power between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat, every political revolution—the overthrow of one government and its replacement by another in order to preserve the rule of a given class—is associated with the danger of the overthrow of a class that is the antipode of the entire system of economic and political rule of the class.

The bourgeoisie and the proletariat are constantly watching for each other in order to overthrow the enemy’s rule at the moment of the most intense struggle in the ranks of the enemy.

The danger of a "third force" currently exists not only for the Soviet Union but for every capitalist country, albeit to varying degrees. For us, the "third force" is the force of global capitalism and the Soviet government's internal enemies, who dream of restoring capitalism. For the bourgeoisie, the "third force" is the force of the revolutionary proletariat of their own country and the force of the entire global revolutionary proletariat, led by the Soviet Union.

However, the danger of a given class losing its economic and political dominance in any given period depends not only on the strength of the enemy class's attack. It also depends to a huge, and sometimes decisive, degree on how the executive organs of the ruling class—that is, the government—implement its will, its class interests, and its class policy. Moreover, even the strength of the enemy class's attack depends to a large extent on the tactics of the ruling class government, its ability to maneuver, its flexibility, its ability to soberly assess the political and economic situation, and so on.

The defeat of the bourgeoisie and the victory of the proletariat are ultimately inevitable. But history is not made automatically. We are not fatalists. It is made by living people! The strategy and tactics of the proletariat and its parties, on the one hand, and the strategy and tactics of the bourgeoisie, on the other, are crucial components of the historical timeframe within which the overthrow of the bourgeoisie and the construction of communist society throughout the world can be achieved.

With the right strategy and tactics on our part, and mistakes on the part of the bourgeoisie, these periods could be shorter, and victory could be achieved with fewer casualties; with our mistakes and sober tactics on the part of the bourgeoisie, these periods could be extended by decades, and the struggle would require greater sacrifices.

The foolish, adventurist, or opportunistic policies of a proletarian government can destroy even the proletariat's true cause and set history back 30–40 years. The clever, cunning policies of the bourgeoisie can save even the "lost" cause of capitalism from final collapse over a long period.

This is precisely why, even in the present period, when capitalism is experiencing a general crisis and the proletarian revolution is knocking at all the doors of capitalist society, the bourgeoisie still allows the violent overthrow of its governments when it sees that they are pursuing a policy that is dangerous from the point of view of its dominance and at the same time do not want to voluntarily give way to others.

Indeed, over the past two decades, we have experienced three such upheavals: Spain, Poland, and China. All of these upheavals, as is well known, were fraught with enormous dangers for the bourgeoisie. All of them, as is well known, led to mass revolutionary movements and uprisings of workers and peasants. In some of these countries, things almost came to the overthrow of the new bourgeois governments and the establishment of the dictatorship of the proletariat. And yet, the bourgeoisie, its most class-conscious segment, took this risk.

Can these coups be justified from the standpoint of the current "historical" interests of the bourgeoisie? Of course they can. The policies of the overthrown governments, foolish from the standpoint of bourgeois interests, could have quickly led the state to a proletarian revolution. The most conscious elements of the ruling class accepted the risk of a coup. And even if only temporarily—for for a class historically doomed, such a move is impossible over the long term—they nevertheless succeeded in strengthening their state. For us, the removal of Stalin's dictatorship and his clique also carries a risk. We are unleashing the forces of the internal enemies of the proletarian revolution and international capital. This danger is great.

But for us, even now, there exists an even more serious danger of the collapse of the proletarian dictatorship at the hands of Stalin himself and his clique. Stalin has already destroyed the proletarian dictatorship and socialist construction, in their true essence, to a large extent.

The proletarian dictatorship is already dying, and it must be saved. Stalin and his clique will certainly destroy the proletarian dictatorship completely, but by eliminating Stalin, we have a good chance of saving it.

The party finds itself in the position of passengers in a car whose driver has suddenly gone hopelessly insane, veered off the road, and is driving the passengers over bumps and potholes, downhill at full speed, straight into an abyss. The car creaks, squeals, breaks down, tumbles from side to side, bounces over bumps so hard that the passengers' teeth chatter. The passengers are indignant, many are confused, some panic, some are thrown out of the car by the jolts, while the deranged driver berates the passengers for being opportunists and "stealers," and reassures them that these are all the inevitable difficulties of driving. In such a situation, it is foolish and difficult for the passengers to wait for the "driver" to drive them off the road. They must try to throw the driver off the wheel while the car is moving, and while the car is moving, someone skilled in steering the car must take the wheel and steer it back onto the beaten track. The passengers have no other choice.

If we were to choose at the present time which danger is greater for us - the danger of the destruction of the party of the proletarian revolution and socialist construction at the hands of Stalin or from a “third force” as a result of an unsuccessful attempt to remove him from the dictatorship, then the first danger is undoubtedly greater, more real, more serious, and more inevitable.

It must not be forgotten that Stalin's leadership and policy are not only a denial of Leninism and the proletarian dictatorship, but they also directly organize and unite the forces of counterrevolution and at the same time demoralize, disorganize, and disrupt the forces of the party, the working class, and all workers.

The internal counterrevolution and the international bourgeoisie in the person of Stalin have, according to his objective role, the best ally.

Stalin's adventurist, "arch-leftist" policies (according to the well-known law of dialectics—"extremes meet") lead with absolute inevitability to the restoration of capitalism. And the longer this course continues, the longer Stalin remains at the helm of power, the more inexorably this restoration will approach.

No matter how monstrous and paradoxical it may seem at first glance, the main enemy of Leninism, the proletarian dictatorship and socialist construction is currently within our own ranks and even leads the party.

The fight to remove Stalin is fraught with risk. But no great deed, no historical event, has ever been accomplished without risk.

The first and foremost danger in the struggle to destroy Stalin's dictatorship is the possibility of an attack on us by the imperialists.

This is a serious danger. But the possibility of an attack does not necessarily mean it is inevitable. While some factors push the bourgeoisie to attack us, others will act in the opposite direction.

The contradictions within the main imperialist predators, our support from the global revolutionary proletariat, the colonial-revolutionary movement, and our own skillful maneuvering have saved us from imperialist attack to date, and they can protect us from it in the future. Ultimately, everything will depend on the specific political situation.

The second danger is internal counterrevolution.

Stalin has worked diligently to cultivate counterrevolutionary forces in recent years. Counterrevolutionary forces, dreaming of the restoration of capitalism, have grown tenfold in recent years. Masses of urban and rural workers loyal to Soviet power and Bolshevism have been driven into the counterrevolutionary camp by Stalin's criminal policies.

But fortunately for us, these elements are scattered, fragmented and disorganized.

If we prove sufficiently united and organized, if we gather sufficient forces around us and set clear goals for ourselves, then with bold, decisive actions we can take the initiative to smash all restorationist, counter-revolutionary attempts at the very outset and retain our main positions.

If the current Spanish government and Pilsudski managed to successfully suppress all revolutionary actions of the workers during the coups, then why can’t we suppress all counter-revolutionary actions?

The task is to immediately begin mobilizing and uniting party forces on the basis of Marxism-Leninism, on the basis of preparations for the destruction of Stalin's dictatorship. The overwhelming majority of the Party and the working class are against Stalin and his clique. It is only necessary to unite these scattered and terrorized forces, instill faith in this cause, and begin working to eliminate Stalin's leadership.
