# Menshevik attempt to overthrow Bolshevism: Ep.4 – “Komuch”, Volga & Siberia, Czechoslovak mutiny

**THE CZECHOSLOVAK MUTINY**

The plan of the Entente was to land invasion troops into Arkhangelsk and join the White Guards who were waging revolts in the regions surrounding Moscow. Thus Moscow would be encircled and blockaded. However, a crucial part of this plan was the Czechoslovak legion in Soviet Russia. The Entente knew that the White Guard rebellions in Yaroslavl, Kostroma and elsewhere, didn’t stand a chance unless they received help from imperialist troops.

There were Czechoslovak troops inside Russia which wanted to be transported through Siberia to Vladivostok, and then eventually on a ship to the front to fight against the Central Powers. The Bolsheviks had given them permission to travel to Vladivostok and then leave. However, the French officers who led these Czechoslovaks, provoked a battle between them and the Bolshevik government.

The imperialists deceived the Czechoslovaks by saying the Bolsheviks were German agents trying to arrest them and hand them to the Germans or Austrians. This is how they first got them to rebel against the Soviets (Litvinov, The Bolshevik revolution: its rise and meaning, p. 44). This lie was even repeated by the right-SRs in a book they published in 1930 (Grazhdanskaia voina na Volge v 1918 g).

Anti-communist writer Vera Broido, whose parents were Menshevik party members, continued to claim in her book from 1987 that the Bolsheviks wanted to hand the Czechoslovaks to the Germans under German orders (Lenin and the Mensheviks: The Persecution of Socialists under Bolshevism, p. 40).

The British had already tried to create a similar puppet army from the Finnish emigrants, but that project had failed.

The capitalist editor of the 70s ed. of Serge’s Year one of the Russian revolution discusses with official documents the various Anglo-French plans to invade Russia and use Savinkov, the Czechoslovaks and others. (Editorial Postscript).

In May 1918 the Czechoslovak troops traveling on the Trans-Siberian railway began attacking Bolshevik forces, overthrowing the Bolsheviks in numerous towns that they passed through, and helping the Whites, Right-SRs and Mensheviks setup reactionary governments. However, in the end, the imperialist forces still did not reach Yaroslavl, Kostroma, Rybinsk or the other White Guard rebellions in time to provide assistance to them.

***Czechoslovak legion***

The imperialists tried to fuel ethnic hatred between the Czechoslovaks and other nations. There was an armed fight between them and Hungarian prisoners of war. However, there were also many Czechoslovak communists and others who did not want to be involved in this criminal venture. That is why the French officers who controlled the Czechoslovaks, had to rely on deception and lie to the soldiers.

“The French Military Mission had laid its plans well. Just before the rising it had paid out, through the French Consul in Moscow, 11 million roubles to the Czecho-Slovak National Council, and the British Consul in Moscow had paid out £80,000. The money was paid to members of the National Council, M. Shiep and M. Bogumiltchermak, and through their hands it went to the commanders of the Czecho-Slovak detachments in various parts of Russia… The minds of the Czecho-Slovak soldiers had been carefully prepared by propaganda from the French Military Mission to the effect that the Soviet Government had refused to allow them to return to France, and was about to deliver them over to the Austrian military authorities, who would hang them all as deserters. Like hunted animals at bay, the volunteers struck, and it was not an accident that the places where they happened to strike were along the lines of the Volga and the Siberian railway, thus cutting off Central Russia from its last corn reserve. It only required an Allied or anti-Soviet force on the shores of the White Sea to almost complete the ring running from the far North to the North Caucasus and the mouth of the Don” (M. Philips Price, My reminiscences of the Russian revolution, pp. 292-293)

“Documents which fell into the hands of the Soviet authorities when the Czecho-Slovak troops were evacuating Siberian towns in the following year prove that the leaders of these forces were heavily bribed by the representatives of Britain and France to act as they did.” (W. P. & Zelda K. Coates, Armed Intervention in Russia: 1918-1922, p. 111)

According to an American writer who interviewed a Russian sailor “there are about two thousand Czecho-Slovaks in jail, in Siberia for refusing to fight the Bolsheviks.” (News from Russia, The Liberator, Issue No. 14, April, 1919) Eventually the Czechoslovaks would end up mutineeing against their imperialist masters.

“The Czech Legion was unwilling to fight in Russia indefinitely. Its soldiers were tired and wanted to go home, and their morale declined further as the Reds became better organized. By the middle of August, the Czech units were falling apart. Some of the soldiers were socialists and they went over to the Reds.” (Orlando Figes, A people’s tragedy : the Russian Revolution 1891-1924, London : Pimlico, 1997, p. 581)

“The majority of the rank-and-file Czechs were alienated by the out-and-out monarchism of the military commanders in Samara and Ufa, which spread down into the officer corps of the People’s Army [White army of the komuch government]. Many began to doubt the support of the Entente powers for the Czech national cause; they questioned the whole purpose of their struggle against the Bolsheviks. The legion began to disintegrate. Some deserted to the Red Army. Others became more interested in selling the legion’s vast quantities of military provisions to the surrounding population than in fighting the enemy.” (Figes, Peasant Russia, civil war : the Volga countryside in revolution, 1917-1921, p. 173)

After White General Kolchak came to power in Siberia “The rank and file of the Czecho-Slovak legions now understood for what purposes they had been used. Many of them had from the first unwillingly taken part in the adventure and had been largely forced by their influential commander Colonel Gaida. The latter had acted all through as their evil genius, and now that he was losing the confidence of his rank and file, he openly became one of Koltchak’s generals. From this time forth the Czecho-Slovak troops took no part in the operations against the Red Army, and remained sullenly in the rear along the Siberian Railway, waiting for the first opportunity to return to Europe.” (Price, p. 355)

According to Right-SR writers, 18 “most prominent socialists” among the Czechoslovaks, by which they mean Czechoslovak social-traitors, opportunists, published the following statement in order to fool their troops into supporting the Whites:

“Our mission is democratic. . . We, as socialists, assure you that our army will not and cannot be used for the purpose of counter-revolution… Rest assured that we fully sympathize with the following slogans you [the Komuch, SR-Menshevik White government] put forward… protection of civil liberties, security labor and protection of trade unions, transfer of all lands to the people, preventing military dictatorship.” (Grazhdanskaia voina na Volge v 1918 g, pp. 145-146)

But despite these lies, which were intended to cover up the true character of the White Guards, the truth was soon revealed to the Czechoslovak troops, because the Komuch began to take the land away from the peasants, began to crush workers organizations, carried out massacres and violations of civil rights, and the main parties of the Komuch itself, the Cadets and SRs, began supporting military dictatorship until finally a coup was carried out by monarchist White General Kolchak, who had been working in the Komuch as a minister. In the foreign press the SRs and Cadets continued to lie and claim that White Generals Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich etc. were great democrats, but nowadays we know those were only lies.

“The Czecho-Slovak Commander-in-Chief, General Syrovoy had no delusions as to what the [Kolchak] coup d’etat in essence meant; he publicly declared:

“The change of government has killed our soldiers. They say that for years they have been fighting for democracy and now that a dictator rules at Omsk they are no longer fighting for a democracy. Since the armistice all the soldiers want to go home to fight the Germans and Magyars in their own country rather than fight the Russians.”” (W. P. & Zelda K. Coates, Armed Intervention in Russia: 1918-1922, p. 124)

The Czechoslovaks were told they’d receive transportation away from Russia, but once they had started their revolt against the Soviets, the British and French did not give them the transports, making all kinds of excuses. Then the Entente began telling Czechoslovak leaders that the Entente will not recognize Czechoslovakia’s independence unless they join in the anti-Soviet war.

As a result the Czechoslovak National Council in New York published the following official and dishonest declaration:

“The question, however, of staying in Russia, or getting out does not depend on the Czecho-Slovaks alone. That is something which must be decided by the Allies. The Czecho-Slovak Army is one of the Allied Armies, and it is as much under the orders of the Versailles War Council as the French or American Army. No doubt the Czecho-Slovak boys in Russia are anxious to avoid participation in a possible civil war in Russia, but they realise at the same time that by staying where they are they may be able to render far greater services, both to Russia and the allied cause, than if they were transported to France. They are at the orders of the Supreme War Council of the Allies.” (W. P. & Zelda K. Coates, Armed Intervention in Russia: 1918-1922, p. 112)

**THE “KOMUCH” AND OTHER WHITE GOVERNMENTS ON THE VOLGA, THE DON AND IN SIBERIA**

With the support of the Czechoslovak troops and the British, French and Japanese invasions Mensheviks and Right-SRs also created their own regimes in some places in Siberia.

“The SR Constituent Assembly Committee assumed power in the towns of the Volga following in the train of the Czechoslovaks. Each town became, as it was captured, the scene of a protracted massacre of Communists and suspects.” (V. Serge, Year one of the Russian Revolution, Chicago, Holt, Rinehart, and Winston, 1972, p. 280)

“The chief political beneficiary of the Czechoslovak involvement undoubtedly was the SR party (specifically its Siberian regional affiliates)… In Novonikolaevsk, four Tomsk SRs declared themselves the West Siberian Commissariat of the Provisional Siberian Government, which had been created by the SRD at its January meeting. On 8-9 May, the Czechs also participated in the establishment of the… SR Committee of Members of the Constituent Assembly (Komuch) in Samara…

The Czechs’ role in these developments was… pivotal… without their help the Siberian and Ural anti-Bolshevik forces stood little chance of success.” (Norman G. O. Pereira, The “democratic counterrevolution” of 1918 in Siberia, p. 75)

The Menshevik-SR bourgeois-democratic White-Guard government in Siberia suffered from many weaknesses, but “most importantly, [from] insufficient popular support.” (Pereira, p. 73). Again, the supposed great popularity of the Right-SRs and especially the Mensheviks, which Brovkin talks about, is completely missing!

The Right-SRs were quite conscious that they didn’t have any popularity and needed foreign armed forces:

“‘Unless’, declared Klimushkin, one of the SR leaders in Samara, at the start of May, ‘there is a spur from the outside in the near future, we can give up all hopes of a coup d’état’” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 576)

Right-SR members of the Komuch government later wrote: “The coup we carried out was not supported by the workers” (Grazhdanskaia voina na Volge v 1918 g, p. 48)

Under the Komuch “the Soviets, as class organs, [were] barred from politics… The workers rejected the Komuch as ‘bourgeois’ and passed defiantly Bolshevik resolutions in the Soviet. The factory committees were stripped of their powers and control of the factories was transferred to their former owners… The banks were returned to private control. Free trade was restored and a Council of Trade and Industry, dominated by industrialists, was set up to help formulate economic policy.” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 579)

With Czechoslovak troops a Right-SR Provisional Siberian Government was created in Tomsk and a capitalist government, the so-called “Business cabinet” in Kharbin led by General Khorvat.

“Without any mass constituency, both Derber and Khorvat were obliged to seek legitimation from foreigners—the former in the West, the latter in Japan” (Pereira, p. 76)

The Japanese supported another White General in Siberia, Ataman Semenov. They provided him with weapons and 3 million rubles (V. Panferov, The intervention in Siberia 1918-1922, p. 10). None of the White Guard regimes had any native support, they relied exclusively on foreign imperialist backing.

“An archetypical scene in this eastern Siberian comedy occurred when the unsavory Ataman Semenov (with his multinational crew of cutthroats who had been terrorizing Trans-Baikal) traveled to the United States to seek American approbation. Even Semenov understood that “to get the support of the Allies—which was as necessary for [any] White government as air—required masquerading as a civil democratic regime.”” (Pereira, p. 76)

“In the meantime another tool of the Japanese was organizing a White-Guard uprising in the city of Blagoveshchensk. This was “Ataman” Gamov, He was assisted by the Mensheviks, Socialist-Revolutionaries and the Japanese spies” (Panferov, p. 11)

“The old municipal institutions were born again, and the bourgeois newspapers reappeared with their [ridiculous lies] announcements of the flight of Trotsky, the Allies’ irresistible intervention, the atrocities of the Chinese, Letts and Germans who made up the Red Army. The Metropolitan Bishop of Kazan summoned the faithful for the defence of the Church.” (V. Serge, Year one, pp. 280-281)

“Komuch propagandists made claims that SRs themselves no doubt knew to be mendacious, as when Komuch’s chief propaganda agency—the Department of Agitation, Culture, and Propaganda— claimed that Germans made up half of the advancing Red Army” (Scott Smith, Captives of revolution : the socialist revolutionaries and the Bolshevik dictatorship, 1918-1923, p. 91)

The SR government in Tomsk reversed nationalization and stripped Soviets of power, while another Provisional Siberian government in Omsk led by Vologodsky “abolished the Soviets, immediately reintroduced free trade, denationalized industry while restricting the activity of trade unions, and returned private estates to their original proprietors… In the name of law and order, Cossak punitive expeditions were used to crush peasant and worker organizations, especially the Bolshevik-dominated trade unions.” (Pereira, p. 76)

The Komuch didn’t fair any better. As a result of the policy of free trade “The peasants reduced their food sales to the cities, as money lost its value, forcing the Komuch to introduce bread rationing. Its urban base collapsed even further.” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 579) The Komuch soon started carrying out forced requisitioning of food from the peasants, in other words it experienced all the same problems as the Bolsheviks did. The Komuch liberals had always claimed that food problems were caused by Bolshevik policies, and that free trade would solve everything.

The whole point of the SR-Menshevik ‘Komuch’ government was to restore the capitalist parliament, the constituent assembly, but the masses were not interested:

“Through the village and volost Soviets the peasants were already carrying out their own revolution on the land, and they did not need the sanction of a decree by the Constituent Assembly… to complete this. The Right SRs could not understand this fundamental fact: that the autonomy of the peasants through their village Soviets had, from their point of view, reduced the significance of any national parliament… To be sure, out of habit, or deference to their village elders, the mass of the peasants would cast their votes for the SRs in the elections to the Constituent Assembly. But very few were prepared to fight the SR battle for its restoration, as the dismal failure of the Komuch would prove in the summer of 1918. Virtually all the resolutions from the villages on this question made it clear that they did not want the Assembly to be restored as the ‘political master of the Russian land’… As Sokolov later acknowledged from his experience as an SR propagandist in the army:

“The Constituent Assembly was something totally unknown and unclear to the mass of the front-line soldiers, it was without doubt a terra incognita. Their sympathies were clearly with the Soviets. These were the institutions that were near and dear to them, reminding them of their own village assemblies … more than once had occasion to hear the soldiers, sometimes even the most intelligent of them, object to the Constituent Assembly. To most of them it was associated with the State Duma, an institution that was remote to them. ‘What do we need some Constituent Assembly for, when we already have our Soviets”” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 519)

“Despite the SRs’ expectations, the Volga peasantry proved no more supportive of their government. Had the SRs been willing to support the peasant revolution, things might have been different. But that would have meant recognizing the peasant Soviets and the Komuch leaders were not prepared to go that far…

The Komuch was equally reluctant to sanction the peasants’ seizures of the gentry’s land… a subsequent decree, passed on 22 July, enabled the former landowners to reclaim any winter fields which they had sown. This in effect meant reversing one-third of the peasant requisitions of arable land. Troops often had to be called in to enforce the decree… the local squires saw the decree as a licence to take the law into their own hands. With the help of an army brigade, or their own private militia, they would seize back their property; sometimes they even had the peasant leaders flogged in public to ‘teach them a lesson’” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 580)

“Outwardly the [Omsk] PSG remained an amalgam of moderate socialists, nonparty regionalists and liberal nationalists, but by the summer of 1918 its most important figures—Mikhailov, Guins, and Vologodskii—had become de facto Kadets.” i.e. liberal-monarchist capitalists (Pereira, p. 76).

On the other hand, having lost all popular support “”the Kadets grew disenchanted with liberal democracy… One option was to renounce its liberalism… Another was to find an alternative constituency as surrogate for the black (that is, uninformed) masses. The Kadet leadership, after some divisive flirtation with a third—German gambit— merged the first two”” (Pereira, p. 77)

“the Right SRs welcomed the Kadet incursion. After the fiasco of the Constituent Assembly, they were increasingly prepared to sacrifice parliamentary niceties—and party identity—in order to regain the power which they had lost to the Bolsheviks, even at the risk of civil war.” (Pereira, p. 78)

A local person stated that in Samara during the counter-revolution “When the Czechs had taken our town there was a “Te Deum” in the principal church, and thanks were offered up to God because the country had been rid of a vile enemy, which enemy, the people said, they had elected to govern them. It is true that there was not perfect freedom under the Bolsheviks, but in the welter of ideas in which we all lived the restraints were not very obvious, and, though there was a censorship of the telegraph and to some extent of the press up to the time of the Czech outbreak, we were conscious more of the influence of public opinion than of political pressure put upon us by any one party.

Under the new régime instituted behind the screen of the Czech forces we were conscious of the pressure of one political party, and we noted the suppression of the influence of public opinion. In the town of Buzuluk we saw the Cossacks driving through the streets with a cartload of headless bodies, the peasants remarking, ‘Those bodies were our sons; they joined the Red Guard to defend the Revolution.’ The next day, while the Cossacks were patrolling the town, an “election” was held on a limited franchise, and candidates for office were required 10 have the signature on their papers of certain persons who had held office under the old régime. A Council was thus elected which bore a close resemblance to that which had existed in 1916, the comment of people on the street being, “This is too reactionary for us.”” (John Rickman, An Eye-Wittness From Russia, p. 19)

Brovkin claims the supposedly socialist SR-Menshevik government of Komuch represented true democracy and even created its own armed force which posed a serious threat to the Bolsheviks.

However, in reality “the Komuch… could not count on its own “People’s Army;” many of the latter’s officers openly preferred the political system and military organization of the more authoritarian Omsk regime.” (Pereira, p. 80)

Komuch first called on the people to volunteer for the army, but since very few people volunteered, they needed to implement conscription.

“Of all the Komuch’s policies, none was more unpopular than the call-up for the People’s Army. In any civil war the success of the contenders depends on their relative abilities to mobilize the local population. This test the Komuch failed in no uncertain fashion.” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 580)

“Colonel Galkin, a typical military bureaucrat of the tsarist era, was placed in charge of the People’s Army. His headquarters became a stronghold of Rightist and monarchist officers… Many of them were the sons of local squires and sometimes wreaked a violent revenge on the villages that had seized their families’ estates. No wonder the peasants were not keen on the so-called People’s Army.” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 582)

“the [Komuch] army was organized according to the old principles, on the same basis as the pre-revolutionary tsarist army… Galkin, manager of the military department… did absolutely nothing in this regard to democratize the officer corps called upon to organize the People’s Army” (Grazhdanskaia voina na Volge v 1918 g, pp. 75-76)

“From the very first day mutual disagreement began between the officers and the soldiers, misunderstanding and dissatisfaction; soldiers, afraid of everything old, did not trust the officers, and the officers did not trust the soldiers” (Grazhdanskaia voina na Volge v 1918 g, p. 87)

People didn’t want to join the Komuch army and tried to escape from it. In response the Komuch used tactics similar to the tsarist counter-revolution of 1906:

“The Komuch tried to stem the desertions by sending punitive Cossack detachments into the villages. Military field courts, reminiscent of Stolypin’s notorious tribunals in 1905-6, were given sweeping powers to punish the deserters and their families. Peasant leaders were publicly flogged and hanged; hostages were taken to force the deserters out of hiding; and whole villages were burned to the ground when soldiers failed to give themselves up. To the peasants, all this must have seemed like a return of the old regime with a vengeance.

The effect of repression was merely to strengthen the peasantry’s resistance and drive many of them into the arms of the Reds. Villagers formed brigades, often organized by the Soviet, in order to resist the People’s Army and its punitive detachments. These village ‘armies’ went to war with rusty guns, pitchforks and axes, and odd pieces of artillery mounted on peasant carts. Some fought as partisan units alongside the Red Army and later became regular detachments of it. The Domashki village brigade was a classic example. It fought against the Cossacks on the southern steppelands of Samara before becoming the nucleus of the 219th Domashki Rifle Division, a regular detachment of the Fourth Red Army. The Pugachey, Novouzensk, Krasnokutsk and Kurilovo Regiments had similar origins.” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 583)

The komuch “failed to arouse any popular enthusiasm, failed to win over the workers… and ignored the peasants.” (Donald J. Raleigh, A Russian civil war diary: Alexis Babine in Saratov, 1917-1922, footnote on p. 88)

“In the towns the new government [Komuch] encountered opposition from the factory workers; it was unable to maintain control without repressions and arrests; and it was forced to abuse the very political liberties which it had proclaimed on coming to power. In the country areas the government was unable, from the outset, to extend its influence by democratic means. Because of the chronic shortage of reliable rural officials, the higher authorities were forced to depend upon provincial and district plenipotentiaries, who were given special powers under martial law to enforce all government orders, to remove officials from office, to break up public meetings, and to arrest suspected Bolsheviks.” (Figes, Peasant Russia, p. 168)

“A good illustration of [the situation] is in the village of Pestravka (Nikolaevsk uezd), where the soviet had remained in power after the establishment of the Komuch. Two army officers who were sent in to condemn the soviet at a village gathering on 21 July were subjected by the peasants to a samosud and then brutally murdered. A complete brigade of the People’s Army later arrived from Samara to substitute a zemstvo for the soviet. After the departure of the brigade, however, the peasants disbanded the zemmstvo and elected a new soviet.” (Figes, Peasant Russia, p. 169)

“In the village of Natal’in (Buguruslan uezd) a Cossack detachment flogged not only the relatives of the deserters, but also the leaders of the peasant commune who had organized the seizure of the gentry estate— a brutal act of repression deliberately designed to ‘break the spirit’ of the defiant peasants, and highly reminiscent of the gentry reaction of 1906-7. In the village of Dmitrievka (in the Orenburg region) another Cossack detachment flogged all the men of the village as a punishment for the division of the gentry land; over a period of three weeks 100 peasants were given a total of nearly 4,000 lashes.” (Figes, Peasant Russia, p. 179)

“Peasants complained that the People’s Army made impossible carting demands and that they “got the whip” when they asked for documents legitimizing the army’s requests or objected in any way. Army units seized geese, ducks, and grain and did not pay for them or paid too low a price; they took horses and refused to provide receipts; and everywhere, they beat up village elders and other peasants when the villagers did not satisfy their demands.”

In some villages peasants resisted violently, attacking requisitioning detachments and the punitive detachments sent out in their wake.” At a peasants’ congress in Samara in September, many speakers reported on the “extraordinary situation” that had developed on the ground in connection with requisitioning. A sympathetic reporter summarized their stories: “arrests, executions, and all sorts of measures of retribution have become so common, that the peasant population is literally terrorized.”… the People’s Army… became almost universally unpopular in the Russian villages of Samara and Ufa provinces.” (Smith, p. 107)

“The peasants did not want to fight the Soviet troops. At military headquarters, Second Lieutenant Gorbachev, member of the propaganda bureau for recruiting soldiers into the “People’s Army” reported that the peasant residents of the village of Osorgin, Ovyatodukhovskoy volost, speaking at a meeting in favor of the policy of the Bolsheviks, declared: “All front-line soldiers are Bolsheviks, and we stand for Soviet power. Bolsheviks have done nothing bad to us, we don’t want any other power, this one is good for us. We voted for the Bolsheviks, and the entire front voted for them, and who elected these members of the Constituent Assembly, we do not know.” Second Lieutenant Gorbachev advised sending a detachment to Osorgino that would “take the new recruits, take away their weapons and restore the zemstvo”. But such detachments, against the advice of Second Lieutenant Gorbachev, traveled around the province and committed terrible atrocities.” (Е. И. Медведев, Гражданская война в Среднем Поволжье, pp. 127-128)

“peasants reacted “extremely hostilely” to the first visit by an agitator to inform them of the draft [by Komuch]. The peasants proclaimed in chorus that the Bolsheviks had not carried out a mobilization, and a mobilization now would only serve to help the “bourgeois” (burzhui) confiscate the land… Agitators reported that peasants in these volosti openly declared their support for Soviet power as the authorities tried in vain to push through the mobilization.” (Smith, p. 109)

Throughout the civil war the only reliable forces of the Whites (besides foreign armies) had been cossacks, a privileged soldier caste kept separated from the Russian people. That is why the first white armies were raised in cossack regions, the army of Krasnov on the Don. White Generals Kornilov and Semenov were also cossack chiefs. However, even among the cossacks, only the richest cossacks were firm supporters of the whites:

“The younger and war-weary Cossacks from the Front — influenced by the officers risen from their ranks — were more inclined to find some accord with Bolshevik Russia than to fight against it. Thus it was really only in the southern Don — where the Cossacks were more wealthy and more determined to defend their historic landed privileges against the demands of the Russian peasants for land reform — that the Cossacks were prepared to fight the Bolsheviks.” (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 562)

During the civil war there was “a wave of Cossack uprisings” against Bolshevism “starting in the villages near Novocherkassk. These had always been the richest in the Don” (Figes, p. 565) Again we can clearly see socio-economic factors, class relations, playing the most important role in determining people’s behavior, as working Cossacks turned against the whites and joined the Bolsheviks.

White general Alekseev wrote:

“I believed that as the Cossacks have their own property and their own territory, they will protect them and thus ensure the security of the Cossack military formation and provide time to create new military units [i.e. to create a White army], but I was wrong.

Cossack regiments returning from the front are in complete moral decay. The ideas of Bolshevism found adherents among the broad mass of Cossacks. They do not wish to fight, even to defend their own territory” from the Reds (Letter of general Alekseev sent from Rostov to Kiev. January 27, 1918. Quoted in Борьба за власть Советов на Дону 1917-1920 гг. p. 236)

SRs protested against some crimes by the white guards, but it was totally ignored. A good demonstration of how little support the SRs must’ve had, is that they never even considered relying on the people, but they considered their only options to challenge the monarchists was to either ask help from the Czech legion or Western imperialist forces, though obviously the imperialists didn’t listen to such arguments either but supported monarchists:

“SR protests seemed perfunctory, as if by then everybody understood that there was nothing to be accomplished by verbal denunciations of even the most gross violations of civil rights. Some thought was given to asking the Czechs for military assistance against Omsk, but that was dismissed as too divisive and risky. The one remaining hope was that the Allies would insist upon a reckoning or at least  
fair play for democratic forces.” (Pereira, p. 81)

“An authoritarian tone was originally set by the influential Omsk Bloc—made up of big merchants and industrialists, members of the liberal professions, civil servants, some military officers, and Kadets—which made clear its preference for a unipersonal military dictatorship. This was not a new idea in conservative circles; General Kornilov had enjoyed the support of many of the same people and interest groups for his coup attempt in September 1917.” (Pereira, p. 82)

In all these coalitions the Right-SRs and especially the Mensheviks, were always the smallest and weakest party, despite the fact that the capitalist Cadet Party, not to mention the Monarchists, also had extremely low popularity among the people, as was consistently shown in all elections. That says something about the low level of support for the Mensheviks.

“The Socialist Revolutionary Party lost control to the Kadets, while the latter traded liberalism for a neo-idealist, romantic, nationalist vision of state power infused with a cult of the army.” (Pereira, p. 83)

The capitalist liberal Kadet party, “the party of People’s Liberty, as it called itself, refused to condemn anti-Jewish pogroms on “liberated” territory. Russian liberals ended up in the company of the Black Hundreds.” (Brovkin, Behind the front lines, p. 411)

“What were the social forces on which the [Constituent Assembly] Committee relied? The Menshevik Maisky, a former member of this government of democratic counter-revolution, has drawn a precise portrait on this point. The hostility of the working class towards the Committee was so great that the latter’s attempt to create a docile ‘Soviet’ with Menshevik assistance collapsed miserably, with the ‘Soviet’ promptly passing a Bolshevik resolution. Mobilization was a dismal failure in the countryside.” (V. Serge, Year one, p. 281)

“Districts which had tried both the Bolshevik régime and that set up under the Czechs when free to do so reverted to Bolshevism. The Czechs were forced to retire from the Volga to the Urals because of uprisings among the people. The Soviet of Vladivostok was returned at the July election though the “Reds” were in prison. From the Urals to the eastern coast the people were discontented with the directorate and the dictatorship and preferred a return to the Soviets” (Rickman, p. 23)

Czechoslovaks occupied at least Chelyabinsk, Penza, Syzran, Novo-Nikolayevsk, Samara, Ufa, Kazan, Simbirsk, Verkhne-Uralsk, Zlatust, Ekaterinburg. According to Rickman, the people in all these places supported the Bolsheviks and voted for the Bolsheviks if elections were allowed to be held. The sources I’ve provided consistently note that the Mensheviks, SRs and White Guards had no popular support at all in Siberia.

The Samara Soviet passed a Bolshevik resolution calling for the arming of all workers, restoration of Soviet power and all Soviet laws. “Unnerved by the prospect of a Bolshevik majority in the soviet, Gendel’man editorialized somberly in the Samara SR organ Zemlia i volia: “There is no reason to repeat old mistakes. The soviets no longer assure unified action, since Bolsheviks are tolerated in their midst. We cannot march together with people who advance slogans about fighting for an unrealizable socialist revolution by unacceptable means that infringe on democracy; we cannot march with people who in the final accounting have betrayed the cause of the working class. They must be isolated. . . We must abandon the soviets and build new nonparty political associations of the working class that stand for a platform of class struggle and popular sovereignty.”” (Smith, p. 100)

As a result “Many Soviet delegates were arrested. The White Terror intensified.” (Медведев, p. 121)

According to a Menshevik writer “The Samara Soviet with its Bolshevik majority was disbanded” by the Mensheviks and Whites (David Dallen in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 172)

A worker conference was held in the Komuch territory in June 1918 “In a situation of terror supporters of the Soviet government were unable to put forward their candidates. The election results were rigged. But, despite this the Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries faced serious opposition at the conference. Of the 80 mandates read out, only 28 supported the power of Komuch” while the majority didn’t dare openly state their view (Медведев, p. 117).

At the worker conferences “the Mensheviks suffered losses and failure, although its composition was selected by the Mensheviks in advance… The majority of workers were hostile to Komuch in all the cities of the Middle Volga region.” (Медведев, p. 117)

“The conference was the scene of heated discussions… On June 22, the secret police arrested conference participants Romanov and Elkin. Delegate M. Orlov was arrested “for agitation against the war with the Bolsheviks” and was brought before a military court. Socialist-Revolutionary Lebedev at the meeting of the conference on July 15 threatened: “Those who remain at the meeting and do not want to go along with us… I warn my friends on the other side of the front… we are not joking, with an iron hand we will force you to submit and those who persist will be mercilessly destroyed by us.” (Медведев, p. 118)

Before the Czechoslovak and White Guard invasion “In the general mass of provincial congresses of Soviets [in the middle volga] in the spring of 1918 Communists made up more than half (52.4 percent). The remaining part of the mandates was allocated to non-party members (23 cents), less than a fifth (16.8 percent) were left-wing SR, and less than a tenth (7.7 percent) are representatives of the right the Socialist Revolutionaries, Mensheviks and other petty-bourgeois parties… Communists and sympathizers of the Bolsheviks made up 70 percent of the provincial executive committees… The district Soviets were also mostly Bolshevik.” (Медведев, pp. 45-46)

The population of Siberia resisted the Menshevik, Right-SR, White Guard and imperialist forces ferociously.

“During the last six months Siberia has been aflame with revolutionary uprisings. Since October [1918] a long series of peasant revolts has swept Western and Eastern Siberia, embracing the provinces of Altay, Akmolinsk, Tomsk, Enisseysk, and Irkutsk. The uprisings bore the broad character of mass action, involving not merely townships, but entire Siberian districts extending vast areas… volunteer detachments… had been organized in the course of fighting and had become closely bound together… Red volunteers… have assumed leadership in the guerilla warfare commenced by the people…

Red forces have succeeded in establishing an almost uninterrupted front between the station of Kamarchaga, 80 versts east of Krasnoyarsk, and the station of Nizhneudinsk—a front of over 200 miles. Backed by the sympathies of the peasant population of that district, the Red volunteers have during the present year gathered a considerable force… There were also many cases of open desertion of the mobilized Kolchak troops to the side of the Red volunteers…

Kolchak and the Allied command have thrown large forces into that district… Two Czech divisions have also been sent there. But the Czechs have long ago lost their desire to fight and the command itself does not put much confidence in them, endeavoring to bring up the more reliable Japanese soldiers.” (“In the Rear of Kolchak’s Army”, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, October 25, 1919 No. 21)

Red guerrillas had dealt devastating blows to the Whites in Siberia: “when the Red Army entered a city, or a village in Siberia, or else took possession of a part of the railroad, it found the ground prepared and the road ready for its coming. It was always met by a population not only friendly, but enthusiastic and impatiently waiting for it” (Zippin, “The Power Behind the Red Gun”, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 21, 1920 No. 8)

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/red-partisans-civil-war.jpg?w=700)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/red-partisans-civil-war.jpg)

***Siberian Red Partisan commanders***

A proclamation of the Siberian Red Guerrilas says:

“Comrades: The foreign bands, in collusion with our thickheads and hangmen, have throttled our October revolution here in Siberia, and have overthrown our people’s government.

“The enemies of the working masses, the enemies of of our revolution and the Soviet republic, the friends and the sons of the bourgeois elements in alliance with the soft-bodied traitors, the Mensheviki and the Socialists-Revolutionists, are robbing us of all the freedom and rights that we have won by our blood, and for which the Russian working class has striven for long generations. They are reviving the old order that the parasites, the manufacturers, the land-owners, and the czarist officers may again live in luxury. They are returning to the village exploiters the land that they robbed from the peasants, and to the officers their golden braid and high pay.

“Our enemies are destroying our revolution order, our acquired rights, together with the land riches that the workers, peasants and Cossacks have created by much labor and endurance. Let us, therefore, all of us, revolt as one man against them and let our battle cry be: Down with the exploiters.” (Reprinted in Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 21, 1920 No. 8)

To the Red Guerrillas, only volunteers recommended by three trusted comrades are accepted and “there is a uniform decision by the “local reds” that the revolutionary staffs under no circumstances shall expropriate the poor or even the middle peasants.” Their source of weapons and resources was mostly by simply expropriating from the Kolchak forces (Reprinted in Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 21, 1920 No. 8).

The Associated Press wrote that “According to well informed circles the Siberian armies were demoralized under Bolshevist propaganda, and, due to the long retreat, the men did not desire to fight. Their officers did not dare give battle under the circumstances. Because of desertions, the armies have dwindled to mere skeletons. Several units have been killed and some of the officers have gone over to the Bolsheviki.” (Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 21, 1920 No. 8)

“One would suppose that in the villages the Socialists-Revolutionists, with their mission of democracy, and the great words of the Constituent Assembly on their lips, have more luck. Not so. In the villages as in the cities it is the “local reds” that dominate and whose propaganda is supreme.

The Russkaya Ryetch (Novo Nikolayevsk) tells of a whole series of villages where the peasants refuse to pay taxes to the [White] “government” as well as to the Zemstvos. In the village of Novotroitsky, county of Bakinsk, the peasants held a meeting and — the following shortest resolution on record:

“Taxes we will not pay and you can’t do anything to us.” The collector sent out a hurry call for a punitive expedition, but no punitive expedition dared approach this village. The above Kolchak organ takes rather needless pains to prove that it is all the work of the horrible Bolsheviki. In the villages of Isakovskaya, Zhuravlevskaya, Moskovskaya, Tulskaya, the paper adds, the Bolsheviki have actually all the peasants with them, and have organized them for resistance.” (Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 21, 1920 No. 8)

“There is a confession of one of the Zemstvo workers that went to the villages to carry to them the great tidings of the organization of the [SR-Menshevik] Siberian committee… It was in the Amur region where he intended to work and his experiences are related in the “Rabotnik” of Blagovieshchensk, one of those little struggling Bolshevist organs that appear and disappear again, under the nose of the watchful Allied commanders and their allies, the Semionovs and the Kalmykovs.

“Almost all of the young men in the villages have left for the Bolshevist fronts, and there remain only the old folks and the children. And it is remarkable how even the old pious peasants are supporting the Soviets. They are not at all convinced that their sons have acted right in leaving for the numerous red fronts. They are not at al sure that this action is in accordance with their religion, and their old beliefs. But then it is their own sons, their own blood, and right or wrong, they cannot help siding with them. But while their feeling towards the Bolsheviki is somewhat hazy, their hatred toward Kolchak is both open and outspoken, and their hostility towards zemstvos and all the other so-called democratic organizations is even more glaring. Often our workers were attacked bodily by the old villagers who blamed all the troubles on us. ‘Why don’t you let our sons alone? Why do you come to aggravate us in our pain? It is enough that we have two fighting sides, and there you come with a new one.’—were interogations and explanations that were hurled at our workers from all sides at the meetings.

“One of our workers was nearly torn to pieces because he had chosen to make an insulting remark about one of the leaders of one of the villages, one who had since gone to the red front. Grishka was his name, and the Zemstvo instructor tried to belittle him, telling the peasants that Grishka was after all only an illiterate. A wonderful cry of anger came from all sides. They have known Grishka since childhood and they can all vouch for his honesty. Besides, ‘if he was as unimportant as you say’, came from one of the audience, ‘how is it that the Americans, the Japanese, the English, the French, and all the world are after Grishka?’ One had to hear the outbursts of the peasants. They are actually proud that one of theirs has become so important a figure as to have the whole world of governments after him.”…

The relater tried to organize the remaining few into a local of the Socialist Revolutionist party. He was nearly stoned. They wouldn’t hear of any other party but the Bolsheviki, or rather the Soviet party. They openly lay all the blame for their plight at the door of the Socialists-Revolutionists and the Zemstvos. Weren’t these parties once supporting Kolchak and the Allies? Not a kopeck would they give for the so-called democratic organizations. Surely nothing to the tax collectors. But the secret emmisaries of the “local reds” that come in the nights are fed with the peasants’ last crumbs.” (Zippin, “The Power Behind the Red Gun”, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 21, 1920 No. 8)

Even after Kolchak’s government was defeated, he tried to organize a new government in the Far East, which still masqueraded as supposedly “compatible with socialism”, supported by Mensheviks, SRs etc.:

“Kolchak… may “instruct his new premier to organize a new cabinet with the greatest possible socialistic tendencies compatible (?) with Kolchak’s stand against Bolshevism,” as the Associated Press tells us, but it is clearly the Island of Sakhalin whither he is heading, where he may organize that “socialistic” cabinet, with men like Cherven-Vodaly as the managers, of whose activities as well as of the so-called “National Center” [capitalist Kadet government] that he represents” (Max Zippin, “Back at the Old Folly”, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, January 31, 1920 No. 5)

In Irkutsk Mensheviks and Right-SRs with the support of Czechoslovak troops setup a government, the so-called ‘Political Center’ “which was just another disguise for a bourgeois government. But their plans came to grief. The insurgent workers of Irkutsk routed Semyonov’s men and other White forces, set up a revolutionary committee and dispersed the “Political Center.”… Irkutsk triumphantly met the victorious regiments of the Red Army.” (Panferov, p. 44)

In Vladivostok the underground Bolsheviks organized a general strike of workers which paralyzed the city on January 3rd 1920. [About an earlier strike see “The Meaning of the Strikes in Siberia”, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, November 1, 1919 No. 22) which cites other newspapers and also prints the statement of the Central Bureau of Trade-Unions, Strike Committee of the Merchant Marines etc.]

On January 26 1920 the workers of Nikolsk-Ussuriisk revolted, the White Guard troops mutinied and joined the Reds, the Red Partisans came to reinforce the rebellion and the city again established Soviet power under the Bolsheviks (Panferov, p. 45)

“Viscount Yasuya Uchida, former [Japanese] Ambassador at Washington… on his return here from his post as Ambassador to Russia, expressed doubt as to the wisdom of entering Siberia at this time. His conviction was that Bolshevism to-day represented the thought of a great majority of Russian people.” (W. P. & Zelda K. Coates, Armed Intervention in Russia: 1918-1922, p. 104)

In 1920 the Japanese had to begin evacuating their troops from Russia. The White Guard Semenov begged the Japanese to not leave. The Japanese replied to his request:

“The Imperial Japanese government have discussed your request from every angle. They are grateful to you and wish to maintain friendly relations, but the situation which is embarrassing us from every side does not permit us to accede to your request. Furthermore, the Japanese government do not consider you strong enough to help Japan in the achievement of her great aims. Your influence among the Russian people is waning day by day and the hatred which the Russians entertain for you is affecting our policy” (Quoted in Panferov, pp. 50-51)

“Semenov himself, along with Kalmykov and the remnants of their forces, crossed the frontier into Manchuria in 1921 and entered the direct employ of the Japanese secret service.” (N. G. O. Pereira, “Siberian Atamanshchina: Warlordism in the Russian Civil War”, in Brovkin (ed.) The Bolsheviks in Russian society, p. 133)

**VLADIVOSTOK**

Vladivostok, a far Eastern Siberian city, is an illustrative case so I’ll discuss it separately.

Vladivostok was another Soviet city which the imperialists invaded very early. Already in January 1918 the Japanese cruisers Iwanti, Asahi, the British H.M.S. Suffolk and the American cruiser Brooklyn arrived in the waters before Vladivostok (Panferov, p. 8)

The Japanese said they wanted to intervene in the city to protect their own citizens. The Mensheviks and Right-SRs in the municipal duma supported this demand.

“On March 29, 1918 the Menshevik-Socialist-Revolutionary municipal council fell in with the wishes of the Japanese military by declaring itself unable to maintain order in Vladivostok.” (Footnote in Lenin cw vol. 27)

The Japanese invaded Vladivostok “to protect Japanese citizens” on April 4th 1918 and “the treacherous Municipal Council of Vladivostok, which was controlled by Mensheviks and Socialist-Revolutionaries, upheld the action of the Japanese.” (Panferov, p. 12)

At first the Japanese did not commit any armed acts, but when the Czechoslovak troops arrived they and all the imperialists attacked the local communists.

“The political situation in Vladivostok caused the greatest anxiety to all parties. In the early morning of July 3 the Czechs attacked the town from the surrounding hills and drove the Bolsheviks into the central square. At that moment the Bolsheviks were surprised by machine-gun fire from the roof of the British Consulate, H.M.S. Suffolk having contributed the guns and, we heard, also the men. The Bolsheviks regarded this as an act of war” (Rickman, p. 13)

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/japanese-vladivostok.jpg?w=1024)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/japanese-vladivostok.jpg)

***Japanese, Czechoslovak, American, British troops in a joint parade in Vladivostok.***

A bourgeois observer in the naval city of Vladivostok says that “the sailors [are] Bolsheviki almost to a man” (D. Galton and J. L. H. Keep, “Letters from Vladivostok, 1918-1923”, The Slavonic and East European Review Vol. 45, No. 105 (Jul., 1967), p. 501). The bourgeois writer says that when traveling in the nearby industrial region the workers were preparing a strike in sympathy with the Reds of Vladivostok.

The White Guards, Czechoslovaks, British and Japanese forces all worked together. “the Czechs… shortly afterwards shot without trial two Bolshevik leaders, Sukhanov and Mel’nikov.” (Galton & Keep, p. 500)

“The Allies and the Czechs took the town, put the Bolshevik civil authorities in prison, and declared that they would give the town a free election without the possibility of Bolshevik coercion. The Bolshevik civil authorities who were in prison were returned at the election, and the Allies and Russians who were co-operating stated that as the men who had been elected were serving their sentences in prison a new Government would have to be constituted.” (Rickman, p. 13)

The Siberian Red Partisans were able to liberate Vladivostok eventually, but In April 1920 the Japanese heavily bombarded and re-occupied it and arrested the leader of the revolutionary committee, Bolshevik Lazo. He was given to the White Guards, who burned him alive. The Czechoslovak troops finally had enough and rebelled against the Whites and joined the side of the Reds. (Max Strypyansky, “Japan and the Russian Far East”, Soviet Russia, Vol. V New York, March 1, 1922 No. 4)

“the entire Maritime region (from Vladivostok to Pogranichnaya and to Khabarovsk) and the region of Amur (from Khabarovsk to Stretensk) are freed from the rule of Kolchak and from that of the atamans. In the Amur region and in Khabarovsk the Soviet Regime has been re-established” (“Soviets favored in Vladivostok, March 15, 1920”, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, April 24, 1920 No. 17)

When the Whites had decisively been defeated in Siberia the Far Eastern Republic was created, in which the Bolsheviks played a leading role. Even after this the Japanese and French both brought White troops, consiting of Semyonov’s forces and the Right-SR komuch forces, which landed in Vladivostok, establishing the Merkulov puppet dictatorship. This government proclaimed Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich the new Tsar of all Russia.

White Czechoslovak commander “Gajda subsequently departed for Czechoslovakia, where he later became the leader of the Czechoslovak Fascists during the interwar period.” (Smith, p. 208)

In 1922 the Japanese were finally forced out of Vladivostok and the White regime collapsed with them.

Again, no sign of Menshevik or Right-SR popularity. As told by several sources, including an American Red Cross activist who was present at the time, the Mensheviks and right-SRs with their White Guard troops arrested all the Bolsheviks and forced the Soviets to carry out new elections. But the people still voted for the Bolsheviks. The result was simply declared invalid by the Mensheviks and Right-SRs because the Bolsheviks were in prison and thus couldn’t participate in the Soviets!

Even in Khabarovsk the masses voted for the Bolsheviks, despite it being a military town where Bolshevik popularity was lower than elsewhere.

“Q. How many people do you suppose there were who were opposed to the Soviets before the Allies came?

A. You mean in Habarovsk ? Well, you see that city is really an officers’ city. It was the capital always of the Far East, and all the officials and all the banks and many governmental institutions were there, and, of course, all these officers were against it. Out of 50,000, perhaps, five or six thousand would be against the Soviet. All the higher officials were in the beginning against it. They would try to sabotage. Banks would go out on strikes. The higher teachers, too, went out on strike, but the parents called meetings and compelled them to go back and teach their children. We declared that if they did not go back they would have to go to work in the shops, and so they went back.

Q. Do you think that there was a higher percentage of people against the Soviets there than there was in other cities?

A. Oh, yes, because it is an officers’ city.” (“The Soviet of the Far East: Verbatim Report of a Conversation with’ Gertrude M. Robinson, wife of Krasnochokov, President of the Far Eastern Soviet in Siberia”, The Liberator, Issue No. 14, April, 1919)

I discussed quite a few cities in this article, but I still have election results and other data for many other cities, for example Saratov. I’ll discuss those in a later episode.
