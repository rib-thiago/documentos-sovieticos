# Menshevik attempt to overthrow Bolshevism: Ep.7 – “Were the Soviets destroyed? Who wanted to destroy them?”

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/soviet-congress1.jpg?w=611)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/soviet-congress1.jpg)

I’ve written several articles where I debunk anti-communist Vladimir Brovkin’s claim that the Mensheviks were extremely popular and the Bolsheviks extremely unpopular. In this article I’m not going to be talking about popularity or election results, I’ve done that in [a previous article](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/2024/10/13/menshevik-attempt-to-overthrow-bolshevism-ep-6-the-workers-assemblies-movement-1918-21/). Instead I’ll be focusing on another one of Brovkin’s main claims, which is that the Bolsheviks had to “destroy” the Soviets in order to maintain their own power.

In reality the Soviets continued to exist throughout the existence of the Soviet Union and they served as the local executive and legistlative organs of the state, and the Congress of Soviets in the Lenin era and the Supreme Soviet in the Stalin era, were the highest government organs.

In fact the number of Soviets continued to increase under Bolshevik rule, doubling in the rural areas after 1918\*. E. H. Carr has written about the creation of Soviets even in the tribal communities in the 1920s. He says nothing about the destruction of the Soviets, quite the contrary.

\*By the end of October 1917, Soviets existed in approximately half the villages. By 1918-19 “Bolsheviks succeeded in winning over the peasant masses and the lower Soviets.” (The Soviet Peasantry: An Outline History (1917-1970), p. 33)

Therefore we must ask what Brovkin and his ilk mean by “destruction of the Soviets” by the Bolsheviks.

There are a number of options, and in fact I think Brovkin believes a combination of all of these:

1) The anti-communist parties were eventually removed from the Soviets. For Brovkin and other anti-communists, this seems like horrible tyranny and an outright destruction of the Soviets, because they see the Soviets as nothing but municipal councils where different parties bicker.

2) They claim that the Soviets were practically destroyed, because of temporary restrictions or other arrangements implemented during the civil war. However, these arrangements were all only temporary, lasting at most 3 years. Therefore they do not count as “destroying” the Soviets.

3) They claim that since certain changes took place in the structure and functions of the Soviets, it amounted to their destruction. This argument warrants a more thorough examination.

When the Soviets were created after the February Revolution, they were more like permanent mass meetings than government organs. Their election procedures and function was extremely chaotic. Brovkin and other anti-communists claim that this was the only genuine Soviet movement. Brovkin claims that Bolsheviks ruined the Soviets by giving all the power to the Soviet Executive Committees and functionaries, but in reality the Soviets could never have remained in the same form eternally. The early chaotic form couldn’t achieve what the Soviets wished to do. The Soviets were far too large to get any effective work done, which was pointed out constantly by the Soviet delegates themselves.

The Soviets of the early days were in constant or nearly daily meetings but obviously “the mass meeting character [of the Soviets] of the early days could not be permanently maintained” (Kunal Chattopadhyay, “Revolutionary Democracy in 1917 and the Bolsheviks”, Economic and Political Weekly, Vol. 52, No. 44 (November 4, 2017), p. 66)

My main claim is that the Bolsheviks didn’t destroy the Soviets, and although the Soviets changed from being continous mass meetings into being state organs, and although they started to become more centralized, having a powerful executive committee etc., all of that was taking place already before the October Revolution. And furthermore, the Soviets were initially after the February revolution, elected quite unfairly, where the soldiers had many more delegates than workers, despite being a smaller number of people than workers.

The elections started becoming more fair and the number of workers in relation to soldiers started to increase, which benefited the Bolsheviks and helped them win power, but it is important to note that the soldiers themselves also went over to the Bolshevik side and abandoned the Mensheviks.

I’ve stated my claim, so now I will go on to prove it:

Anti-communists claim that because of bureaucratic Bolshevik tyranny, the Soviets delegated power to the Executive Committees, held meetings more rarely etc., but all of these things happened already when the Mensheviks and SRs had the majority in the Soviets.

The election practices in the early days were also not satisfactory. In some Soviets delegates could be elected to represent a 100 people, while in another they might represent a 1000 people. A work place of 500 people might have as many delegates as a work place of a 1000 workers. Workers were also seriously under represented in the early Soviets, while soldiers were hugely over-represented.

“In the early weeks of its existence the Petrograd soviet resembled a huge permanent assembly of workers and soldiers. The number of delegates grew from day to day; in the first week of March it reached 1,200; by the second half of March it rose to almost 3,000. Of this number, about 2,000 were soldiers and only 800 were workers, although at this time the total number of workers in Petrograd was two or three times that of the soldiers stationed in the garrison. The reason for this disproportion was that each military unit, even the smallest company, sent its own delegate to the soviet. There was as yet no rigorous check of delegates’ credentials, so that a number of outsiders found their way to the Tauride Palace. Under such circumstances, even though all delegates were never present at any one time, the Soviet’s plenary sessions were poorly organized; they resembled demonstrations and rallies more than they did a working parliamentary institution. Aside from the plenaries, the worker and soldier deputies conferred in separate sections on particular problems. But even these specialized bodies were still numerically too large to carry out regular work. On March 18 and 19, therefore, both sections discussed various detailed proposals for a reorganization to reduce the number of deputies and increase efficiency. Several speakers pointed out that the Soviet’s composition was fortuitous and that new elections would have to be held to create a truly democratic basis.” (Oskar Anweiler, The soviets : the Russian workers, peasants, and soldiers councils, 1905-1921, pp. 106-107)

However, Anweiler claims that the Executive Committee played a most important role since the very beginning, already when the Mensheviks and SRs held a majority. The important role of the Executive Committee was probably enhanced in those early days because the bloated Soviet session was ineffective:

“Most practical activity, however, remained in the hands of the Executive Committee (Ispolnitel’nyj komitet), which maintained its preeminence dating from the first days of the revolution.” (Anweiler, The soviets, p. 107)

“A special “Bureau of the Executive Committee” with 7 members was formed in mid-March to deal with current business. On April 12 the Bureau was authorized to take independent political decisions in emergencies, with only retroactive confirmation by the Executive Committee required…

In the course of about two months the Petrograd soviet thus changed from a provisional revolutionary organ into a well-organized administrative machine. The execution of its business required several hundred employees… However, as the soviet worked more efficiently, it lost proportionately its direct contact with the masses. The plenary sessions, almost daily during the early weeks, were less frequent and only sparsely attended by the deputies.” (Anweiler, The soviets, p. 108)

I think its not correct to claim the Soviet was losing contact with the masses simply because meetings were not held every single day, and simply because not everyone attended every meeting. These kinds of criticisms by anti-communists are totally unreasonable. A revolution is an extraordinary time. During a revolution, the masses might spend all day in mass meetings, but that cannot go on for very long. People have to go back to work, into production. It is not possible for the thousands of delegates to sit in permanent assembly all their lives.

Ironically if the delegates spent all their time in Soviet meetings and never went back to their regular jobs, that would actually mean the Soviet would lose contact with the masses.

Figes agrees with Anweiler:

Already under the Mensheviks and Right-SRs Soviet meetings “began to decline in frequency and attendance as the initiative switched to the executives and their quasi-governmental commissions, whose members were increasingly nominated by the party caucuses. From popular organs of direct self-rule, the Soviets were thus already beginning to be transformed into complex bureaucratic structures, although this process is more commonly associated with the period after 1917. At the time, it seemed a natural development” (Orlando Figes, A people’s tragedy : the Russian Revolution 1891-1924, London : Pimlico, 1997, p. 458)

“Election procedures favored preponderance of soldiers in the united Soviets in many places besides Petrograd. In Saratov, for example, 2 delegates were elected for each 350 workers and also 2 soldiers for each company (250 men) ; in Tula one delegate for each 500 workers and one for each company; in Ivanovo Voznesensk one delegate for each 500 to 1,000 workers and one for each additional 1,000, but among the soldiers again one delegate for each company.

In elections of workers delegates small plants were usually favored over large ones, since even factories with few workers were eager to have a delegate in the soviet. In Moscow one deputy was elected for each 500 workers, but never more than 3 from a single factory. Thus a plant with 1,500 workers sent 3 delegates to the soviet, but so did one with 7,000 workers. In Samara factories and workshops of 20-100 workers elected one deputy, those of 200-300 had 2, those of 300-1,000 3, and those of 1,000-2,000 5 deputies.” (Anweiler, The soviets, pp. 114-115)

After the February Revolution “The official ratio of representation for the workers section in the soviet was one deputy for 1,000 workers; however, concerns employing fewer than 1,000 workers were also allowed to send one delegate. Thus large plants (with over 400 employees), accounting for 87 percent of all Petrograd workers, furnished 424 delegates, while concerns with fewer than 400 employees, accounting for 13 percent of all workers, had 422 delegates. One could not, therefore, speak of equal suffrage; this failing was occasionally discussed in the soviet.” (Anweiler, The soviets, pp. 108-109)

Under the Mensheviks the soldier deputees were over-represented and “The soldiers deputies included relatively numerous “intellectuals”: clerks, young ensigns with socialist or liberal leanings, medics, and the like. The masses of politically inexperienced and still partially illiterate “peasants in uniform” voted for those who were most overtly revolutionary.” (Anweiler, The soviets, p. 109)

“In general, officers had the greatest influence in soldiers councils wherever these were independent and the working class weak.” (Anweiler, The soviets, pp. 116)

The Mensheviks sometimes claimed that they represented the workers, while the Bolsheviks supposedly received their support only from the peasant soldiers. In reality, as I will show later, the workers were the most firm support of the Bolsheviks. When soldiers were over-represented, it benefited the Mensheviks. However, the soldiers also eventually went over to the Bolsheviks, long after the workers had already done so.

Menshevik Sukhanov also admits that after the February revolution “the number of members of the Soviet had reached Homeric and absurd figures” (N. Sukhanov, The Russian revolution 1917: eyewitness account, vol. 1, p. 86) and that there was need for centralization.

Sukhanov even calls the Menshevik-SR Soviet illegitimate, because it was elected in such improper ways, had such over-representation by soldiers and didn’t correspond to the true opinion of the masses. He mentions that the Mensheviks therefore tried to prevent re-elections in order to retain their undeserved position:

“There were two or three times as many workers as soldiers in Petersburg. The workers elected one deputy per 1,000, the soldiers one per company. That is, the soldiers had between four and five times greater representation…

What about the crying disproportion in the representation of the workers and soldiers, with the Petersburg proletariat being completely swamped by the unstable peasant-soldiers? This question too was not settled for many months, almost down to the October overturn itself. It was not in the interests of the new Soviet majority, which controlled Soviet (and state) policy, and was completely reliant on this artificial, illegitimate peasant-soldier majority. In the last analysis, however, this ostrich-like policy did not help them.” (N. Sukhanov, The Russian revolution 1917: eyewitness account, vol. 1, p. 223)

Right-SR Eroshkin was complaining that even throughout 1918 there was too little centralization and “Each “business Soviet” was deciding things for itself without regard to, or taking account of the general industrial condition of the country” (Eroshkin, p. 24). Eroshkin of course was totally hostile both to workers’ control and to the Soviets.

According to a social-democratic writer:

“The Revolution therefore brought the Soviets into life on an economic basis and for an economic purpose, and in their first inception they were anarchic and without any common plan of action. During the first few weeks of the Russian Revolution, one Soviet knew nothing of what the other was doing. Only after the first month was it possible to talk of an organization which was gradually uniting and coordinating the actions of all the Soviets scattered about the country. This co-ordination became most imperative for the safety of the Revolution, because the forces of the old social order, which had been overthrown, soon began to gather strength again.” (M. Philips Price, “The Truth About Soviet Russia” (third installment), Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, August 16, 1919 No. 11)

“One group of workmen often struggled with another group in the attempt to get hold of the much needed raw materials. Meanwhile, famine became worse and worse and the Workers’ Soviets were in danger of turning into committees for grabbing whatever they could get for their own members. Then the Bolsheviks came along, and in October, when they came into power to control the Soviets, gave the latter political as well as economic power, as an organized proletarian mass.” (M. Philips Price, “The Truth About Soviet Russia” (first installment), Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, August 2, 1919 No. 9)

Even the magazine of the Anarcho-Syndicalist IWW writes that the Soviets are not sufficient for the task of reorganization of the economy unless suplanted by a national trade-union (“Bolshevismin Leveneminen”, Tie vapauteen, N:o 2/1919).

The Bolshevik party or government did not seek to undermine the Soviets or centralize their functions, instead “changes in its [Soviets] role and position appear to have been gradual, unplanned, and unsystematic, with surprisingly little in the way of direction or attempts at control by higher governmental or party authorities. Indeed, the evidence shows that many of the new responsibilities that the district soviet assumed during the first half of 1918 were undertaken on the soviet’s own initiative, in response to this or that new problem or crisis” (Alexander Rabinowitch, “The Evolution of Local Soviets in Petrograd, November 1917-June 1918, p. 36)

Before winning over also the soldiers, the Bolsheviks gained the most popularity in the organis which were thoroughly proletarian:

“Factory committees… became footholds for the Bolshevik Party much sooner than did workers and soldiers councils… At the first municipal conference of Petrograd factory committees, May 30 to June 3, 1917, 499 delegates met… Bolsheviks… predominated in the central council elected by the conference.” (Anweiler, The soviets, p. 126)

Bolshevik “influence made itself felt much earlier in the country’s industrial centers and in the principal cities, especially among factory workers. Thus, the Conference of Factory Committees in Petrograd at the end of May adopted a Bolshevik resolution by a large majority; demonstrations in Petrograd on June 18 used almost exclusively Bolshevik slogans” (Anweiler, The soviets, pp. 176-177)

“By October all trade unions in the large industrial cities supported Lenin’s party, except for the important railroad workers association, the postal and telegraphic union, and the printers.” (Oskar Anweiler, The soviets, p. 177) The highly paid printers supported Menshevism, while the postal and telegraph union supported the SRs.

Brovkin claims that the Bolsheviks wanted to make the Soviets controlled by small undemocratic Executive Committees, but the recognization that the huge size of the Soviets must be scaled down was reached by the Soviets themselves long before Bolsheviks gained a majority there. In Sukhanov’s opinion even the Executive Committee was too large and required more centralization:

“Towards the middle of March the Ex. Com. was already a corporation of about forty men, if not more, and was rather cumbersome” (N. Sukhanov, The Russian revolution 1917: eyewitness account, vol. 1, pp. 227-228)

Brovkin claims that the Mensheviks and Right-SRs represented real democracy while the Bolsheviks wanted to destroy the Soviets. But what was the actual view of the Right-SRs and Mensheviks? The Right-SRs and Mensheviks often stated that the Soviets should be dissolved.

The Right-SR “Kerensky himself assured the British ambassador Sir George Buchanan, as early as May: “The Soviets will die a natural death.”” (Anweiler, The soviets, p. 141)

Menshevik leader Tseretelli said: “Whenever in any region democratic organizations [city dumas and zemstvos] replace the Soviets, the latter will voluntarily hand over their work. Soviets will exist only before the change. When democratic self-governing bodies are elected by universal suffrage, they will assume the functions of self-government previously exercised by the Soviets.” (Anweiler, The soviets, 1905-1921, p. 141)

“Mensheviks and Social Revolutionaries wanted a gradual transfer of soviet functions to the Provisional Government and later to the national constituent assembly, the democratic municipal dumas, and the local zemstvos. During this transfer the Soviets would become ineffective or disappear altogether” (Anweiler, The soviets, p. 142)

According to a Right-SR writer:

“When the revolutionary storm had subsided and when, with the aid of the Soviets and the political parties, the necessity of the retention of the acquired liberties had penetrated deeply into the consciousness of the people; when laws fixing definitely the rule of the people locally (township, village and the Government Zemstvo) and centrally, for all Russia (the Constituent Assembly), based on the fundamental practices of universal democracy and the Socialist International — universal, direct, equal and secret suffrage — were drawn up and adopted, the Soviets should have retired in favor of the new governmental democratic institutions.” (Eroshkin, p. 6)

“the Soviets are not a democratic institution… they do not represent the people, and that they cannot, in fact, owing to their very form, represent it… we, Russian democrats and socialists, are opposed to the Soviets, and stand for a Constitutional Assembly and for all those who support it.” (Eroshkin, p. 10)

“The Menshevik leaders feared its convocation and tried to delay it. The Izvestiia editorial “The Crisis of Soviets” on 13 October argued that the soviets were losing their democratic potential. By their very composition they represented only the workers, soldiers, and peasants. They began to act as centers of political authority, and were divisive and counterproductive. The Mensheviks preferred to support institutions of local self-government, the dumas and zemstvos, which were elected on the basis of universal franchise and represented the entire society. It is difficult to reproach the Mensheviks for this reasoning, for their goal was establishing a parliamentary republic” (O. V. Volobuev, “The Mensheviks in the Fall of 1917: Decisions and Consequences”, ed. Brovkin, The Bolsheviks in Russian Society: The Revolution and the Civil Wars, p. 55)

In other words, the Mensheviks constantly demanded “universal suffrage”, by which they meant that capitalists, landlords, aristocrats, priests and okhrana members should be allowed to vote and be elected into the Soviets. Because the minority of terrible exploiters was not allowed into the Soviets, the Mensheviks and SRs denounced the Soviets as “non-democratic”!

The Menshevik CC stated in its declaration of Dec. 2, 1919 that:

““All Power to the Soviets” in its original sense, as it was understood by the masses that made the October Revolution, means anarcho-federalist chaos” (David Dallen in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 206)

Martov said “”the form of Soviet organizations was in all respects inferior to democracy… He defined the Soviets as being nothing more than ‘one form of militant organization of the working class arising in time of revolution’ which should be judged according to their capacity to realize the political and other tasks of the proletariat.” (Israel Getzler, Martov: a political biography of a Russian social democrat, p. 174)

Lenin wrote:

“Kautsky has to admit that the Soviet form of organisation is of world-wide, and not only of Russian significance, that it is one of the “most important phenomena of our times”, and that it promises to acquire “decisive significance” in the future great “battles between capital and labour”. But, imitating the wisdom of the Mensheviks, who have happily sided with the bourgeoisie against the proletariat, Kautsky “deduces” that the Soviets are all right as “battle organisations”, but not as “state organisations”.

Marvellous! Form up in Soviets, you proletarians and poor peasants! But, for God’s sake, don’t you dare win! Don’t even think of winning! The moment you will and vanquish the bourgeoisie, that will be the end of you; for you must not be “state” organisations in a proletarian state. In fact, as soon as you have won you must break up!” (Lenin, The proletarian revolution and the renegade Kautsky)

As we have seen, Mensheviks and Right-SRs often demanded that the Soviets be dissolved completely, and that is also what they did in practice almost everywhere. However, some of them dishonestly paid some lipservice to the Soviets, saying that the Soviets should exist temporarily, or that they were only struggle organs but not state organs, or that they were organs of municipal self-government (this was the Menshevik position in 1905), meaning that they should become the same as municipal dumas and also include capitalists in their ranks. Some Mensheviks stated that such capitalist Soviets, in fact municipal dumas, should exist under the power of the bourgeois parliament.

In reality the Soviets existed originally as dual power. They controlled nearly all the power in their locality, and competed with the capitalist state. They even gave orders to the army. No capitalist government could ever tolerate dual power for long. It was impossible for the Soviets to remain in existence if power was given to the capitalist parliament. Soviet power could only exist if Soviets took all the power to themselves and overthrew the capitalists, which the Mensheviks and SRs firmly opposed.

Brovkin tries his best to support the Mensheviks, but the truth comes out. Mensheviks “as democrats, they believed in universal suffrage. Hence, if they (or they together with the SRs) won a majority in a soviet, their duty was to vote to pass power to the city duma, since that body was elected by universal suffrage. Yet as socialists they were bound by the wishes of their worker constituents, among whom the idea that power should belong to the soviets was still quite popular. The workers… did not necessarily want to see power transferred to the dumas… the Mensheviks in the soviets increasingly emphasized their role of protecting the soviets” (The Mensheviks after October, p. 205)

He is using all kinds of mental gymnastics, but the meaning of his words is that: the mensheviks wanted to dissolve the Soviets, and the workers didn’t approve of it!
