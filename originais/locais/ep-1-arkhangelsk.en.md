# Menshevik attempt to overthrow Bolshevism: Ep.1 – Arkhangelsk

**INTRODUCTION**

Lately a theory has been going around, which claims that the Bolsheviks lost their popularity immediately after the October Revolution and that the Mensheviks regained a lot of mass support. For example, there is a video on youtube called “How the Bolsheviks destroyed the Soviets”, which advocates that theory. I’m not going to go over that video in detail because all of its claims essentially come from one article by anti-communist historian Vladimir Brovkin. So instead I will examine Brovkin’s claims, and the other information which is available.

Brovkin’s basic claim is that in the local Soviet elections of early 1918 the Mensheviks (together with Right-SRs, or the “Right-Socialist Revolutionary Party”) received a significant election victory against the Bolsheviks. From this he concludes that the masses supported Menshevism and opposed Bolshevism. Both the premise and the conclusion are immediately problematic, and ultimately false. Brovkin begins by admitting frankly that there is not enough information available on this topic.

He writes that “such study cannot be completed without access to central and local Soviet archives” (Menshevik comeback, p. 3), which is obviously true, and immediately raises the question: if he is not relying on archival information – then what is his source of information? He says the source is “shreds of evidence available in the West… reports in the opposition press and in the Bolshevik regional papers”, “reports by the Menshevik, SR, and Bolshevik leaders to their respective Central Committees (CCs)” and “a Menshevik report to the Second International”.

But that’s being overly generous, in actuality he uses practically only Menshevik and right-SR newspapers and statements by Mensheviks and right-SRs. He uses sources like Delo (Right-Menshevik paper), Delo Naroda (central organ of the Right-SRs), and white guard publications. He also cites a book edited by Solzhenitsyn. What can a Menshevik report to the Second International possibly prove? The Mensheviks obviously tell their foreign audience that the Russian masses support them, but that is not automatically a credible claim. Brovkin claims to also use some Bolshevik sources to verify his claims, but usually they don’t do that.

Brovkin recognizes that the Mensheviks and Right-SRs allied themselves to white guards and foreign imperialist powers which invaded Soviet Russia, and that they carried out a number of conspiracies, anti-communist uprisings, acts of terrorism etc. but he claims the Mensheviks and Right-SRs only did that in retaliation. I will demonstrate how that is false.

Brovkin’s thesis is that the Mensheviks and Right-SRs represented a genuine liberal-democratic alternative, which simply did not have a chance to develop because the civil war between the communist Reds and the monarchist-imperialist Whites polarized the situation, and because violent struggle replaced electoral democracy.

It is of course not inconceivable that the Mensheviks could’ve gained or retained some popularity in at least some places in Russia. After all, the October Revolution took place first in the two most important cities (Petrograd and Moscow) and began spreading elsewhere. The Bolsheviks gained the majority in the Soviets of most cities and came to power either peacefully or using revolutionary force. This usually took place within weeks or a couple months of the revolution in Petrograd, but not at the same time in all places, generally the further away places, and the places where the workers were weakest, lagged behind.

However, we know from facts that in some places the Mensheviks were able to stay in power or come back into power for some time. Let’s examine what those places were, how representative they were, and how it happened.

According to Brovkin, the Mensheviks gained popularity and challenged the Bolsheviks in places like Yaroslavl, the Volga Region, Arkhangelsk etc. In those places white guard uprisings against Soviet power took place at the same time as the supposed increase in Menshevik popularity. I claim that is not a coincidence, but actually the Mensheviks were acting as witting or unwitting tools of the foreign imperialists and Russian white guards, and in all or many cases the Menshevik claims about their supposed popularity and electoral success were merely intended to cover-up or justify the white guard uprisings, claiming they were responses to Bolshevik tyranny against the Mensheviks.

In this series, in the course of several episodes I’ll discuss Menshevik and Right-SR attempts to come to power and also election results and their popularity in those places for which data is available. This first episode will discuss Arkhangelsk. For now, I think the second episode will discuss Yaroslavl, the third episode will discuss Kostroma and Tambov, the fourth episode will discuss all of Siberia, and possibly others. Many other cities will be discussed only very briefly.

After that I will discuss Menshevik and Right-SR arguments and tactics they used to gain popularity, as well as reasons which might’ve contributed to their growing strength or to the weakening of Bolshevik power. I will also discuss who the supporters of Menshevism were, what classes and segments they came from etc.

In the final episodes I will discuss Brovkin’s sources and the question of sources on this topic in detail. Then I will make a much more thorough analysis of Bolshevik popularity, of election results to local Soviets and other similar information, and I will deal with the question “did the Bolsheviks destroy the Soviets?”

**ARKHANGELSK AND MURMANSK**

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/the-entry-of-british-interventionist-troops-into-arkhangelsk-in-1918-2c97426.jpg?w=1024)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/the-entry-of-british-interventionist-troops-into-arkhangelsk-in-1918-2c97426.jpg)

British invasion troops in Arkhangelsk

Many sources seem to agree that Mensheviks somehow gained majority in the Arkhangelsk Soviet, and in Murmansk, or—if not the majority—then at least the ideas and policies promoted by Mensheviks became dominant there. Why is that?

We know that the British and French imperialists had made a common plan with the Russian White Guards, Right-SRs and Mensheviks from the Savinkov organization, to launch an invasion into Soviet Russia and try to overthrow the Bolsheviks. I will discuss the Savinkov organization more once we get to talking about what happened in Yaroslavl. The plan of the Anglo-French invaders was to launch White Guard revolts in 23 cities, chosen based on their strategic position (from what I understand about strategy, apparently because they controlled the main transport routes of the country, railroads, rivers and the sea ports). As a strategic sea port, Arkhangelsk was one of those locations. It was chosen by the imperialists as their point of entry.

The British told the local Soviet quite openly through their representatives, that they wanted to send troops there to “protect Murmansk from the Germans”. The Mensheviks and right-SRs of the Soviet supported this proposal, while Bolsheviks and others opposed it.

According to Litvinov in Arkhangelsk the Anglo-French bribed local Soviet officials with money into accepting the proposal: “they had resource to the method of bribing the local leaders [of the Soviet] by specious promises and hard cash into concluding a “treaty of alliance” with them, whereby the Allies were allowed to land for the protection of the country from the Germans” (Litvinov, The Bolshevik revolution: its rise and meaning, p. 45)

The main henchman of the Anglo-French in the Arkhangelsk city Soviet was right-SR “Nicholas V. Chaikovski who was determined to eradicate every vestige of sovietism in the region” (according to capitalist historian Leonid I. Strakhovsky, “The Liquidation of the Murmansk Regional Soviet”, The Slavonic and East European Review. American Series Vol. 2, No. 2 (Nov., 1943), p. 19)

This will become a theme throughout this series, but even though Brovkin claims Bolsheviks destroyed the Soviets and that the Mensheviks and right-SRs defended the Soviets, we will consistently see from all the evidence that the Mensheviks and right-SRs were either committed to destroying the Soviets completely, or at least to minimizing their importance and allowing their destruction. However, the Mensheviks and Right-SRs understood that the masses would not accept the dissolution of the Soviets, so they had to be very careful and use deception.

In Murmansk Menshevik “Yuriev, [commander working for the British] Zvegintsov and [their assistant] Bramson argued that outright… abolition of the Soviet would… be unpopular with the masses” (Strakhovsky, p. 20)

“The leaders of the Murmansk Soviet, Vesselago and Yuriev, though anti-Bolshevik themselves, realized that the transition from the soviet form of government to a representative one on a democratic basis [a bourgeois parliamentary system] had to be a slow and gradual process. They believed that it could be done successfully only in such a way as their past experience had revealed to them when they… induced the Murmansk Regional Soviet to adopt a pro-Ally [Entente]… platform” (Strakhovsky, p. 21)

In other words, when they had induced the Soviet to accept the arrival of the British, they knew the British would then help them destroy the Soviet. That is in fact what happened. The Mensheviks and other pseudo-socialists were able to invite the British to Murmansk, who then arrested the Bolsheviks and dissolved the Soviet. Before being invited the British had promised they would not intervene in the affairs of the Soviet but were there only against the German danger (Strakhovsky).

This was because even despite literal bribery and other fraud, the population and the Soviet would only accept the British coming there if they promised to leave the Soviet unharmed, but of course it was a lie! This was the typical Menshevik and Right-SR method of operating.

Despite all their schemes, at least according an officer in the western military intervention into Arkhangelsk, the Mensheviks did not yet have power there until the imperialist troops arrived: “when our governments sent out this expedition the government of Archangel… was Bolshevik” (Ralph Albertson, Fighting without a war; an account of military intervention in north Russia, p. 8)

Colonel Thompson, an agent of entente imperialism in Russia, created a committee to spread propaganda to convince the Russians to continue their participation in the World War. He understood that the Russian people did not want to remain in the war, and therefore they would have to be fooled and scared into it. For this purpose he hired the Mensheviks and Right-SRs:

“Colonel Thompson… perceived, as a fact, that the Russian peasant was not interested in “saving” the Allies. The Russian peasant did not think of the Allies as his allies. He thought of them as the Czar’s allies. He saw no reason for being ”loyal” to them. The Russian peasant and the Russian workman, Colonel Thompson perceived, were interested, really, in just one thing — the revolution and the fruits of the revolution. He proposed, therefore, through writers writing very simply, and through speakers speaking very simply, to go to them and say:

“The one imperialistic power which is next door to you, and which is able to conquer you and which is actually trying to conquer you, is the power of the Central Powers. Its soldiers are advancing. You cannot stop them with Socialism. They knew Socialism before you knew it. They are hypnotized by the Kaiser and by false patriotism. They are mad. They keep coming on; and with them, as you know, as you can see, on comes the old day once again in Russia. Wherever the Germans go, the old day goes with them. Back of the German bayonets are your own Russian barons, returning to take from you the land which is yours by the Revolution. Back of the German bayonets are your own old industrial masters, returning to give you the old twelve hours a day and the old two rubles a day instead of the eight hours and the fifteen rubles of the Revolution. Back of the Kaiser is the Czar, the Kaiser’s kin, returning to destroy the Revolution itself. To keep the Czar down, to keep the revolution up, you must fight the Kaiser.”

Truer words, in the light of that moment, it would manifestly have been difficult to frame. Colonel Thompson proposed to have them written and spoken throughout Russia. The Russian members of this new committee were in full agreement with him. They were persons of a standing certainly unimpeachable. They were:

Madame Catherine Breshko-Breshkovskaya, grandmother of the revolution, anti-Bolshevik. Nicholas Tchaikovsky [Chaikovsky], leader of the peasant co-operatives, afterward head (under the Allies) of the Government of the North in the Archangel district, anti-Bolshevik. B. Lazarov, formerly manager of the revolutionary “underground station” in Switzerland, anti-Bolshevik. General Neuslochowsky, one of the most trusted of Kerensky’s generals, anti-Bolshevik. David Soskice, Kerensky’s private secretary, anti-Bolshevik.

Such was the committee. It was strong in personnel, but it was weak in money. Colonel Thompson attended to that detail. He provided money to the extent of a million dollars.” (William Hard, Raymond Robins’ own story, pp. 35-37)

This is only one example of the dishonesty of the imperialists, Mensheviks and Right-SRs. They claim that in order to protect socialism and oppose monarchism it was necessary to join with the imperialists, but the imperialists themselves secretly (and later even openly) supported monarchist White Guards. This also demonstrates the artificial nature of Menshevik and Right-SR organization – it was totally funded by foreign powers.

The Mensheviks and right-SRs had a stronger base of support in Arkhangelsk compared to other places, because Arkhangelsk was not a working class area.

“The composition of the city’s population also determined the nature of the Soviet. Most of the people in it belonged to petty-bourgeois elements, full of all sorts of prejudices, among which patriotic illusions played the main role.” (Mints, Anglijskaya Intervenciya I Severnaya Kontrrevoljuciya, p. 44)

The petty-bourgeois is the most susceptible to narrow nationalism, unlike the workers with their international solidarity and the capitalists with their ties to the world market. The Brest-Litovsk Peace Treaty which caused Russia to lose Ukraine and the Baltics, offended the nationalism of the petty-bourgeois and also caused them to be afraid of Germany, making the British intervention seem more acceptable to them.

The Right-SRs also retained stronger support in Arkhangelsk compared to working class or poor-peasant areas, because the region had never had any landlords. The Bolshevik land-reform against landlords won the support of the poor and middle-peasants in most places, but in the Arkhangelsk area there had never been any landlords.

Furthermore, the local economy depended largely on commerce, not only on farming. It was a heavily petit-bourgeois commercial economy. The October Revolution (or rather the blockade imposed by the imperialists) put an end to that commerce, causing dissatisfaction. The local kulaks were strong, the middle peasants were wavering towards the kulaks, and the proletariat was small and weak. The proletariat still consisted mostly of semi-proletarians, people who were half-time peasants themselves, and recent migrants from the villages. (Mints, p. 62)

Instead of idealistic claims that “our side” or “their side” has the support of the masses, we must look at these kinds of material realities. Different classes have different interests and different behavior.

However, it is very telling that the Mensheviks and right-SRs were able to maneuver and gain positions of power only in places like these, where conditions were exceptionally favorable, where the proletariat did not exist or was very weak etc. and even in those places they had to use outright lies and bribery. The Soviets of Workers’, Soldiers’ and Peasants’ Deputies were of course elected only by the working people (this was even stated in the Soviet constitution, which codified the Soviet structure that had already formed) meaning that the bourgeoisie or anybody who exploited the labor of others or was a commercial speculator, was not able to vote in Soviet elections, only working people were. However, this was not the case here:

“the form of government that had developed in the Murman region (i.e., the Murmansk Regional Soviet not merely of workers’, soldiers’ and peasants’ deputies, as under the Bolsheviks, but representing the entire population” (Strakhovsky, p. 22)

If only workers or working peasants had been able to vote, the Mensheviks and right-SRs would’ve had absolutely no support, despite all their other trickery, but the Soviet was elected mostly by petty-bourgeois and partially by big capitalists. The bourgeois had boycotted, tried to destroy, and stayed out of Soviets. Yet Mensheviks invited them to vote in Soviets against the Soviets.

Early in 1918 a White Guard organization was created for the restoration of capitalism in Russia called the Union for the Revival of Russia. According to its leader, S. P. Melgunov, discussions to form the Union between the capitalist party of Cadets, right-Mensheviks, right-SRs, “trudovik” fake socialists etc. were already going on in February 1918. Already at that time Chaikovsky from Arkhangelsk was involved (Melgunov’s book quoted in Mints, p. 63).

From Vologda, where all the western imperialist embassies were located, and which was a center of imperialist espionage, hundreds of tsarist officers and French officers funded by the French went to Arkhangelsk and Murmansk and signed up as “right-SR” party members to avoid the accusation of being white guards. These French and tsarist officers began filling the Soviet and other institutions. The workers would’ve rebelled had they known what these people were:

“Through only one Vologda organization in a short period in one month, more than a hundred officers passed.” (Mints, p. 64)

“The Soviet… organisation was honeycombed in the upper and technical grades by Russian officers who took service with them with the intention… of deliberately betraying their cause” (W. P. & Zelda K. Coates, Armed Intervention in Russia: 1918-1922, p. 92)

The Entente plot was also helped by the fact that Trotsky was willing to make a deal with the Entente and to accept their “help” against the Germans. He wanted to accept material aid, training for the Red Army and a potential military alliance. Lenin and Stalin were both against this and the scheme fell through.

Anti-communist historian Schapiro cites “a record of a telephone conversation between Stalin and [Menshevik] Yur’ev of the Murmansk Soviet… when Stalin was urging a more vigorous anti-allied policy. Yur’ev then referred to Trotsky’s original telegram in the spring, which had urged full co-operation with the allied force. Stalin’s reply is of interest: ‘Trotsky’s telegram has nothing to do with the case. It won’t help matters and we are not proposing to look for scapegoats’”. He also cites “an angry note from Lenin, of early June, stressing that Soviet policy is ‘equally hostile to the British and to the Germans’.” (Leonard Schapiro, The origin of the communist autocracy; political opposition in the Soviet state: first phase, 1917-1922, Footnote on p. 119)

“In February and March 1918, British naval forces were landed at Murmansk” (Coates, p. 81)

The British, the Mensheviks, SRs and capitalists began discussing dissolving the Soviets when the British forces arrived. Menshevik Vesselago declared that the workers would rebel if they discovered what was happening. “Vesselago… declared that if the… Soviet… were destroyed, it would be “impossible for us under present political conditions to preserve order since a new form of government at this time **would not have the support of public opinion**.” (Strakhovsky, p. 22)

The British invasion forces officially come to Arkhangelsk in August 1918 but already for half a year they had had their people in Arkhangelsk and in Murmansk surrounding Arkhangelsk, and thus were able to control the situation there. Before their invasion troops arrived the British tried to create an armed force from Finnish worker refugees, trying to use them to carry out an anti-Bolshevik coup. However, this failed.

The British tried to trick the Finnish refugees into joining this armed force by promising it would be used only against Finnish White Guards who were invading Soviet Karelia. Again the same old tactics!

Major General Maynard wrote in his memoirs that among the Finnish refugees who had been recruited by the British:

“Dissatisfaction among them was so apparent already months ago, that it was impossible to ignore, and which I followed with the greatest worry… Politically speaking the members of the Legion were revolutionary “reds” so it proved impossible for me to use them against the Bolsheviks” (Major General, Maynard, “The Murmansk Venture”)

“The Legion consists of approximately 1200 fighters, with a well trained artillery battery. They won’t fight the Bolsheviks, in particular because many of these Finns have the same political views as them, and have joined the Bolsheviks” (Maynard’s telegram to general Radcliffe)

As a result, the British had to wait until their own invasion troops arrived, and to find some other troops from somewhere. This is where the Czechoslovak troops come in. I will talk about that later in this video. Once the British-Menshevik-SR dictatorship was in place, it decreed “To instruct the governor-general of the northern region to appoint a commission for the liquidation of the institutions of the Murmansk Regional Soviet” (Strakhovsky, p. 23)

“Following the issue of this order the red flag over the building of the Soviet was lowered and the national tricolor was hoisted in its stead.” (Strakhovsky, p. 24)

The reactionaries stated the “District Soviet was an authority organized on the pattern of the Central Bolshevik Authority… by its actions devoted all its efforts to the realization of the fundamental purpose of the Bolshevik Authority” (Strakhovsky, p. 28) and on this basis the Soviet was dissolved and members of it, some even non-Bolsheviks, were arrested by the reactionaries.

“The way in which they [the British] have respected their pledge not to interfere in the internal affairs of the region is best illustrated by the fact that after capturing Kem they arrested the local Soviet and shot three of its members, and that having set up at Archangel a bogus “native” Government with the renegade Socialist, Tchaykovsky, at its head they have now, through that Government, abolished all the local Soviets” (Litvinov, p. 46)

“Tschaikowsky was made President… His government… was only the creature of foreign military authority… the venerable Tschaikowsky was gradually put aside and ignored and before long sent to London on an important mission, never to return, but still a valuable figurehead, while a Russian military government grew up under the aegis of the British army, composed of monarchists and military men of the old school. The head of this government was General Miller (Mueller) a militarist and monarchist who is without popular Russian support and whose position is entirely due to his standing with the British military establishment.” (Albertson, pp. 9-10)

The organization setup by the capitalist Cadets, Mensheviks, SRs and White Guards with Anglo-French support, The Union of Revival accepted a demagogic program, trying to gain popularity and fool the population. It appealed to stupid nationalism and promised “everything good to everyone” as these types of programs always do.

It mainly demanded non-recognition of the independence of Ukraine, Finland and Poland and promised food to everyone. In practice however, in Arkhangelsk and the Murmansk region the Union dismantled workers’ control, ended the 8 hour work day, arrested workers, evicted trade unions from their buildings, gave work places back to their private owners. The SRs and Mensheviks attempted to keep the workers calm and wanted to “negotiate” with the capitalists. The Union of Revival did not dare dissolve the social insurance system created by the Soviet government, because the workers would’ve rebelled, but capitalists stopped paying insurance taxes (Mints, p. 72 quoting right-SR Likhach)

Menshevik organizers told the Union of Revival government that workers are complaining about the new laws, that a trade-unionist was beaten for hoisting a red flag and the culprit, a bourgeois specialist, “is at large, while many workers are in prison, without any charges being brought against them”

The metal workers adopted the following decision in their meeting:

“The Union of Metalworkers considers it necessary to create a council of workers’ and soldiers’ deputies… it is proposed that all unions form committees for re-establishing it in the very near future.” (Minutes of the meeting of the Executive Commission of the Council of Trade Unions, reported by Menshevik Northern Ray, quoted in Mints, p. 93)

The Mensheviks and SRs considered it smart to allow some kind of workers’ organizations, because workers would regardless try to establish Soviets or something analogous, but the capitalists did not agree, and instead all members of Soviets were arrested (Mints, p. 93).

When the British landing troops came, most workers who were Bolshevik supporters or Red Guard members fled the area to avoid death. But even those who stayed, who were either temporary supporters of Menshevism or at least undecided, quickly turned against the Mensheviks and SRs.

“The active part of the workers left the city with the Red Guard detachments, with various institutions of Soviet power, with party organs. Among the workers only the Mensheviks remained… It is characteristic that it was these groups [i.e. even the more backward workers] that first raised the demand for the restoration of Soviets.” (Mints, p. 76)

“However, even remaining entirely under the leadership of the Menshevik leaders, the working masses did not react joyfully to the coup [by the British, Menshevik and SRs]. The mood of the workers was characterized, for example, by the meeting of printers on August 5. According to the report on the current moment, all speakers agreed that they shouldn’t trust or distrust the new government as a matter of course [but should wait and see]… The next day, a meeting of electricians adopted a resolution to provide support to authorities only if they really stand in defense of the working masses, i.e., again, trust was only issued conditionally.” Another general meeting of workers also accepted a resolution saying it doesn’t trust the new Menshevik-SR government. (Mints, pp. 76-77)

In Arkhangelsk martial law was imposed by the reactionaries, tsarist police were restored, striking workers were arrested (even printers, despite the printer union being the one were Mensheviks had their strongest basis), immediately when the British arrived hundreds were arrested by the counter-revolutionaries (not counting those arrested by the British) and since these are from official figures of the Union of Revival, the real figure is probably higher. This is despite the fact that most Bolsheviks, their sympathizers and the Red Guards had already fled the city previously (cf. Mints).

Albertson says that the dictatorship installed by the SRs, Mensheviks and the imperialists quickly turned everyone totally against them, and implemented ruthless white terror:

“The inquisitorial processes of the Russian puppets of the Military Intervention were necessarily so much like those of the old regime that they went far to dispel all illusions about the Military Intervention that might have remained in the peasant mind.

When night after night the firing squad took out its batches of victims it mattered not that no civilians were permitted on the streets. There were thousands of listening ears to hear the rat-tat-tat of the machine guns” (Albertson, p. 71)

“the Russian soldiers whom we organized, equipped, and paid to fight the Bolsheviki went over… in whole companies to the Bolsheviki” (Albertson, p. 73)

The imperialist-Menshevik regime relied on lies, they even lied to the invading troops because otherwise they might mutiny:

“It was important to inspire fear and hatred of the Bolsheviki… After the evacuation of Shenkursk we were told, with complete details, of the murder of the nuns and the Abbess, and of the members of several families who were well known to us, also of the forced marriage to favored Bolsheviki of some of the young ladies who in the happy days had danced with our officers. We were told of rape and of tortures, all in convincing circumstantial setting…

In July, however, we learned the truth — at least I did. Three Russians whom I had known all winter and in whom I have the utmost confidence, went to Shenkursk; stayed there incognito a week, and came back. They told me that they had seen the nuns, and talked with the people who were supposed to have been murdered, that the Abbess was alive, that the girls were unmarried, and that there had been no forced marriages whatever.” (Albertson, pp. 84-85)

The next year there were already mutinies among the American invasion troops: “in Arkhangelsk, the Americans are refusing to obey the order to attack” (“Bolshevismin Leveneminen”, Tie vapauteen, N:o 2/1919). There were also mutinies among other invading troops, the British, French etc. and many of them were pulled away from Russia to avoid “infection with Bolshevism”.

Lieut.-Colonel Sherwood-Kelly wrote in the Daily Express on September 6, 1919:

“That the troops of the Relief Forces [British invasion troops] which we were told had been sent out purely for defensive purposes, were being used for offensive purposes, on a large scale and far in the interior, in furtherance of some ambitious plan of campaign the nature of which we were not allowed to know…

“I discovered, what is now a matter of common knowledge even in England, that the much vaunted ‘loyal Russian Army,’ composed largely of Bolshevik prisoners dressed in khaki, was utterly unreliable, always disposed to mutiny, and that it always constituted a greater danger to our troops than the Bolshevik armies opposed to them.

“This was tragically demonstrated early in July, when the Russians mutinied and murdered their British officers.

“I formed the opinion that the puppet-Government set up by us in Archangel rested on no basis of public confidence and support, and would fall to pieces the moment the protection of British bayonets was withdrawn.

“At the same time I saw British money poured out like water and invaluable British lives sacrificed in backing up this worthless army and in keeping in power this worthless Government, and I became convinced that my duty to my country lay not in helping to forward a mistaken policy, but in exposing it to the British public.”” (Quoted in Coates, pp. 170-171)

“the French troops who had fought on the Western Front had no desire to die in Russia. They succumbed to Red propaganda so fast that they had to be evacuated in haste from Odessa and Sevastopol in April 1919.” (D. F. Fleming, The Western Interventions, p. 3)

“The French troops were themselves affected by the Communist propaganda, and practically the whole fleet mutinied.” (The World Crisis: The Aftermath, p. 167)

“If [anyone] now proposed to send a thousand British troops to Russia for that purpose, the armies would mutiny. The same applies to U. S. troops in Siberia; also to Canadians and French as well. The mere idea of crushing Bolshevism by a military force is pure madness.” (The Bullitt mission to Russia, 1919, p. 8)

Madness or not, the imperialists continued to try for three years.
