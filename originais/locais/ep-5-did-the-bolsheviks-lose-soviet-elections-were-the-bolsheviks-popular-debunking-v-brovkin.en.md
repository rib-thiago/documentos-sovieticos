**WORKERS’ CONFERENCE ELECTIONS IN 1918**

The Mensheviks and SRs had been banned from the Soviets after their participation in the Yaroslavl counter-revolution and other similar actions in the summer of 1918, but in Workers’ Conference elections all parties competed. Let’s see what the voting results were:

“The original impulse for the district workers’ conferences that were convened in Petrograd in the spring of 1918 came from the dramatically expanding dissatisfaction of Petrograd labor with deteriorating economic conditions…

The First City District workers’ conference was by no means an exclusive enterprise of Bolsheviks and Left Socialist Revolutionaries; in accord with the Interdistrict Conference’s instructions, all political groups in the First City District Soviet were represented in the organizing and credentials commissions.

Among the voting delegates to the conference itself were delegates from all local factories and Red Army units, the First City District Soviet, district trade unions, united cooperatives, the unemployed, and Bolshevik, Left Socialist Revolutionary, Menshevik, Menshevik-Internationalist, and Socialist Revolutionary committees in the First City District. A majority of conference participants were elected at special factory and office assemblies and at meetings of the unemployed according to specified norms; an estimated 23,000 to 25,000 “working citizens” were represented. While Bolsheviks and Left Socialist Revolutionaries had some advantages in the election of delegates, even Bolshevik opponents seemed to concede at the conference’s close that, on the whole, it had been fairly organized and run. All told, among the 201 voting delegates were 134 Bolsheviks (67 percent), 13 Left Socialist Revolutionaries (6 percent), 30 Mensheviks and Menshevik-Internationalists (15 percent), and 24 Socialist Revolutionaries (12 percent).

Among an additional 30 delegates with deliberative (nonvoting) rights were 24 Bolsheviks, 3 Left Socialist Revolutionaries, 2 Mensheviks and Menshevik-Internationalists, and 1 Socialist Revolutionary.” (Alexander Rabinowitch, “The Evolution of Local Soviets in Petrograd, November 1917-June 1918: The Case of the First City District Soviet”, Slavic Review, Volume 46, Issue 1, Spring 1987, pp. 29-30)

“the conference format assured the airing of opposing views. Each of these spokesmen was allotted fifteen minutes. “The political make-up of the First City District Soviet, as it was reformed following partial elections in mid-June 1918, is described in Vestnik, no. 16-17, 6 July 1918, pp. 7-8. **RURAL SOVIET ELECTIONS (1919)**

Orlando Figes, who is an extremely anti-communist historian, talks about the rural Soviet elections and states that they were completely fair:

“The commission’s chairman opened the meeting by asking the peasants if anyone should be added to or struck off the list of voters; each suggestion was debated in turn. They told what their plans had been and how they had carried them out, and ended: “Look in your own pay-envelope and decide whether you are better off this year than last” . . . The Communists got a larger percentage of the votes than at any time before.” (Anna Louise Strong, The First Time In History: Two Years Of Russia’s New Life (August 1921 To December 1923), p. 22)

At one election meeting “A Social Revolutionary got up, and denounced the government for failing to keep its promises. ‘Two years ago they promised you a new world,’ he said. ‘Now they offer you a better water supply and a few more electric lights.’… But here also the Communists were chosen.” (Strong, The First Time In History, p. 51)

“How do the Communists keep the power in these elections? How would the workers throw out the Communists if they didn’t like them? Where would dissatisfaction express itself first? The Communists know all the tricks of politics that are known in any country; the control of press, of election boards, of political machinery. In the elections themselves I could not detect any atmosphere of compulsion; they asked questions freely and went out satisfied. I have no doubt that, when necessary, the Communist Party uses all the various means known in politics for keeping itself in power. But these means do not work forever in other countries ; the opposition strengthens and at last supplants the government. In Russia the opposition grows less with each new year ; old parties dissolve and beg for peace. The Communist control goes farther back into industry and life than any mere political trick or compulsion… it has organised itself to keep in power for a generation, by studying the desires of all the people, over thousands of miles of country, and by supplying those desires, as far as it finds it necessary, moulding them always a little further in the direction of its aim.” (Strong, The First Time In History, p. 52)

“The year before the complaint had been about food, made in the form of protest meetings and near-riots. Now everyone had enough to eat. There were no more protest meetings. The complaints went in orderly manner through unions and factory committee and press, and were eventually attended to in slow Russian fashion.” (Strong, The First Time In History, p. 68)

“At the time of Captain Sadoul’s writing [1918-19], the election returns showed that seventy per cent of the people were Sovietists [on the side of the Bolsheviks], but by this time the number must have been considerably augmented.” (Jacques Sadoul, “Impressions of Moscow a Year Ago”, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, January 17, 1920 No. 3)

“the force of Brovkin’s major theme-that the socialist opposition and the Mensheviks in particular were embraced by Russia’s workers as a serious viable alternative to the Bolsheviks is not sustained, in particular being weakened by the author’s narrowness of focus… the impressive work by Jane Burbank, Intelligentsia and Revolution: Russian Views of Bolshevism 1917-1922… clearly shows their inability to take into account the fundamental social, economic and political differences between the European environment that nourished Menshevik ideology and Russian political, cultural and economic realities. Under these conditions it was extremely unlikely that Menshevism could have harnessed the proletariat to their cause. As a result, Brovkin’s work has not managed to dispel the contention that the Menshevik revival in 1918 was ever anything more than… ephemeral” (Zuckerman, p. 615)

In his biography of the Menshevik leader Martov (Martov: a political biography of a Russian social democrat, p. 179) Israel Getzler says that in some places Mensheviks and Right-SRs won majorities in Soviets, but he cites no source of any kind. He must be basing this claim exclusively on Martov’s statements, because at one point in the book he quotes Martov making that claim.

A capitalist reviewer of Brovkin’s work says that Brovkin takes a far-right cold-warrior position, that he attacks all modern research as “Soviet apologism” (indeed he even calls E. H. Carr, who is not a recent author, a “marxist”). His work is intellectually indebted to the cold-war propaganda of Richard Pipes:

“Brovkin is equally if not more dismissive of the revisionist historians of the 1970s and 1980s, whom he characterizes as apologists for the Soviet regime. The politics of this collection, or at least that of several contributors, profoundly compromises its value. In this context, one might speak of a “Pipes school,” as Pipes himself is a contributor; Brovkin and Anna Geifman are obviously intellectually indebted to him, and Jonathon Daly, Leonid Heretz and Scott Smith were all his graduate students (this accounts for nearly half of the contributors). The reader has the impression that, for some of the writers at least, the newly opened archives are simply a cudgel to be wielded against the revisionists\* rather than an opportunity to increase our knowledge of the revolutionary period.” (Rupp)

\*The “revisionist school” are historians like Arch Getty, who wanted to reconsider Soviet history from a more evidence based perspective and with a focus on the people and not only on the state leaders. They are all moderate anti-communists.

“Brovkin’s own contribution… is little more than a diatribe against the Bolshevik regime… Brovkin’s piece borders on the outrageous, and is far from persuasive scholarship.” (Rupp)

Indeed, Geifman is so far-right they complain that western capitalist historians are too strongly influenced by ‘marxist’ views (Brovkin ed., The Bolsheviks in Russian Society: The Revolution and the Civil Wars, p. 25).

Brovkin is clearly part of the far-right Pipes school. In his book Behind the Front Lines of the Civil War: Political Parties and Social Movements in Russia, 1918-1922, he thanks his mentors and helpers, Richard Pipes and Adam Ulam, both famous far-right cold-warriors.

Pettit, Mr. Bullitt’s assistant stated “In Petrograd I presume the present communist government has a majority of the workingmen behind it… my conclusions are based on conversations with not only communists, but also many opponents of the communist government, members of the aristocracy, business men, and foreigners” (The Bullitt mission to Russia, 1919, p. 131)

“The Soviet form of government is firmly established. ***Perhaps the most striking fact in Russia today is the general support which is given the government by the people***” (The Bullitt mission to Russia, 1919, p. 51)
