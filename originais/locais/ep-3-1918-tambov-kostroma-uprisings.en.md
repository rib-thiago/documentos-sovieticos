# Menshevik attempt to overthrow Bolshevism: Ep.3 – 1918 Tambov & Kostroma uprisings

**TAMBOV**

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/tambov4.jpg?w=771)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/tambov4.jpg)

Anti-communist historian Brovkin claims that the Mensheviks and Right-SRs enjoyed much more popularity than the Bolsheviks, and won practically all local elections of 1918. He cites an article in Menshevik Sukhanov’s newspaper in the Soviet elections of Tambov in April 1918 the Bolsheviks, Mensheviks and SRs tied, each having roughly the same amount of votes, though he doesn’t say anything about Left-SRs. However, the Left-SRs everywhere supported the Bolsheviks in elections. According to the Menshevik newspaper, the Bolsheviks pointed out reasons how the election was carried out improperly. The newspaper calls this an excuse, but doesn’t contest that it was so. I haven’t been able to find any other information to corroborate any of these claims (Brovkin, Dear comrades: Menshevik reports on the Bolshevik revolution and the civil war, p. 85).

James Bunyan, Intervention, civil war, and communism in Russia, April-December, 1918 : documents and materials (p. 559) states that the Novaya zhizn article which Brovkin cites on Tambov, is actually only quoting a Menshevik paper called “Iskra”, the organ of the Kiev regional organization of Mensheviks, published in Ukrainian nationalist territory. Novaya zhizn is usually considered a slightly better source because it is a left-Menshevik paper, but as we can see, it is actually citing an ultra-rightist Menshevik newspaper published by Mensheviks collaborating with Ukrainian nationalists and White Guards.

Some other sources also state that Mensheviks and the Right-SRs received a majority in the Tambov city Soviet, but those other books also cite the same Menshevik source given by Brovkin. I tried very hard to check all their sources. Anweiler cites the same bad source as Brovkin and also two Soviet books. I was able to find one of them, but it doesn’t actually discuss Tambov, it discusses Vyatka. It also doesn’t say that the Mensheviks won.

Anti-communist historian Anweiler writes:

“In some localities, as in Tambov… and Izhevsk in Vyatka province, the two parties [Mensheviks and right-SRs] even won a majority.” (Oskar Anweiler, The soviets : the Russian workers, peasants, and soldiers councils, 1905-1921, p. 229)

It seems his statement about Tambov is based only on the same bad source as Brovkin. Brovkin doesn’t discuss Izhevsk because his study focuses on province capitals, and Izhevsk is not one of them, but a smaller settlement. However, if we examine Izhevsk regardless, we find that Anweiler’s statement is not exactly true. Mensheviks and SRs did not win, instead most voted non-party, and the election result really might be considered invalid because only 20% voted. The situation in Izhevsk was rather exceptional.

According to his source in Izhevsk the number of workers largely diminished (by at least 25%) after October 1917. About half the workers were semi-proletarians from the countryside, and another half were semi-proletarians from the city. The countryside semi-proletarians didn’t own any property, while the local semi-proletarians were actually petit-bourgeois, owning land, forest and other property, also occupying a privileged position in their workplace. Due to migration the scale tipped in favor of the petit-bourgeois, but the Soviet was elected with only 20% of the workers voting and most voting for non-party. The communists said new elections should be carried out. Before that happened, White Guard revolts already broke out. (Soviets in the era of war communism vol 2., p. 423-424)

According to Right-SR writers who participated in the White government on the Volga:

In “Izhevsk… the workers… are a curious type of half-worker, half-peasant. Working at the factory, they at the same time continue to work own peasant farm in the village: or one member of the family works at the factory, and the rest work at land.” (Гражданская война на Волге в 1918 г, pp. 78-79)

Izhevsk really is a special case not representative of anything, and I will make a separate article about it at some later time.

So according to Anweiler, in some localities, but not all, and not even most, the Mensheviks and Right-SRs won elections. I would be completely willing to accept that in some places they probably did, but even in these two examples, the claim is proven either wrong or unverified.

Brovkin cites a Soviet book from the Brezhnev era which, according to him, states that in Tambov the Soviet was behind SR-Menshevik counter-revolutionary forces, but it doesn’t clearly state that the Mensheviks and SRs had a majority of deputies, and it doesn’t mention that the election result was called into question. That book is not inherently trustworthy, but it is the best proof for Menshevik support in Tambov. The book also states that Izhevsk was behind the counter-revolution, although we know most voters in Izhevsk didn’t exactly vote for the Mensheviks and Right-SRs.

According to capitalist historian John Keep, in Tambov (and Kaluga and some places in the Urals) the industry and the workers were not located in the town but in the outlying region. As a result the Bolshevik city committee was rather weak while the branches in the outlying areas were strong (John Keep, “October in the provinces” in Richard Pipes (ed.), Revolutionary Russia, pp. 183, 196). This would also explain why, as the Mensheviks claimed, the Bolsheviks easily won a strong majority in the Tambov Province Soviet and handed power to it, while the Tambov city Soviet was contested. Again we must ask, what good is a Soviet, if it leaves out the workers?

Brovkin himself says that the province capitals were often not very proletarian. But if that is so, what is the purpose of limiting his study to the province capitals? What is the purpose of examining Soviets (working class institutions) but not focusing on the areas with the most workers?

This topic was quite difficult to research, but I tried to look into what Keep says. According to bourgeois historians Dyatchkov and Kanitschev in Tambov “weak urban economic development meant that the towns remained as places for the officials and dependent strata, mainly consisting of the non-service gentry who lived off the rents from their estates or who were kept by their relatives. Together with numerous servants these non-productive strata made up the **majority of the urban population** of the Tambov region until the 1900s… There were, however, some 300 handicraft shops in Tambov in 1900-1918.” (Vladimir Dyatchkov and Valery Kanitschev, “Tambov regional development in the context of integral history, 1800-1917. Contradictions in the modernization of Russian society on a basis of microhistory”, p. 214)

“The regional urban economy was not able to absorb the extra rural population. The towns existed primarily as administrative-political and **not as economic centres**. The censuses of 1890 and 1917 show that the need was mainly for… servants, etc. A study of the Tambov social structure, based on the primary sources, showed that only a few hundred peasants found employment in the regional centre as craftsmen, traders or low ranking civil servants” (Dyatchkov & Kanitschev, p. 212)

This information strongly points to there being very few workers within the area of the Tambov city Soviet. Bourgeois historian Gorelov writes that there was really only one industrial work place in Tambov at the time, a railway wagon repairs shop:

“The only truly industrial facility in Tambov at that time was the wagon repair shops.” (А.А. ГОРЕЛОВ, ЭПОХА ЗАВОДСКИХ ГУДКОВ (о становлении индустриальной базы города Тамбова))

The largest factory in Tambov region was the Kotovsk gunpowder factory, a separate urban type settlement outside the city of Tambov. It was founded only in 1915 so naturally there was no developed working class culture or workers’ movement there. Other factories like the Znamenka sugar factory, which was much smaller, were also located in separate settlements outside of Tambov city.

According to Zemstvo statistics, in late 1800s and early 1900s there were 14,000 workers in the whole Tambov governorate, but only 2000 or less of them in Tambov city itself. There were 30,000 agricultural proletarians in the governorate working in the countryside. There were also mines e.g. in Morshansk, a 100 km outside of Tambov city, in which apparently mostly seasonal workers and semi-proletarians who are not included in the other figures worked. I don’t know the number of these mine workers but there were more than a 100,000 peasants in the Tambov governorate who participated in seasonal work (fishing, bee-keeping, handicrafts and mining). (Издания губернского земства: “Сборник статистических сведений по Тамбовской губернии” (труды статистического отделения при губернской управе, том I—XXIV, 1880-1900)). Obviously in the countryside the Bolsheviks relied also on support from poor peasants, and to a lesser extent from middle peasants, but that wasn’t the case in cities.

The total population of Tambov Gubernia was more than 3 millions at the time. It was predominantly an agricultural region and had an exceptionally high concentration of rich capitalist farmers:

“The peculiarity of socio-economic development in Tambov province was that in the first years of Soviet power there remained a high percentage of the wealthy peasantry, hostile to the state of the dictatorship of the proletariat. The average percentage of kulak farms in some counties in 1920 was up to 14 percent. It is not surprising that in the Tambov region for a long time there was a strong organization of the Social Revolutionary Party, based on the wealthy peasantry. And this had specific consequences for the political development of the province after October Revolution: all actions of the Soviet authorities in the villages met with hidden or blatant resistance of the Social Revolutionaries and that part of the peasantry who followed them.” (Игорь Петрович Донков, Антоновщина: замыслы и действительность, p. 16)

Just like Dyatchkov and Kanitschev said, there was only little industry and few workers, and the majority of the workers were really handicraftsmen, or working in small workshops instead of factories. The most important role was played by smaller enterprises processing agricultural goods:

“Level of industrial development in Tambov Gubernia was low. Of the 8 thousand enterprises [and this is in the whole Gubernia, not in the city], only 15 employed 500 or more people, the overwhelming majority of workers were employed in small enterprises, most of which were of an artisanal nature. The food industry played a dominant role: distillation, beet sugar and molasses factories, oil presses, mills, etc.

The state and location of industry determined and affected both the numerical and qualitative composition of the proletariat of the province. And besides, its insignificant numbers were sharply reduced due to military mobilizations and promotion to party and Soviet work, and the replenishments came from unconscious elements, wandering migrants from the village. This contributed to the strengthening of petit-bourgeois influence on the workers of Tambov Gubernia.” (Донков, pp. 16-17)

Of course Brovkin is a dishonest anti-communist who wants to support Menshevism no matter what, but also as a bourgeois historian, he doesn’t care what classes the various Soviets consisted of. He doesn’t care that capitalists and petit-bourgeois shopkeepers elected the Arkangelsk Soviet, and not the workers. He doesn’t care to examine which classes supported Menshevism and where the workers were located.

“Most central Russian provinces had Communist governments by the end of November 1917, but Tambov suffered from a weak, disorganized Bolshevik organization and strong SR representation” (Delano Dugarm, “Local politics and the struggle for grain in Tambov, 1918-21”, in Donald J. Raleigh (ed.) Provincial landscapes : local dimensions of Soviet power, 1917-1953, p. 63)

The Tambov party organization and Cheka were also infiltrated by class-enemies, corrupt people, a “gang of criminals” according to Delano Dugarm (p. 65). Several corrupt Cheka died resisting arrest by Bolshevik authorities.

“Local communist forces were weak. Class-alien elements penetrated the party ranks. Some village cells sometimes consisted entirely of former Social-Revolutionaries and anarchists, among whom were people who had joined the RCP(b) in order to carry out subversive work from within.” (Донков, p. 17)

According to the Menshevik newspaper, the election in Tambov was highly contested, and just like in Yaroslavl, there were all kinds of clashes before it.

Brovkin claims that the Bolsheviks were simply being tyrannical and all their actions were unjustified, and that the Mensheviks surely wouldn’t attempt any kind of White Guard uprising. By this time the Mensheviks had already supported the White General Kaledin’s and Krasnov’s coup attempts, supported the officer rebellion in Moscow, invited British invasion troops to Arkhangelsk and Murmansk, had joined in a huge uprising with the Czechoslovak troops backed by the Entente, and were waging violent agitation and riots everywhere. Without any shame he claims the Bolsheviks unjustifiably declared martial law in Tambov and then immediately states that a White Guard revolt immediately broke out.

In Tambov “On June 14, the Bolsheviks declared martial law and on June 17, an uprising broke out… The disbanded city duma was assembled, and some socialists were already celebrating an easy victory. However, real power in the city during these three days belonged not to the duma, but **rather to the White general Bogdanov [!!!] and his detachment of officers**.” (Brovkin, Menshevik comeback, p. 27)

The martial law was clearly a rational response to the White Guard plot which was being prepared, and which had already been launched in various other regions, and not any kind of unjustified tyranny.

“THE INSURRECTION AT TAMBOV [An Account in Pravda, June 22, 1918]

According to information received by the Extraordinary Commission to Fight Counter-Revolution, the unsuccessful attempt to overthrow Soviet rule in the city of Tambov was started and carried on under the direction of Socialist-Revolutionists of the Right. The lackeys of the bourgeoisie, crying at every crossroad about the submission of the Soviets to the Germans, everywhere and continually interfered with the creation of a powerful revolutionary Red Army. As soon as mobilization was called for, the counter-revolutionists started a widespread agitation against the Soviet. . . . Having assured themselves of the support of the petty-bourgeois and kulak elements, the betrayers of the working class risked making an attack on the Soviet. Kulaks were summoned from nearby villages to the aid of organized army officers. Almost the entire Soviet, together with prominent members of the Communist party, were unexpectedly arrested.

When the counter-revolutionists felt sure that their cause had won, they went completely mad: they shot the Commissar of Finance and two other Communists; the army officers donned glittering shoulder-straps and put on spurs [like in tsarist times].

At the head of the government stood the so-called “ Military Committee,” which included army officers and several kulaks. This committee issued the following proclamation:

“The reign of the usurpers and robbers, who tore Russia asunder and betrayed her to the Germans, has come to an end. A new government, honest but strong, has been organized.

“Since the fight against the Bolsheviks demands that our forces be united, full power is vested in the Military Committee of Officers and Soldiers.

“All opposition to and agitation against the Military Committee will be punished with the death penalty.” (James Bunyan, Intervention, civil war, and communism in Russia, April-December, 1918 : documents and materials, pp. 163-164)

**KOSTROMA**

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/kostroma-kremlin.jpg?w=852)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/kostroma-kremlin.jpg)

In Kostroma White Guard conspirators arrived and began preparing an uprising. As in many other places, they infiltrated the state apparatus first:

“Soligalich [in Kostroma] served as a gathering place for active officials of the tsarist police. Sometimes with fake passports, they came to the city and made their way [infiltrated] into the district office: the post office and telegraph office, the city and zemstvo administration, and even the police. At the head of the impending uprising was the tsarist officer Drobyshev, a faithful assistant to the famous Yalta Black Hundreds, General Dumbadze.” (V. A. Neumark, The formative years of the Kostroma police)

“The core of the underground organization in Kostroma consisted of officers of the former Pultus regiment. Salaried officials, high school students, and technical school students\* also took part in the plot. It was headed by Colonel Zhadovsky, the cash office was managed by a former attorney at law, leader of the Kostroma cadets N.A. Ogorodnikov. Retired General Durnovo played a prominent role in the organization… [the center of the plotters] rented premises in house No. 18 on Rusina Street… which belonged to a large merchant and the owner of a confectionery workshop F.I. Borovsky. The activities of the organization were directed from the center: instructions and money came from there. There was a connection with the officers of Yaroslavl and personally with Savinkov, who was in Rybinsk.” (M.A. Lapshina, “Civil war in Kostroma province (1918–1919)”)

**\*in this time period usually only relatively rich people, bourgeois and aristocrats had the ability to become students**

Officers involved in this conspiracy were arrested, but due to a government amnesty they were released. “Many officers later served in the Red Army, some ended up in the opposite camp – with the Whites.” (Lapshina)

Before the uprising the Whites provoked violent riots and pogroms to escalate the situation. The reactionary character of many of these riots and uprisings is demonstrated by the fact that even the Mensheviks, and even SRs were killed by the rebels and the Soviets abolished, despite the Mensheviks and SRs helping to organize them:

“In most of these smaller towns, the Mensheviks were swept aside by the tide of violence… In Belyi, all the members of the soviet were killed [not only Bolsheviks]. In Soligalich [Kostroma region], Pavlovskii Posad [Moscow region], and other towns as well, the soviet was locked and burned with everyone in it.” (Brovkin, Menshevik comeback, p. 45)

According to Brovkin the rioting mob in Pavlovskii Posad shouted “Down with the soviets!”.

“In Kostroma the mob cried: “Down with the commissars! It used to be better under the Tsar!” Rebellions of this kind were reported in Orekhovo-Zuevo, Ivanovo-Voznesensk, Vologda, Rybinsk, and other towns.” (Brovkin, Menshevik comeback, p. 46)

It is not a coincidence these clearly black-hundred rebellions took place in exactly those places decided by the imperialists. Is it likely that these movements supported Menshevism? Probably they didn’t support any kind of socialism, at most they might have wanted to temporarily use Menshevism as the most right-wing party, as a weapon against socialism, until socialism could be overthrown altogether.

In this example you can clearly see how the White Guard officers from Savinkov’s organization together with the local clergy spread lies to the peasants, got them enraged and tried to get them to attack the communists. The Whites relied on the most backward and ignorant segments of the rural population, who were fooled into believing their lies. They claimed the communists were going to remove crosses from graves, and demanded the disarming of the Red Guard, even though Soligalich did not even have a Red Guard:

In Kostroma “In Soligalich, at the beginning of February, the local intelligentsia, merchants, officers and clergy launched widespread anti-Soviet agitation…

There was talk in the city that Soviet power was leading to the collapse of Russia, to the death of the Christian religion, and that the district council, headed by Vyluzgin, had already decided to requisition all church and monastery property, remove crosses and fences from graves in order to turn them into agricultural tools. On February 26, an angry crowd led by officers and members of the Union of the Russian People [Black Hundreds], with the support of the clergy, primarily the monastery, moved to the Ukom building with a demand to cancel the requisition of grain and disarm the Red Guard, which, by the way, had not yet been organized in Soligalich. The Chairman of the Council of Vyluzgin, coming out to meet the crowd, fired a warning shot into the air and ordered them to disperse. However, the rebels wounded and disarmed him, and most of the members of the executive committee were beaten and arrested.” (Neumark)

“At the same time, a message was received about a counter-revolutionary rebellion in the city of Kologriv… The inexperience of the party activists, members of the newly elected Council, and the confusion of the Red Guards in this situation seriously affected the state of affairs in the district… the Kologriv Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, with the support of the local intelligentsia, officers, high school students and former gendarmes, disarmed the Red Guard detachment in the barracks, seized the armory, arrested and beat members of the district Soviet.

Detachments of working Red Guards from Kostroma and Galich Buy came to the aid of the Kologriv Bolsheviks, who eliminated the unrest in the district without bloodshed.” (Neumark)

“in the province in May-June 1918. The Central Committee of the Socialist Revolutionary Party sends provocateurs to Kostroma to organize subversive work, who establish contact with local Mensheviks and right-wing Socialist Revolutionaries. To join forces in May, they entered into an alliance with the illegal “Society for the Salvation of Russia,” which was in constant contact with similar organizations in many cities of central Russia. At the beginning of June, they provoked the kulaks of the Kostroma district to a “Peasant March” under the slogans of “free trade and the abolition of the grain monopoly.”…

But the situation was complicated by the fact that in the spring and summer in several volosts and districts the Soviets were led by village rich people under the guise of being left Socialist Revolutionaries. Thus, in the village of Shunga, Kostroma district, the kulaks dispersed the volost Soviet and elected a headman instead. On May 23, kulaks and right-wing Socialist Revolutionaries, under the slogans “Down with Soviet power and the grain monopoly,” tried to organize a rally in the central square of Kostroma, also demanding the disarmament of workers’ squads and city police.

On May 26, the workers of the fighting squads of the Kostroma factories, the apparatus of the provincial and city police, having heard the message of N. K. Kozlov and G. A. Burichenkov about the counter-revolutionary uprising in Kostroma, vowed as one to stand up for the defense of Soviet power and demanded that the Kostroma military revolutionary committee for the arrest of the Menshevik leader A. Dyakonov.

In mid-June 1918, Kostroma found itself at the center of events organized by the leader of the right Socialist Revolutionaries Boris Savinkov with the support of the Anglo-French imperialists. According to their plan, an Anglo-French landing force was landed in Arkhangelsk. The Left Socialist Revolutionaries wanted to support this invasion with an armed uprising in order to occupy the upper Volga with the help of a French landing. Thus, from the upper Volga the road was opened for an invasion of Moscow…

Shortly before the Yaroslavl uprising, anti-Soviet officers began to arrive in Kostroma. On the streets, squares and restaurants they behaved extremely defiantly, without hiding their hostility to Soviet power.” As a result of the [Yaroslavl events](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/2024/06/11/menshevik-attempt-to-overthrow-bolshevism-ep-2-the-yaroslavl-uprising/), martial law was declared in Kostroma (Neumark)

“The autumn of 1918 was especially alarming in Kostroma, when kulak uprisings broke out in… Urensky region… In this most prosperous region in the east of the Kostroma province, kulaks and officers resisted food detachments and incited the population to mass riots on the occasion of grain accounting.

Mistakes by the district and volost executive committees aggravated the situation… the bribed commission… did not take any measures to combat grain speculation, which in the summer of 1918 reached unprecedented proportions, or against moonshine.

Finally, the volost Soviet took away grain indiscriminately from the middle peasants and kulaks, without observing the norms, without organizing a record of it when sending it to the district. As it turned out later, the chairman of the Urensky executive committee, Rekhalov, and the volost commissar, Lebedev, made crude threats and assault, turning grain accounting into ordinary robbery… These mistakes caused the spread of anti-Soviet sentiments and plunged the broad peasant masses into a counter-revolutionary uprising. True, some villages, even in these conditions, joined the counter-revolution only after punitive expeditions of the rebels [forced them to]… the right-bank volosts refused to join the rebels”

“On August 26, a group of White Guard officers came to the leaders of the uprising. Together they killed the surviving members of the Soviet… and their families… Subsequently, they decided to cut the railway at the Sharya station and move their forces to the southeast, to the Kazan region, to connect with the White Czechs.” (Neumark)

In Kostroma’s Makaryevski district, Bolshevik commissar Galkin called a village meeting. The rebels arrested him but “Galkin, seeing that among the rebels the majority of the peasants were deceived by their kulaks, explained to them the senselessness of counter-revolutionary actions and warned that if they did not voluntarily lay down their arms, they would still be defeated. Despite the threats of their leaders, the peasants put the question of further resistance to a vote and, by a majority vote, ended the uprising and freed the commissar and his assistants.” (Neumark)

“On May 23, at a mass rally, a Menshevik speaker blamed the Bolshevik ExCom for the breakdown of the provisionment and supply mechanism. Famine was no longer a threat, it was a reality. Bread riots flared up in the neighboring towns.” (Brovkin, Menshevik comeback, p. 8)

Right-SR propaganda claimed that the October Revolution “did not give either bread or peace” (M.A. Lapshina) and tried to raise a revolt among the peasants under these demagogic slanders. Their dishonesty is blatant. Notice that the accusation doesn’t say ‘the socialist revolution didn’t give land’, because it was known to everyone that the revolution did give land. The land together with the peace were supposed to provide bread. But due to the continuation of the war and the devastation caused by it, there were still huge food problems. But whose fault was it, that the war still continued? The right-wing forces were the ones who launched a civil war and asked foreign armies to help them! And while the right-SRs simultaneously blamed the Bolsheviks for war, they also attacked the Bolsheviks for signing the peace with Germany, which ended Russia’s involvement in the World War.

The Bolsheviks had delivered land. They had ended the country’s participation in the war. It was not their fault the foreign imperialists invaded them on behalf of white generals, who launched the foreign interventionist civil war. And further, the Mensheviks and SRs had refused to give land, had been unable to solve the food problem, and had wanted to keep fighting the World War “to a victorious finish”. The problems would’ve been equally bad, or even worse, under the Mensheviks and Right-SRs.
