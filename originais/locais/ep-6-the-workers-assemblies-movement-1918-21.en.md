# Menshevik attempt to overthrow Bolshevism: Ep.6 – “The Workers’ Assemblies” movement (1918-21)

**FREEDOM OR REPRESSION?**

I didn’t have space in the previous article to discuss this issue, so I will include it here.

According to the Mensheviks and Right-SRs, the Bolsheviks were persecuting all freedom, and anti-communist hacks claim that the popularity of anti-Bolsheviks would’ve been evident, if it wasn’t for the “tyranny”. However, anti-communist philosopher Russell said about his visit to Russia: “We were all allowed complete freedom to see politicians of opposition parties, and we naturally made full use of this freedom. We saw Mensheviks, Social Revolutionaries of different groups, and Anarchists ; we saw them without the presence of any Bolsheviks” (Bertrand Russell, The practice and theory of Bolshevism, p. 26)

“In May 1920 members of the British Labour delegation visiting Moscow “were allowed complete freedom to see politicians of opposition parties”, and even attended a session of the Menshevik central committee.” (E. H. Carr, The Bolshevik Revolution 1917-1923 Vol. 1, Norton, 1985, pp. 174-175)

The capitalist editor of V. Serge’s *Year One of the Russian Revolution* writes that: “the pitiful showing made by the non-Bolshevik but ‘Sovietist’ organizations… [in Soviet elections] is probably due not simply to police action” (Editor’s introduction in V. Serge, Year one, p. 7)

Landsbury, a pacifist social-democrat and a friend of anarchist prince Kropotkin, traveled around Soviet-Russia with anarchists Emma Goldman and Alec Berkman as his translators, and also spoke with American anarchist workers at a co-operative they owned. He visited many factories and said:

“While going round these factories we were informed that the day before the election of representatives to the Moscow Soviet had taken place. I enquired if it was true that only communists could be elected and that terrorism was used to prevent the free exercise of the vote. No one I asked had ever seen any terrorism and everyone assured me that irrespective of opinion any person qualified to vote might be elected.” (George Lansbury, What I saw in Russia, p. 84)

H. G. Wells tells his experience of the Petrograd Soviet. First he saw Zinoviev argue that peace should be signed with Poland. Then spoke “an oldish man who made a bitter attack upon the irreligion of the people and government of Russia ; Russia was suffering for her sins, and until she repented and returned to religion she would continue to suffer one disaster after another. His opinions were not those of the meeting, but he was allowed to have his say without interruption. The decision to make peace with Poland was then taken by a show of hands.” (H. G. Wells, Russia in the shadows, p. 116)

How did the Mensheviks, Right-SRs and capitalists use their freedom?

Until July 1918 “enemies of the regime enjoy free speech with a more than parliamentary latitude. With monotonous enthusiasm they celebrate the praises of the Constituent Assembly… ‘The autocracy of the commissars has within six months produced the total ruin of Russia, laid waste by German imperialism,’ cries a Right SR, who demands the Constituent Assembly, the cancellation of the Brest-Litovsk treaty and re-entry into the war on the side of the Allies. The lawyer Kogan-Bernstein (a Right SR) shouts at the Bolsheviks: ‘Get out before you are chased out! You are only in power thanks to bayonets!’ He denounces ‘the October counter-revolution’ and ‘Lenin the insensible’… ‘You are savages, madmen, bandits!’ screamed another, at the end of the session of 14 May, at Sverdlov’s imperturbable face.” (V. Serge, Year one, p. 243) In July they are expelled from the CEC due to their support for the Yaroslavl white guard rebellion.

Menshevik writer Denicke says that in the Soviet congresses and meetings: “Menshevik leaders were allowed to speak freely, to criticize the government in the sharpest way… The chairman of the CEC, Sverdlov, was an unimpeachably “correct” parliamentarian. Once, it is true, he made Martov leave the hall (25 April), but then Martov had disregarded several calls to order, and Sverdlov did what any chairman of a democratic parliament would have done… For several months the Mensheviks were able to use the CEC as a tribune for their antigovernment views.” (George Denicke, in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 136)

In the Soviet Central Executive Committee Left-SR leader “Maria Spiridonova now attacks the Bolsheviks with a passion bordering on hysteria. She speaks of the martyred and betrayed Ukraine; accuses the‘ Bolshevik usurpers’ of ruining the peasantry, of sending the Germans secret trains loaded with gold, of being in the service of Germany. Lenin shakes his head as he listens. His rebuttals, which are sometimes drowned in interruptions, are notable for their common-sense. ‘A party which drives its sincerest representatives to wallow in this mire of lies and errors is a doomed party.’” (V. Serge, Year one, p. 260)

“On March 12, the 4th All-Russian Congress of Soviets opened in Moscow. This congress was to have the final say on war and peace. As soon as the congress opened, Martov made the following statement:

“This congress is deciding the question of the first partition of Russia and the sale of the Russian revolution to German imperialism… Remember, nothing will ever save or allow any of those who will take part in deciding this question here in this hall to escape. Years, decades will pass, but the international people’s proletariat will remember you with a curse.”

Martov made this menacing philippic in connection with the demand for the admission of Menshevik representatives to the… credentials committee of the congress. Martov argued that the congress, which was supposed to decide the peace question, “assembled in the dark.” The credentials committee was composed on the basis of proportionality. Levin entered it from the Mensheviks. But he, of course, could not detect any signs of “rigging” the congress.” (В. А. Быстрянский, Меньшевики и эсеры в русской)

The SRs and Mensheviks made violent threats and outrageous slanders and lies in the Soviet Central Executive Committee, but they were not persecuted for it. They also spread all kinds of other lies as much as they could in order to enrage the public.

Citing the British Government White Book The Liberator (Issue No. 16, June, 1919) tells of the following lies invented by Mensheviks and SRs: “On July 19, a delegation of working women came to the Extraordinary Commission [Cheka]. All were dressed in mourning . . . One of them, weeping hysterically, begged that the body of her husband be given her for burial…

The husband of this woman had been arrested a few days before, during investigation of alleged counter-revolutionary activities of a strike-committee of which he was a member. He was found to be alive and in good health.” Asked how she concluded her husband must be dead “she said that a Menshevik agitator had told her so. The other women made similar statements.”

Worker delegations came asking the Cheka if there was “truth in the rumors about the mass-shooting of workingmen. They had heard of it from Menshevik and Right Social Revolutionary agitators.”

An example of a bourgeois paper banned by the Bolsheviks: “the “Evening Life” of Moscow was suspended forever, and fined 25,000 rubles, ” for printing an invented decree regarding the socialization of women.”” (The Liberator, Issue No. 16, June, 1919)

The Mensheviks and Right-SRs were excluded from Soviet executive committees and their newspapers were suppressed, only after they organized the Yaroslavl White Guard uprising and other similar attacks in July 1918. They were not suppressed yet for the Menshevik and SR participation in all the earlier counter-revolutionary plots, but in some cases met with only local restrictions.

Could any other government be expected to show as much tolerance as the Bolsheviks? In Georgia, where the Mensheviks were in control, the Mensheviks themselves had already completely outlawed the Bolshevik party months before. And yet the Mensheviks hypocritically pretended like they were being treated unfairly.

After a small section of the Menshevik party, including Martov and Dan, claimed that they do not agree with allying with the White Guards (although we know they were not being fully truthful), they were allowed to continue operating legally, until eventually they got involved in more counter-revolutionary intrigues.

Brovkin says that the Menshevik party Right (which supported armed counter-revolution) and the Menshevik party Left (of Martov) were able to unite due to Martov’s move further Right (Mensheviks after October, p. 200).

The Left-SR party was banned only after they launched an armed coup attempt against the Bolsheviks. Those Left-SRs who denounced the coup were allowed to stay in Soviets and operate legally. They changed their name to narodnik communists or revolutionary communists. A section of the Right-SRs, the centrist faction of Volsky, also denounced allying with the White Guards, and they were allowed to operate without restrictions by the Bolsheviks.

The Mensheviks, SRs and their capitalist allies never showed the same level of toleration for Bolshevism, murdering them en masse, totally outlawing the Bolsheviks everywhere that they came to power.

The daughter of Menshevik parents, Vera Broido, writes that: “Executions of Mensheviks were rare.” (Vera Broido, Lenin and the Mensheviks: The Persecution of Socialists under Bolshevism, p. 88) On the other hand, the Mensheviks and their capitalist allies carried out several massacres and mass executions of Bolsheviks.

Its actually quite funny that the anti-communists always claim the Bolsheviks came to power in an unpopular plot, but the anti-Bolshevik forces themselves used practically only that method:

“the Left and Right SRs and the anarchists (not to speak of the monarchists and liberal-capitalists) conducted futile putsches, or preparations for putsches, without the slightest popular backing.” (Editor’s introduction in V. Serge, Year one of the Russian Revolution, Chicago, Holt, Rinehart, and Winston, 1972, p. 14)

And of course such plots always failed, because one cannot come to power like that. The Right-SR officer revolt, and the Left-SR coup, failed miserably.

Menshevik claims about persecution are even less credible because even in emigration they accused each other of being ‘just as bad’ as the Bolsheviks:

One Menshevik “caustically rebuked [Menshevik] Dan and his supporters in 1925: “At bottom, this is a continuing heritage of Bolshevik methods… Opposition within the party is something impermissible … it must always remain silent.’” (Abraham Ascher, The Mensheviks in the Russian Revolution, p. 38)

“Iurii Denike has spoken of a purge and of a police regime in the [Menshevik] party, and in 1920 the Central Committee undertook a reregistration of party members, never fully carried out, to exclude automatically those party members who disagreed with the controversial new party platform.’ Petr Garvi had ample grounds to complain that “the organizational bolshevization of the party took place under war communism.”” (André Liebich, From the other shore: Russian social democracy after 1921, p. 80)

**WORKER’S ASSEMBLIES AND THE “NON-PARTYISTS” (1918-21)**

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/22740.jpg?w=1000)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/22740.jpg)

Now let’s get to the main topic of this post. I want to discuss the largest working class opposition movement in Bolshevik Russia, the “Workers’ Assemblies”.

Anti-communist historians (especially V. Brovkin) claim the assemblies were massive, very anti-Bolshevik, and posed a serious threat to the Bolsheviks. In reality the worker assemblies were actually never a large movement, never posed a serious challenge to the Bolsheviks, and in fact, the movement wasn’t consistently anti-Bolshevik. The Mensheviks tried to recruit labor aristocrats, criminals, black-hundreds and the least conscious workers into the movement.

However, many people in the movement supported Bolshevism and only criticized certain policies, hoping that they could help the Bolshevik government by pushing for certain changes. Furthermore, the Mensheviks and Right-SRs always characterized the assembly movement as non-party, because they knew workers wouldn’t support the Menshevik party. The Mensheviks also presented themselves as simply “better Bolsheviks”, claiming they support the same goals, but only want to suggest better ways of reaching those goals.

The Mensheviks’ real goals were abolishing the Soviets and replacing them with a Constituent Assembly, dismantling socialism and setting up a capitalist state, re-joining the world war, and the Right-SRs even said the land-reform would have to be rolled back, and that it would take 20 years to implement any land-reform in Russia. The workers would never have supported such goals, so the Mensheviks and Right-SRs had to claim they supported socialism and Soviet power.

When the Mensheviks lost all support in the Soviets in late 1917-early 1918 they tried to create a competing movement of “assembly of shop delegates as an organization of non-party workers… The workers boycotted the assembly of shop delegates and as a result the assembly, according to its own admission, “represented only a small section of the Petrograd workers”.” (International press correspondence, Vol. 2, No. 50, June 17, 1922)

Anti-communist Rabinowitch writes that the worker assembly project which tried to compete with the Soviets, and which the Mensheviks tried unsuccessfully to use for their purposes, was strengthened significantly by “the massive move of Bolsheviks from factory to military and government work” (Alexander Rabinowitch, “Early disenchantment with Bolshevik rule: new data from the archives of the extraordinary assembly of delegates from Petrograd factories”, Politics and society under the Bolsheviks: selected papers from the Fifth World Congress of Central and East European Studies, Warsaw, 1995, p. 39)

Rabinowitch actually says the worker assembly movement consisted of people who were dissatisfied with all parties and intellectuals, i.e. seemingly the most backward unorganized people. In their meetings they complained about spreading anti-semitism among their supporters (p. 40). Of course the Mensheviks tried to utilize such people.

According to Figes, the Worker Assemblies did not support Menshevism or the Right-SRs, though the Mensheviks and Right-SRs participated in them as actively as possible. Most meetings of the Assemblies expressed that they agreed with the Bolshevik goals, but were upset that they had not been fulfilled:

“the thing that exercised the workers most was a general feeling that the promise of a ‘workers’ revolution’ — a promise that had led many of them to support the Bolsheviks in the autumn of 1917 — had not been fulfilled. As the striking workers of the Sormovo factory declared in June: “The Soviet regime… promised to bring the workers Socialism but has brought them empty factories and destitution.’” (Orlando Figes, A people’s tragedy : the Russian Revolution 1891-1924, London : Pimlico, 1997, p. 625)

These workers simply did not understand that the goal wouldn’t be achieved so quickly. Carrying out the revolution and socialist construction turned out to be much more difficult and protracted, but it was done. Thus even the complaints of these workers were fully satisfied by the Bolsheviks.

Figes also claims the Mensheviks won Soviet elections in some places, but he provides no source for this claim. He writes that Brovkin claims workers who made complaints about the Bolshevik government consciously supported Menshevism, but according to Figes the opposite view, that those workers didn’t support any party, is “more persuasive”. (Figes, A people’s tragedy, p. 852 footnote 62)

One Menshevik described the sentiment of these workers as “A curse on you all—Bolsheviks [and] Mensheviks—with all your politics.” (Brovkin, The Mensheviks after October, p. 166)

The non-party workers often said: “We don’t care who has the political power, just give us bread.” (Sergei Pavliuchenkov, “Protest Movement Against War Communism” in Brovkin (ed.) The Bolsheviks in Russian society, p. 145)

“the largest number of [worker assembly] “delegates”… came from… plants formerly engaged in defense production, including seven that had been entirely shut down. The only other significant representation came from 16 typesetting and print shops, strongholds of Menshevism.” (William G. Rosenberg, “Russian Labor and Bolshevik Power after October”, Slavic Review Vol. 44, No. 2 (Summer, 1985), p. 231)

“those most active in the movement seem to have been preponderantly semiskilled metal workers, who, despite their strong protests in the spring of 1918, had long had close ties to the Bolsheviks. Probably their political loyalties still lay in that direction. Their dissidence seems to have been directed more toward obtaining effective relief than in support of the Mensheviks or other opposition parties; and votes of protest are never the same as a commitment to the opposition.” (William G. Rosenberg, “Russian Labor and Bolshevik Power after October”, pp. 237-238)

Rosenberg also states that even those workers who protested, still understood that stricter methods of discipline were necessary in the current conditions and that economic hardship was to an extent unavoidable, and thus still put their hope in the Bolshevik state and believed it would solve these issues, and that protest and criticism would only hasten it (p. 238).

“Bolshevik newspapers throughout the country covered the assembly’s proceedings fairly accurately… Here, for example, is the report on the session of June 1 published by the Saratov Izvestiia:

“The parties of the social traitors have decided to use the food-supply crisis for their own ends. By demagogical agitation, the Mensheviks and SRs have managed to arouse backward elements of the Petrograd proletariat and to create a provocateur organization parallel to the soviet, the so-called Assembly of Upolnomochennye of the Factories and Plants, which was not in fact elected by anyone. Here open propaganda is going on for a political strike directed at the overthrow of soviet power. At the meeting on June 1, the right SRs were for an immediate strike and the Mensheviks against it, because it was clear that an immediate strike would not be successful.”” (Brovkin, The Mensheviks after October, p. 195)

The Workers’ Assemblies tried to organize a one-day protest strike, but even that failed, and the whole movement dissipated: “According to official Bolshevik sources, the “yellow strike” on July 2 failed: only a “bunch of counterrevolutionaries” participated.” The non-Bolshevik papers… also spoke of the strike’s failure.” (Brovkin, The Mensheviks after October, pp. 247-248)

Menshevik Yastrebov said that the Menshevik-SR bloc and the ‘worker assemblies’ “did not represent anything serious in the sense of a broad mass movement.” (В. А. Быстрянский, Меньшевики и эсеры в русской, p. 144). The Mensheviks who tried to organize the ‘assembly’ movement received money from the PBO, Petrograd monarchist combat organization (PBO in Russian, PCO in English), the main secret organization of the capitalists. Menshevik leader Dan mentions the offers of money from White Guard circles in his book, but denies accepting it.

“In Petrograd the remnants of the SRs, Mensheviks and various anarchists banded together… [and] collaborated with the newly formed monarchist Petrograd Combat Organization (PCO), as the PCO itself asserted (PCO Report to Helsinki Department of National Center, no earlier than 28 March 1921; reprinted in Kronstadt Tragedy). The [monarchist-capitalist] PCO even printed the Mensheviks’ leaflets! On March 14… [they] issued a leaflet in solidarity with Kronstadt that said not one word about socialism or soviets, but instead called for an uprising against “the bloody communist regime” in the name of “all power to the people” (“Appeal to All Citizens, Workers, Red Army Soldiers and Sailors,” 14 March 1921; reprinted in Kronstadt Tragedy).” (Kronstadt 1921: Bolshevism vs. Counterrevolution)

Menshevik proclamations were printed by the PBO and these Mensheviks also sent their greetings to the Sotsialisticheskii Vestnik, thus showing they were “official Mensheviks”, not ultra-rightists (Быстрянский, p. 149)

“Here is a remarkable message we find in Dan’s book “Two Years of Searching” published in Berlin: “After my arrest, our organization received an invitation to send its representatives to the “meeting of authorized representatives of the Petrograd factories and plants.” They decided to send one comrade to get acquainted. It turned out that the “meeting” was a pure bluff. There were no authorized representatives, but there were individuals who called themselves Plekhanovites, Left Socialist-Revolutionaries, anarchists, etc., who were definitely not elected by anyone.” (Быстрянский, p. 146)

“little is known of the conduct of these [worker assembly] elections.”   
(Scott B. Smith, Captives of revolution : the socialist revolutionaries and the Bolshevik dictatorship, 1918-1923, p. 18). As Dan and even Brovkin’s source states, they were not elected by anyone.

At the 1918 assembly the workers largely complained about the evacuation due to the German threat. Some said the evacuation was too chaotic, others that it was too strict, many were scared and wanted to be evacuated soon. This is the main complaint that Smith notes (Smith, Captives of revolution, p. 20).

In 1921 the non-party workers movement demanded the equalization of rations, essentially NEP policies, but also attacked bourgeois specialists. They were not really anti-communist:

“The egalitarian workerists [non-partyists] were the Bolsheviks’ natural constituency, and had supported them in the October revolution and the civil war. At least one of them, Mosolov from the Ustinsky works, was close enough to Bolshevism to regard cleansing its ranks of bourgeois elements… as a central task:… It is communist ideas that we are for; we will throw the rubbish out [of the party]’. The affinity between some aspects of Bolshevism and the non-party egalitarians was also evident in a session on the trade union question then being debated within the party.” (Simon Pirani, “The Moscow workers’ movement in 1921 and the role of non-partyism”, Europe-Asia Studies, Volume 56, 2004 – Issue 1, p. 148)

However, apparently in Moscow the high-point of opposition to Bolshevism among workers in 1921 was a strike by “relatively privileged workers… protesting at their loss of privileges.” (Pirani, p. 149). Their privileged ration had been equalized, just like demanded by many non-partyists.

“historians, relying on the memoirs of the SR S. S. Maslov, wrote that… a special security detachment  
intervened [to stop the strike], killing and wounding several people. Archives now available throw doubt on this.” (Pirani, p. 149)

According to Pirani the cause of the strikes nearly everywhere was the same, bad economic conditions. He notes only very few instances of political statements, especially among non-Bolsheviks, and even the few Menshevik resolutions passed (such as by chemical workers) demanded the collaboration of all socialists for better economic conditions.

“Moscow workers were extremely angry about the supply crisis, but divided as to its causes” (Pirani, p. 149)

“The evidence available suggests that, politically, those who voted for non-partyist candidates were first and foremost striving towards some sort of soviet rule” (Pirani, p. 151)

Non-partyist workers “do not trust the Mensheviks and SR”, instead of communists they “voted for non-party people “on a trial basis”” (Pirani, p. 151)

“Sotsialisticheskii Vestnik, the Menshevik newspaper published in Berlin, argues that the threat of repression… dissuaded workers from voting for Menshevik–SR candidates or resolutions, and that non-partyism was essentially used as a cover. But there is significant evidence to the contrary.” (Pirani, p. 154)

A leading non-partyist “(who joined the Bolsheviks in 1924) said in 1920-21 he had been a non-partyist on principle… Contemporaneous records show that his speeches to the February Moscow metalworkers meeting exuded egalitarian workerism: there he said that the Russian economic crisis was due to the failure of western European workers to follow the Bolsheviks’ revolutionary path; he expressed support for the fight inside the Bolshevik party against ‘bourgeois elements’ and implicitly accused the Moscow party leader, Kamenev, of petty corruption” (Pirani, p. 154)

“The workers’ protests over supply in Moscow in February 1921 were not united or directed toward overthrowing the Bolsheviks.” (Pirani, p. 155)
