**THE BOLSHEVIKS AND GERMANY**

The Mensheviks and SRs used deceitful demagogic arguments to try to win popularity especially in areas near the front where German troops were (Murmansk, Tambov, Georgia) claiming that Bolsheviks were traitors to their homeland, that they were German agents etc. Historians agree that at one point most people in Russia and abroad believed this lie, although nowadays it has largely been abandoned, though some anti-communists still try to resurrect it.

I’m not going to go deeply into the topic of the “German-Bolshevik conspiracy” here, I will write about it later. However, I will say that definitely the biggest and nearly only reasons why people believed in the claim that Bolsheviks were German agents, were 1) forged documents created by White Guards, (which have been debunked even by bourgeois historians such as George Kennan) and even more importantly 2) that Lenin arrived from Germany in the so-called “sealed train”, meaning a special train which didn’t let him meet anyone and didn’t let him get out during the trip. This train deported him from Germany to Russia.

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/forged-german-bolshevik-document.jpg?w=618)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/forged-german-bolshevik-document.jpg)

***Forged German-Bolshevik documents***

I’m not going to discuss this in detail here, but one should keep in mind that Lenin was not the only one to arrive on a “sealed train” from Germany. His rival, Menshevik Martov, also arrived on the same kind of train. So did Chernov, the leader of the Right-SRs and a minister in Kerensky’s government – the government which accused Lenin of being a German agent, because he was sent on a German train!

Once the Bolsheviks had overthrown the capitalist government of Russia, they tried to help the German workers in overthrowing the German Kaiser. The Mensheviks and SRs accused Lenin of being a German agent because he signed a peace treaty with Germany, but before signing the treaty, Lenin had made a serious examination of the conditions of the troops. Based on this examination he had concluded that the army could not resist the German attack, so there was no choice but to make the peace.

However, Lenin still did not trust the Germans, and called on the people to wage a heroic defensive war if Germany were to attack. Lenin also launched the creation of the Red Army, so that it would be ready as soon as possible to face any attacker. (see e.g. Lenin, [“Questions To Delegates To The Army Congress On The Demobilisation Of The Army”](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/dec/17a.htm) and Lenin, [“The Socialist Fatherland is in Danger!”](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1918/feb/21b.htm))

But what were the relations of the Mensheviks, SRs and other counter-revolutionaries to the Germans

**Going on the “sealed train” was apparently actually Menshevik leader Martov’s idea:** “Martov… was apparently the first to broach the idea of a deal with the German authorities to allow passages through Germany in exchange for the repatriation of German prisoners of war held in Russia.” (Israel Getzler, Martov: a political biography of a Russian social democrat, p. 147)

Yet, Martov still said Lenin **“is not only under the protection of the Red Guards, but also of Kaiser Wilhelm.”** (Getzler, p. 192)

The Mensheviks and SRs strongly condemned the peace treaty made by the Bolsheviks and screamed that “the Bolsheviks have surrendered Ukraine and the Baltics to the Germans”. But in Ukraine and the Baltics as well as Georgia, the Mensheviks collaborated with the German occupiers.

The Mensheviks and SRs supported the White forces in the civil war, while White Generals Krasnov and Alekseyev were armed and funded by the Germans. The Germans and the Entente actually had an implicit agreement to collaborate against the Bolsheviks. Even after Germany had been defeated by the Entente, the British told the German army to remain in the Baltic countries which they had occupied, to repress the communists and workers.

“It is true the Allies did not dare to admit openly that they were ready to use Germans in the task they had now set before them. But it is a fact that the French commander, General Anselm, who arrived in Odessa soon after the armistice with the Central Powers, proceeded to enter into relations with all anti-Bolshevik parties that he found there. Among them was the “gromada,” a pro-German group, which had been formed during the German occupation. Through this group General Anselm arranged that the German troops in the Ukraine should hold the front against the Red Army, until other forces, either Allied or local Russian Whites, could be formed to take it over. That the Allies were at this time using the armed forces of the Central Powers against the Red Army is also proved by the instructions issued to the British naval forces in the Baltic.” (M. Philips Price, My reminiscences of the Russian revolution, p. 356)

“In South-East Russia the Allies appear to have gone even further. Allied commanders of small naval and military detachments, which arrived there from the Turkish war theatre in November, concluded a sort of joint agreement with the local German commander. On November 16th the Neue Nackrichten, organ of the German forces of occupation in the Ukraine at Berdiansk, printed a manifesto of the Allied command in South Russia. It ran as follows : ” We inform hereby the inhabitants of Odessa and the neighbourhood that we have arrived on Russian territory with the object of restoring order and of freeing the country from the Bolshevik usurpers. Both the Germans and ourselves have come here not as conquerors, but as champions of right. Hence their objects and ours are identical” (Price, Ibid., p. 357)

British agent Lockhart writes:

“The one aim of every Russian bourgeois (and 99 per cent of the so-called “loyal” Russians were bourgeois) was to secure the intervention of British troops (and, failing British, German troops) to re-establish order in Russia, suppress Bolshevism and restore to the bourgeois his property.” (Bruce Lockhart, Memoirs of a British agent, The Shenval press, 1934 p. 213)

The plan of many tsarist capitalists and also of the Menshevik-SR government of Kerensky, had been to abandon Petrograd to the Germans. This failed because the masses said they would arrest Kerensky if he tried to move the capital to Moscow. However, the idea that Germans should be allowed to invade so that they would destroy the revolution was almost universal among the capitalists and reactionaries, despite the fact that they accused the Bolsheviks of being German agents:

“Reaction is open and arrogant. Kornilov, instead of being tried for high treason, is lauded as the Great Patriot by the bourgeoisie. But with them patriotism is tawdry talk and a sham. They pray for the Germans to come and cut off Petrograd, the Head of the Revolution.

Rodzianko, ex-President of the Duma, brazenly writes: “Let the Germans take the city. Though they destroy the fleet they will throttle the Soviets.” The big insurance companies announce one-third off in rates after the German occupation.” (Albert Rhys Williams, Through the Russian revolution, p. 89)

“Among the prominent representatives of the “bourgeois” class was Rodzianko. He had been a member of the Duma. He had been president of the Duma. The public press had continually reported his view of a possible German capture of Petrograd. Such a capture, according to Rodzianko, would be no great misfortune. It might indeed be a blessing. The Germans had captured Riga. Did they do any harm? They did good. They abolished the Soviet and restored the old-regime police.

Col. William B. Thompson, Robins old chief in the American Red Cross Mission to Russia, had noticed this strong streak of welcome for Germany among the Russian “bourgeois” during his stay in Petrograd.” (William Hard, Raymond Robins’ own story, p. 129)

“Miliukof’s famous intervention in the Duma should not be forgotten, when he denounced from the rostrum “ the plots woven for the purpose of reaching the unprecedented infamy of a separate peace.” When the Revolution broke out, all the elements that were reactionary or simply bourgeois turned in distress towards Germany. The anxious expectation of German support was openly advertised, we can positively state, in all the clubs and in all the salons. The same Miliukof who had formerly denounced the separate peace from the rostrum of the Duma in 1918 entered into suspicious cabals with Germany in the name of his party. It may be stated that at the present time there is not a reactionary or a bourgeois in Moscow or Petrograd who is not still ready to give half of what is left of Russia to see order restored in these cities by the Germans. Imperialistic Germany of the Junkers and the Prussian soldiery is the sole hope of these people” (Étienne Antonelli, Bolshevik Russia, p. 174)

“none other than Miliukoff, the well-known leader of the Russian Liberals, publicly stated in the Duma, in reply to a taunt by the Monarchists, that “rather than organise the country for national defence, if that should help the organisation of the revolutionary forces, he would leave her as she was,” that is, defenceless against the Germans.” (Litvinov, p. 17)

The Mensheviks allied with the above mentioned Milyukov, the leader of the capitalist Cadet Party. The Right-SRs, the Mensheviks’ main ally, also plotted to have Germany occupy Petrograd in order to topple the Bolsheviks (International press correspondence, Vol. 2, No. 50, June 17, 1922).

Menshevik Liber tried to defend the capitalists and “stated that the fact that a part of the Kadet party had adopted a “German orientation” and had concluded an alliance with the monarchists did not mean that the entire Russian bourgeoisie was guilty of such a turnabout.” (Brovkin, The Mensheviks after October, p. 209)

“The question of yielding to the Germans and crushing the Communists with their help was eagerly discussed in connection with the plan of a monarchical restoration. The idea found favour among the Rights and was supported among the Cadets by P. Miliukov, who had fled to Kiev, and considered that the game was definitely lost by the western Allies and that it was wiser to accept defeat from the Germans than from the Bolsheviks.” (Encyclopedia Britannica, Vol. 31-32, p. 323).

As was widely known, Milyukov wanted the restoration of the tsar. He said they must ally with the Germans and: “The government which is to emerge must be an all-national government …, monarchical in character… An effort should be made to determine the candidate for the throne at once. . . . I would suggest the Grand Duke Mikhail Alexandrovich” (James Bunyan, Intervention, civil war, and communism in Russia, April-December, 1918 : documents and materials, p. 178)

The facts demonstrate that not the Bolsheviks, but the Mensheviks, allied with forces who hoped for German intervention, and the Mensheviks also collaborated with German occupiers in those territories that they occupied. The arguments they used against the Bolsheviks were mere lies, simply trying to fool people.  
  
**SHORTAGES AND THE EMBARGO**

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/civil-war-invasion.png?w=717)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/civil-war-invasion.png)

The Mensheviks and SRs tried to utilize the famine and scarcity of all resources to attack Bolshevism. They blamed everything on the Bolsheviks, often saying the Bolsheviks have destroyed the country. However, everybody now understands that the devastation was caused by the three years of world war, which the Mensheviks and SRs wanted to participate in, as well as the civil war, launched by the Whites and the Entente invaders, both of whom the Mensheviks and SRs supported.

However, the scarcity of food, fuel and resources was especially caused by the embargo placed on Soviet Russia. The embargo was imposed by the Entente, i.e. by the allies of the Mensheviks:

“There is a shortage of fuel; petrol is held up by the British (the Baku oil wells); and coal is held up by Denikin, who is supported by the Entente.” (Paul Birukoff, “The New Russia”, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, October 11, 1919 No. 19)

“It is necessary to note in passing the vast campaign of economic encirclement, the pitiless blockade established against Russia. If the Entente is obliged to renounce all armed intervention, direct or indirect, she hopes at least to conquer Bolshevism through bringing about industrial ruin and famine. Holding in her hands the wheat granaries of Siberia, she is already beginning to starve out this unhappy country, and already she has condemned to death hundreds of thousands of innocents.” (Jacques Sadoul, “Impressions of Moscow a Year Ago”, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, January 17, 1920 No. 3)

“That is what is being called the economic encircling; it is the lack of food which is one of the means of the counter-revolution by which it tries to force the Russian popular revolution to capitulate. Never has famine in Russia attained the fabulous proportions which were assigned to it by the campaign of calumnies which was systematically worked up during the past few months. Our situation as regards the food supply is difficult and painful, and it is only with the greatest efforts that we succeed in maintaining some food allowances to the people.” (“Allied Intrigues Against Russia”, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, August 9, 1919 No. 10)

“I know that the starvation and misery from which the Russian people are suffering is due not to those who are building up the new socialist form of society but to those who for three years drove Russia into an exhausting war and then sent armed forces to invade her territory and cut off her food supplies and the raw materials of her industries.” (“The Truth About Soviet Russia” (third installment) by M. Philips Price, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, August 16, 1919 No. 11)

According to capitalist philosopher Russell:

“The typical Russian peasant has never heard of the Allies or of this country ; he does not know that there is a blockade ; all he knows is that he used to have six cows but the Government reduced him to one for the sake of poorer peasants, and that it takes his corn (except what is needed for his own family) at a very low price. The reasons for these actions do not interest him, since his horizon is bounded by his own village.” (Bertrand Russell, The practice and theory of Bolshevism, pp. 103-104)

It is very convenient that they orchestrate the embargo and the scarcity, and then try to use the scarcity to fool people into attacking Bolshevism! This again demonstrates that the Mensheviks and SRs could only hope to gain any popularity by deception.

“The cities were the stronghold of the Bolsheviks. The bourgeoisie [and their helpers] sought to play the country against them. “Look!” they said to the peasants, “The cities work but eight hours a day, why should you work sixteen? Why deliver your grain to the cities when you get nothing in return?”” (Albert Rhys Williams, Through the Russian revolution, p. 168)

“there was another method of bribery by which opponents hoped to break the spirit and power of the Government, and that was by undermining the morale of the soldiers by means of money bribes, and it is here where Allied gold came in and where it was spent so very plentifully. Of course this sort of thing is very difficult to track down and takes a very large number of men and women in order to do it thoroughly.

Then there was the industrial side of life… many of the leading agents of the Allies recognised that the Soviet Government would not be pulled down by fighting, and so they set to work in the industrial centres to create unrest and dissatisfaction owing to the shortage of food. These agents were able to corrupt other agents, who in turn went into the factories and spread their poison, declaring that with peace there would come plenty, and that the responsibility for [the interventionist civil] war and shortage lay with the Soviet Government.” (George Lansbury, What I saw in Russia, p. 116)

**THE STRIKE OF THE EXPERTS**

The Mensheviks tried to cause as much harm to the economy as possible, so that they could blame all the difficulties on Bolshevism. It is the typical situation, the capitalists and their Menshevik hirelings have ruined the country due to their reactionary policies and due to their imperialist war. The communists come to power in the impoverished country, and immediately the capitalists and Mensheviks organize sabotage and blame the hard conditions on the Communists – the typical method they’ve used ever since.

It is well known that after the October Revolution all the state officials, bank officials, telegraphic operators and other civil servants went on strike in order to resist the working class. With capitalist funding this strike was able to go on for months. It was organized by the Mensheviks together with capitalists. It was just the same kind of strike as those organized by the CIA in Chile to try and topple Allende – truly democratic. The expert strike was funded by Russian bankers (Price, My reminiscences of the Russian revolution, p. 165) and the striking state officials also received funds from the French government (Imprecor Vol. 2, No. 52, June 22, 1922).

Even a certain capitalist writer, whose words are quoted in the Swiss social-democrat paper Volksrecht (July 9-11, 1919) quite correctly stated that: “Immediately after the accession of the Soviet Government a comprehensive sabotage set in. Directors, employees, and in some cases, also the foremen of nationalized businesses went on strike”

“The civil servants’ strike started in various departments in the first days of November. It is noteworthy that the employees of the Ministry of Labor (a bastion of Defensist Mensheviks) were among the most resolute organizers of the strike, which lasted nearly two months with varying degrees of endurance in different departments.” (Brovkin, Dear comrades : Menshevik reports on the Bolshevik revolution and the civil war, p. 45)

The Mensheviks and SRs attacked the Bolsheviks, saying, “you cannot govern”, “you do not have the necessary management skills”, “you’ve thrown the country into chaos”, but the chaos was caused by the Mensheviks themselves, and they had done their best to deprive the Bolsheviks of management personnel. The often barely literate workers had to quickly learn how to do everything themselves, as best they could, until after a couple of months the officials finally agreed to collaborate with the new workers’ government.

The Mensheviks loudly complained about nationalization and workers’ control, implemented by the Bolsheviks, but these policies were largely a response to the actions of the Mensheviks and the capitalists. The Bolsheviks had to nationalize all the factories and let the workers manage them without any experts or managers, because the managers had refused to collaborate. It caused chaos, but there was nothing else that could be done, and eventually the workers beat the resistance of the bourgeois experts and succeeded in getting them to collaborate.

“It was not communism that plunged this huge, creaking, bankrupt empire into six years of exhausting war. It was European imperialism. Nor is it communism that has pestered this suffering and perhaps dying Russia with a series of subsidised raids, invasions, and insurrections, and inflicted upon it an atrocious blockade. The vindictive French creditor, the journalistic British oaf, are far more responsible for these deathbed miseries than any communist.” (H. G. Wells, Russia in the shadows, pp. 27-28)

Even according to one Menshevik writer, who had become critical of his party, the Bolsheviks were “determined to put an end to the war, and to try by means of energetic economic measures to remedy the poverty situation. The Bolsheviki have not succeeded in these two things, but Fate, and not they, are to blame… We must admit without exception that the organization that has been created is quite insufficient, but the fault falls upon other shoulders, particularly on those of the intellectuals who sabotaged this reorganization.”

“it is certain that only they [the Bolsheviks] dared to save Russia, by their own force, out of this extraordinarily difficult situation, and to oppose the old imperialism and to throw into the masses the torch of social revolution. Disregarding the gigantic effort of the Bolsheviki in the field of education, their rule, as such, has a magic influence on the masses. Without doubt the material situation of the workers has grown considerably worse during the past few years, since Russia was generally impoverished. Without doubt the struggle for existence is today much more difficult than it was, and yet the mass of the Russian workers rallies to the Soviet power, since it is their own power, since thanks to this power they have attained an entirely different social position.” (“Two years ago”, Leipziger Volkzeitung, No. 253. 1919)

When they were in power, the Mensheviks and SRs opposed all strikes as anarchy, and called for firm discipline. Under the Soviet power they began always calling for strikes. They claimed that nationalization was reactionary, but under the NEP they also called the ending of nationalization a disastrous and dangerous policy and betrayal of socialism. Yet at the same time as they attacked the NEP because it strengthened capitalism, they demanded that capitalist newspapers should be allowed. They criticized and attacked the Bolsheviks in every way they could, but fewer and fewer people believed their lies.

**THE NEP**

As is known, the Mensheviks consistently opposed the socialist revolution and wanted the restoration of capitalism. However, when the Bolsheviks implemented the New Economic Policy (NEP), which temporarily allowed capitalism to exist in a limited form, the Mensheviks attacked this policy. It seems they were going to attack Bolshevism no matter what, which only demonstrates their dishonesty.

“Observers have been at a loss to explain the apparent contradiction between Menshevik support of the idea of NEP and rejection of NEP itself.” (André Liebich, From the other shore : Russian social democracy after 1921, p. 107)

Some anti-communists even claim that the Bolsheviks stole the NEP from the economic program of the Mensheviks. If that were true, it would make it even more strange that the Mensheviks firmly opposed the NEP. The Mensheviks and SRs even tried to assume a “leftist” guise and attacked the NEP as excessively right-wing.

“Mensheviks also quarreled with Bolsheviks over the degree of state capitalism (or “socialism,” in Bolshevik parlance) that existed in Soviet Russia… Martov had warned that… Bolsheviks had capitulated before capitalism to a far greater degree than necessary… The remaining state sector was so isolated and weak that it was not viable. In Dallin’s words, it was like an English lord’s park or an American millionaire’s garden: a pseudo-productive, idle luxury with no economic justification.” (Liebich, p. 116)

According to the SR leadership “The NEP… offered concrete evidence of the counterrevolutionary essence of the Bolshevik dictatorship” (Smith, Captives of revolution : the socialist revolutionaries and the Bolshevik dictatorship, 1918-1923, p. 235)

“In May [1924] a new party programme was adopted in Berlin and was published by the Socialist Courier as The Platform of the RSDRP… it warned that the NEP must not be allowed to restore capitalism” (Vera Broido, Lenin and the Mensheviks: The Persecution of Socialists under Bolshevism, p. 153)

Dallin said: “Moreover, Lenin had decided to “compromise” with experts “who know how to organize industry… In short, his “communism” was a fiasco. The government was openly adopting a bourgeois-capitalist policy — which in itself would not be so bad for the workers, except that this return to capitalism was going on in forms that delivered the Russian proletariat “tied hand and foot” to capitalist exploitation…  
  
David Dallin, also a member of the Menshevik CC, filled out the picture: “Only one class in Russia has benefited and been strengthened by the external and domestic events — the well-to-do peasantry [krupnoe krest’ianstvo]. It will take the helm of Russia’s government. It will be the state power.”” (David Dallin, in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 135)

Dallin’s analysis was completely baseless. According to Marxism, a peasant state cannot exist. States can only be formed by the basic classes of the given mode of production, i.e. either by workers or capitalists. Furthermore, the rich peasants obviously did not gain power in the Soviet Union.

The Mensheviks betrayed their true intentions when they complained that the NEP has not restored the power of the capitalists, has not allowed capitalist parties, capitalist newspapers, and hasn’t restored the capitalist state. The main priority of the Mensheviks was the restoration of the capitalist dictatorship, and after that the capitalist economy could be restored without any hindrance.

“In practice, however, the NEP had little in common with the Menshevik proposals… the Mensheviks came to see that the NEP, though an improvement over War communism, was… of little value without political reform… and SV [Sotsialistischeskii Vestnik] wrote that… the NEP was “a lifeless thing”” (Simon Wolin in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, pp. 247-248)

**CAUSES OF REVOLTS AND DISSATISFACTION UNDER THE BOLSHEVIKS**

All that being said, let’s examine what factors caused dissatisfaction with the Soviet government, such as protests or even peasant revolts. The biggest reasons were food shortages in the cities, confiscation of food from the peasants, and conscription. These were genuine reasons that caused many people to be unhappy, but usually they understood why those things happened and accepted them. Workers usually understood that food was scarce because of war devastation, backwardness of the economy etc. Historians recognize that already after waging war for a couple years, cities of the tsarist empire started to suffer starvation. The situation kept getting worse the longer the war lasted.

Many factories also had to close because of lack of fuel. Both food and fuel shortages were caused by war devastation, but were made much worse by the embargo. Usually workers understood this despite Menshevik attempts to spread lies and to wage discontent. However, peasants often times had no idea that there was an embargo.

“the peasants are too ignorant to have any national consciousness, such as one takes for granted in England or France or Germany. The peasants will not willingly part with a portion of their produce merely for purposes of national defence, but only for the goods they need” (Bertrand Russell, The practice and theory of Bolshevism, p. 101)

The peasants were unhappy that they had to give food for the cities and for the Red Army, and also that they had to be conscripted, but they were usually willing to do it.

“when the Red Army passes they [the poor peasants] assist it with food; they see that their interests are linked with the retention of Soviet power. It had previously been frankly confessed to me that there have been risings of peasants, and the cause was confessed with equal frankness. A year ago, in dire straits for food for the center of Russia, owing to the blockade and the advancing ring of enemies, the Soviet had to send Commissaries and soldiers into the country to take the food. The necessity was great and something had to be done, but the doing of it gave great umbrage to the peasants concerned.” (William Thomas Goode, Bolshevism at work, p. 46)

They didn’t always understand the reasons behind those things, but both food requisition and conscription were things that all armies in Russia did, not just the Bolsheviks. As a result the peasants consistently preferred the Bolsheviks. For them, the main difference between the Bolsheviks and the Whites was that the Whites wanted to restore the power of the landlords, and generally restored tsarist methods of rule, the so-called “land captains” (zemsky nachalniks), local tsarist dictators who flogged the peasants and treated them like serfs. As a result, though the workers volunteered for the Red Army more readily, the peasants also volunteered to join the Reds when the White Army approached.

Some anti-communist historians claim the Bolsheviks shouldn’t have requisitioned food from the peasants, but many sources also say it was clearly the only option. The Mensheviks and Whites also tried to create rebellions among peasants in the Red territories by calling for free trade, but in the territories they occupied the Whites themselves also confiscated food and didn’t rely on free trade, so this argument most be considered dishonest.

“If this policy [free-trade] were adopted, the towns would be faced by utter starvation, not merely by hunger and hardship.” (Russell, p. 102)

In 1918 the Bolsheviks organized committees of the rural poor, consisting of farm proletarians, poor and landless peasants etc. These committees were proletarian and semi-proletarian centers in the countryside. However, they were never aimed against middle peasants, only against the rich exploiters.

**A. V. Moravov, *A Sitting of the Poor Peasant Committee, 1918***

“At first these committees of the poorer peasantry met with resistance in the villages. Rebellions against the Republic broke out in many districts and were fomented by the agents of the German government and other governments, as the documents published by the Extraordinary Commission for the Fight with the Counter-revolution proves. Just as the Vendee and the Champagne revolted against the proletariat and the small bourgeoisie of Paris in the French Revolution were aided by a British naval expedition, which blockaded the French coast in the interests of the “real France,” so now the wealthy and ignorant part of the Russian peasantry struck against the urban workers and the half-proletariat and were assisted by the ruling classes of England, who proved true once more to their traditions as suppressors of all movements for freedom in Europe, by conspiring to overthrow the popular movement in Russia. But the peasant revolts in the central provinces were put down by the Soviet Government. Stern revolutionary discipline was enforced and the saying of Mirabeau was confirmed: “Angels are not made out of butter in time of revolution.”” (Price, The old order in Europe and the new order in Russia, pp. 27-28)  
The Mensheviks tried to use all the genuine hardships caused by the war to spread dissatisfaction in order to overthrow the Bolshevik Soviet state.

However, there were also other more occasional reasons for popular dissatisfaction and protests. Especially the Right-SRs tried to take advantage of the ignorance of the peasants to get them to turn against the Bolsheviks. For example, the peasants knew that Lenin had given them land, so the Right-SRs said that Lenin was actually with them and not a communist. Among some peasants they used the slogan “Down with the communists, long live COMRADE Lenin” (Vadim Telitsyn, “Senseless and merciless”?, The phenomenon of peasant rebellion of 1917-1921).

Right-SRs and kulak rebels told the peasants:

“The Bolsheviks and the Communists are different. The Bolsheviks and the Communists struggle against each other. The Communists want to unite the peasants by force into agricultural communes; the Communists want to liquidate the church, and, finally, the Communists have replaced Soviet power by their own power.” From here follows a logical conclusion: it is necessary to help the Bolsheviks struggle “against Communists and Jews,” it is necessary to struggle for Soviet power against the dictatorship of the Communists.” (Brovkin, Dear comrades, p. 173 quoting from a Bolshevik trade-union organ)

One anti-communist historian says that “Some rebels clearly under SR leadership used slogans ““Death to the communists, long live the People’s Freedom Party, long live the Constituent Assembly!” However, this slogan did not have any noticeable impact” (Telitsyn). Indeed, the peasants didn’t seem to care about the Constituent Assembly and were not very party-minded either.

According to a capitalist historian Russian peasants were very susceptible to reactionary rumors, which sometimes were not caused by the communists in any way:

“dissatisfaction based on short-term interests or constantly changing moods, caused not so much by political as by everyday factors – for example, the envy of neighbor to neighbor (due to rumors about his exemption from duties), village to village (due to alleged free transfer of textiles and salt to one of them), volost to volost (talk about the allocation of free agricultural equipment, seeds, etc.). Rumors for the most part remained rumors, but just one word about the privileged (even hypothetically) position of a neighbor was enough for peasant grumbling to result in open discontent” (Telitsyn)

Sometimes peasant rebellions were sparked due to mistakes or outright wrong behavior by the communists or the Cheka. There were several reasons for this.

The Cheka was sometimes infiltrated by class enemies, tsarist police, Right-SRs, corrupt people. A Cheka official from central Russia in 1920 discusses the class enemies there, mentioning among others “White officers, senior police element. This group consists of old officials, police officers, bailiffs and gendarmes who hid their past, on the one hand, and the sediment of Kolchakism, Dutovshina, Denikinism and other “caliphs for an hour”, a muddy sediment that remained in almost all the outskirts – on the other hand, often attached themselves to Soviet power, even occupying responsible positions.” (Telitsyn)

According to a diary from a Russian White Guard, the White punitive detachments which attacked villages sometimes pretended to be Reds so the peasants would blame the communists (“The Diary of a Russian Counter-Revolutionist”, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, September 6, 1919 No. 14).

“The sad fact is generally admitted that the Russian Communist Party had been permeated by many undesirable elements who made use of the prestige this party enjoyed in Russia for their personal purposes, or for obtaining certain privileges. Thus, the Russian Communist Party in the course of the last few years has admitted far too many petty bourgeois elements, having nothing in common with Communism, who entered the party merely because that step was necessary in order to enable them to occupy some official post. The Russian Communists have been continually struggling with certain adventurers, climbers, bureaucrats, even criminals, who had in some way or other wormed their way into the ranks of the party and who were a source of discredit to the organization among the masses of the people, and who in other ways inflicted injuries on the movement…

(T)he most important point is the fact that all the cleaning up meetings of the party were attended also by large crowds of non-partisan workers. The fate of a communist has thus often been decided by the verdict of a non-partisan colleague. The following sometimes took place: a few “communists” whose consciences were not quite clear would agree among themselves before the meeting to keep quiet about certain things. This calculation, however, was usually upset by the honest workers who, without having become members of the Communist Party, were nevertheless disinclined to tolerate corruption in the party… The cleaning up eliminated not only these elements that were directly harmful, such as usurers, bribe-taking Soviet employees, disguised counter-revolutionists, but also such elements as indirectly injured the prestige of the party and the Soviet power in the eyes of the people, such as drunkards, idlers, etc. The great mass of those excluded, however, consisted of petty bourgeois elements who had been in the party but a short time.” (“The Party Cleaning In Russia”, Soviet Russia, Vol. VII New York, July 15, 1922, No. 1)  
  
The Cheka and the communists also made real mistakes. Although the uprising in Kostroma had been engineered by White Guards, the Cheka still analysed its causes and made the following criticisms against the Soviet officials:

“1. lack of party work and 2. inability, often criminal actions of local representatives of the Soviet government (executive committees and committees of the poor).” These inept actions were determined either by the illiteracy and simple ignorance of Soviet workers on the ground” or “were [carried out] deliberately aimed at discrediting the Soviet government” by members of the old tsar era police forces and officials, who had been able to remain inside the city administration to an extent. (M.A. Lapshina, Civil War in Kostroma Province (1918–1919))

An investigation committee also noted that at the center of the uprising (in Urensko) in some Soviets the entire executive committees had been infiltrated by criminal class enemies, while trying to gain credibility by labeling themselves Bolsheviks.

“In one of the volosts of the Kostroma district – Karimovskaya – a meeting of village committees was scheduled for December 5 to approve a tax of 500 thousand rubles distributed among the peasants of the volost. All willing peasants came to the meeting (according to the attendance list – 194 people). The meeting decided: “The tax of 500 thousand rubles should be rejected due to the fact that there are no wealthy peasants in our volost, therefore there is no one to tax.” A negative decision was also made on the supply of horses and cattle due to the fact that there are no extra horses and cows… The behavior of the secretary of the volost council A. M. Godnev caused particular indignation among the peasants for the arbitrariness he allowed during the apportionment of taxes, as well as the chairman of the Karimov Committee of the Poor A. A. Babanov, who took away bread and potatoes not only from middle peasants, but also from the poor. Completely without remuneration, both were expelled from their positions as oppressors and discrediters of the authorities. Nevertheless, the Cheka arrested 11 people from the village of Klobushnevo, the village of Tereshkino and others in this case. As a result of the investigation, the commission decided to terminate the case due to lack of evidence against the accused.” (Lapshina)

For example Military comissar Lipnyak was convicted by the Cheka for “abuse of power” and “discrediting the name of the Soviets” while he was suppressing a kulak revolt (Telitsyn).

After the Makaryevski revolt ended “The investigative commission of the Revolutionary Tribunal, having analyzed the events here, came to the conclusion that there was no fair treatment for the rebellious and confused peasants. The commission’s report notes that on the part of the punitive detachment, “punishment often turned into real mockery… [reminiscent of the old bourgeois] police regime, which did not strengthen, but completely discredited Soviet power…”. During the unrest in Makaryev, power passed into the hands of the military operational headquarters, headed by Commissar Kitov (later convicted by a tribunal for embezzlement and abuse)… [the punitive detachment had been headed by] two former police officers (Gribin and Budakov, the secretary of the headquarters)” who abused their position, threatened people and stole money and food for themselves. (Lapshina)

In the above case the Cheka discovered the truth, punished the corrupt people and did not take any legal action against the peasants. Such grumblings usually did not escalate to a revolt and the communist authorities and peasants were able to come to an agreement:

In another incident Peasants from the villages of Zashilsky, Davydov, Khlyabishin and others gathered in the volost center and beat up a Red Army conspription official and a militia man. “On May 24, 1919, the investigative commission of the Revolutionary Tribunal issued a resolution to terminate this case and amnesty the peasants, since their actions here were not of an organized nature.”

There was unrest among peasants in the Spasskaya volost due to the decree separating church from the state. But the Bolshevik investigative authorities determined that local communists had acted in a way which insulted religious sensibilities, and thus the “case was also dismissed by the provincial tribunal, which qualified the actions of the council representative as tactless and insulting the feelings of believers.” (Lapshina)

“The presence of even a minimal political sense among the authorities made it possible to quickly find a common language with the villagers, and that, as a rule, was where it all ended. An example of such a situation is the event that took place in July 1918 in the Kostkovskaya volost of Demyansky district (Novgorod province). Here, about a thousand peasants came to the building of the Demyansk District Soviet to demand bread and seeds. But there was no outburst of discontent, since “after some explanations given by the chairman of the Council about the state of affairs in terms of food, the arriving citizens regained their senses and calmly went home”” (Telitsyn)

Despite the fact that corrupt individuals and class enemies tried to infiltrate the Cheka, as a rule the Cheka consisted of honest people, especially the leaders were veteran revolutionaries who had devoted their lives to the cause of the revolution:

“As for the Provincial Chekas, the following figures will show how they are constituted. In 1920, a conference of their presidents and vice presidents was held at Moscow. Of the 69 who were present, there were 45 workers, 13 peasants and 11 intellectuals. All were party members and 28 of them had already been party members under the Tsar.

It is not among this personnel of devoted revolutionists that you will find the undesirable elements. Unfortunately, however, the subaltern functionaries cannot be recruited quite so scrupulously. To be sure, there is a large proportion of Communists, but there are also those that are not Communists…

In spite of the inadequacy of its ranks, which is due to the general low standard of popular education, to which those abuses which have really occurred must be assigned, we could not in full justice form that opinion of the Cheka which one would hold if one should put faith in all the fables that have been handed out.” (Аndre Morizet, “The Cheka”, Soviet Russia, Vol. VI New York, May 15, 1922 No. 9)

The Cheka shot 15,000 people during the whole civil war of four years, while the French bourgeoisie massacred 30,000 Paris Communards in eight days (Аndre Morizet, “The Cheka”), and the Finnish bourgeoisie killed the same number of Finnish Reds in the White Terror within a matter of months.

If the people suffered from shortages of food, so did the communists. Conditions during 1918-20s were so hard even for the Bolshevik “rulers” that they lived in cold, damp and dark rooms, slept and ate little as kollontai says in her memoirs. She felt nostalgia for emigre days before the revolution. Visitors said that foreign affairs comissar Chicherin looked like he was starving to death. President of the Republic Sverdlov and leader of the Bolshevik Women’s Section Armand both died of preventable disease during the civil war.

According to American diplomatic personnel:

“Everyone is hungry in Moscow and Petrograd, including the people’s commissaries themselves. The daily ration of Lenin and the other commissaries is the same as that of a soldier in the army or of a workman at hard labor. In the hotel which is reserved for Government officials the menu is the following: Breakfast — A quarter to half a pound of black bread, which must last all day, and tea without sugar. Dinner — A good soup, a small piece of fish, for which occasionally a diminutive piece of meat is substituted, a vegetable, either a potato or a bit of cabbage, more tea without sugar. Supper — What remains of the morning ration of bread and more tea without sugar.” (The Bullitt mission to Russia, 1919, p. 56)

“the general spirit of Bolshevism is quite honest, and it is set very stoutly against looting and suchlike “ (H. G. Wells, Russia in the shadows, p. 50)

“whatever else we may say to the discredit of the Bolshevik leaders, it is undeniable that the great majority lead not simply laborious but puritanical lives.” (Wells, pp. 95-96)

“In all the villages I visited I found the peasants faring much better than were the Commissars in Moscow.” (Isaac McBride, “Barbarous Soviet Russia,” p. 11)

Why did some soldiers (mainly peasants) desert from the Red Army? Did they do it because they hated communism? The answer is no.

“the large number of deserters surrendering voluntarily highlights the economic, as opposed to the political, motivation of the majority of the peasant desertions—what the Soviet authorities termed desertions of a ‘non-malicious’ (nezloe) or ‘politically unconscious’ (nesoznatel’noe) kind. Whereas the deserters from the White Army rarely returned to it, or even joined the Reds, those from the Red Army could sometimes be persuaded to return to the front… Also known to be effective in this respect were the ‘amnesty weeks’, declared during special propaganda campaigns in aid of the Red Army, when deserters were given the opportunity to return to the ranks without punishment.

The mainly economic character of the peasant desertions was reflected in the causes of the latter. The primary motivation of the peasant deserters was to return to their farm for the harvest. According to one survey, the number of deserters in the Volga region at the peak of the 1919 harvest season (August) was five to seven times greater than during the previous April. The same survey found that 43.7% of the deserters from Tambov, Riazan’, Tula, and Moscow provinces during January to May 1919 had run away because their unit was close to their home village, while a further 23.2% had deserted because their unit had been assigned to leave for the front. A second common motivation was the deserters’ concern for the well-being of their families… A third major cause of desertion from the Red Army was the chronic supply of food” etc. (Figes, Peasant Russia, civil war : the Volga countryside in revolution, 1917-1921, p. 318)

Although at first many peasants were quite unhappy about military service, basing their view on the experiences from the tsarist era, they eventually became satisfied with the Red Army. Peasant soldiers actually usually preferred being in the army instead of returning to the primitive life of the villages:

“The Red Army, on the contrary, is popular— to enter it means opportunity, promotion, and education.” (H. N. Brailsford, How The Soviets Work, p. 49)

“the whole the spirit of the [red] army appears to be very high, particularly since its recent successes. The soldiers no longer have the beaten dog-like look which distinguished them under the Czar but carry themselves like freemen and curiously like Americans. They are popular with the people.” (The Bullitt mission to Russia, 1919, p. 62)

“The testimony was universal that recruiting for the army is easiest in the districts which having once lived under the soviet were over run by anti-soviet forces and then reoccupied by the Red Army.” (The Bullitt mission to Russia, 1919, p. 63)

The tolstoyan writer Birukoff writes:

“The Russian peasants are as a rule conservative and do not trouble themselves much about the forms of government. A good many fables have been told about the peasants’ love for the Little Father, the Czar. We have seen in our own experience that the downfall of the Czar aroused no protest from them. As we said above, the present Government had won the confidence of the peasants by issuing decrees giving them peace and land. The renewal of the war, which has been forced upon the Government, and the subsequent mobilization, have done a good deal to make the people lose confidence in the new Government. But it has no thought at all of overthrowing this Government. The revolts of the peasants, which took place during last winter, were fomented by revolutionary [or rather counter-revolutionary] agitators, belonging to the parties opposed to the Government. But these revolts, serious though they were, were put down without difficulty…

The richer elements among the peasants are opposed to the Government, but they are in exactly the same measure enemies of the people, oppressors of the people; they and the small bourgeoisie of the towns were the strongest supporters of the Czarism, and it is from these elements that the Party of the “Black Hundreds” was recruited” (Paul Birukoff, “The New Russia”, Soviet Russia, Vol. I New York, October 11, 1919 No. 19)

Birukoff says quite accurately that the Bolsheviks initially rallied only the poor peasants committees to support them, but since 1919 or perhaps somewhat earlier, broadened their appeal also to the middle peasants.

**THE TEMPORARY DECREASE IN THE URBAN WORKING CLASS**

Another factor which could’ve temporarily helped Mensheviks and Right-SRs was that millions of workers left the cities during the civil war. On the other hand, the poorest proletarians and semi-proletarians went to the countryside in order to get food, and became declassed or peasantized, while the most conscious communist workers either volunteered for the Red Army and left for the battlefronts, or joined the communist party and were sent away on party tasks.

Later communist party organizations and factories were also frequently told to mobilize more and more of their workers for the war. This left an increasing proportion of politically backward workers, and also wealthier workers in the cities. Meanwhile the ruined bourgeoisie and petit-bourgeoisie had to seek employment and also entered the factories. This change in the composition of the city working population was extremely worrying to the Communists, and of course had an impact on Menshevik activity.

“Bolshevik leaders feared… that the proletariat that demonstrated such revolutionary class consciousness in 1917 was becoming “declassed” as a result of the economic pressures and dislocations of the civil war.” (Diane Koenker, “Urbanization and Deurbanization in the Russian Revolution and Civil War”, The Journal of Modern History, Vol. 57, No. 3 (Sep., 1985), pp. 425-426)

“John Keep writes: “The men who made the October revolution, in so far as they were civilians and not soldiers, were soon dissipated to the four winds… their place would eventually be filled by men who came straight from the village and were cast in a different mold.”” (Koenker, p. 426)

As an example, “over three years, the Nizhny Novgorod organization held 10 party mobilizations and sent several thousand communists to many numerical fronts of the civil war.” (Nizhegorodskaya Organizaciya RKPb V Gody Inostrannoj Voennoj Intervencii I Grazhdanskoj Vojny 1918-1920 Gg., p. 21)

In 1918 “The Petrograd Soviet has resolved to send a quarter of its members… to the Czechoslovak front.” (V. Serge, Year one of the Russian Revolution, Chicago, Holt, Rinehart, and Winston, 1972, p. 275)

Out of 15,000 communist party members in Petrograd in 1919 almost 10,000 were sent away to the fronts or other party tasks away from factories (Brovkin, Behind the front lines, p. 188)

During Kolchak’s general offensive In Syzran district, 50% of the communists were mobilized and 25% of union members” while Simbirsk announced a 100% mobilization of communists. In Pokrovsk the trade-unions mobilized 50% of their members (Е. И. Медведев, Гражданская война в Среднем Поволжье, pp. 294-295)

“From a third to a half of the total Communist Party membership was sent into the Red Army during the Civil War period.” (Merle Fainsod, How Russia is ruled, p. 397)

“Among men, skilled workers suffered the greatest numerical losses (although in percentages, these losses were less than among semiskilled or unskilled workers). It is this group that included the most committed of the revolutionary activists of 1917. Indeed, a different set of figures that permits comparison of 1920 and 1917 shows that the number of metalworkers, the quintessential urban proletarian activists, declined in Moscow by almost 40,000, or 66 percent: 25,000 of these left between 1917 and August 1918…

Like Moscow’s, Petrograd’s losses included large numbers of skilled and valuable longtime workers. In fact, none left in such great numbers, both absolute and relative, as the skilled metalworkers” (Koenker, pp. 440-441)

“evidence suggests that the overwhelming majority of Moscow’s skilled workers were lost to the Red Army itself.” (Koenker, p. 441)

Red Army “recruits were overwhelmingly young, unmarried, and childless; workers of this type had enthusiastically supported Soviet power in 1917, and **their physical absence from the city would clearly affect the political climate**.” (Koenker, p. 441)

“evidence confirms the commonsense assumption that the skilled workers who stayed in the city and continued to work were older family men. They were less likely to have been Bolshevik supporters than their younger brothers or sons… **Older workers had tended to support the Menshevik party in 1917; the Menshevik resurgence in 1918 was made possible in part by the Red Army’s mobilization and removal from the urban political scene of the activist young workers.**” (Koenker, p. 442)

Brovkin and other anti-communist hacks always claim that workers turned against the Communists, but facts undeniably show that the masses continued to support the Communists, and that even though there were changes, these cannot be explained only by the supposed changing moods of the workers, but also by the literal change in the composition of the workers. The Petrograd workers of 1918 or 1919 were not the same as the Petrograd workers of 1917.

According to J. Hatch, worker dissatisfaction was typically caused by fear of food shortages, or by reduction of food supply. This fits the situation in early 1918 completely. He further says that “Bolshevik estrangement from the working class” was not “permanent”, worker dissatisfaction was spread by right-SRs and Mensheviks, but was only temporary (J. Hatch, “Working‐class politics in Moscow during the early NEP: Mensheviks and workers’ organisations, 1921–1922”, Soviet Studies Volume 39, 1987 – Issue 4, p. 557)

Hatch writes **“Although the Communist Party was in no danger of losing the elections… the departure of pro-Communist workers from the factories during the Civil War undermined Communist influence over the working class”** (Hatch, p. 558)

The relatively higher support for other parties in Rogozhko-Simonovskii (4%, not a high figure!) in the early 1920s, were because there “major inroads were being made by the Communist Party’s radical Workers’ Opposition”, an opportunist ultra-left group of demagogues. (Hatch, p. 561)

Hatch says that the increase of opposition and lowering of support for Bolsheviks among workers during that period was because the working class changed. Thousands of workers went back for long periods to the countryside, becoming declasse. The number of workers in general simply diminished and the number of other classes increased in relation. The most politically conscious pro-Bolshevik workers joined the Red Army and also left for the front, this relatively increasing the number of non-workers and less politically active workers. The exodus of workers to the countryside also caused economic problems which worsened discontent:

It “had to do with the extensive changes in the composition of the Moscow working class that had taken place since 1917. In purely quantitative terms, this change was remarkable: during the Civil War years the number of industrial workers in Moscow declined from 187,000 to 84,000. This decline resulted from several factors, including the departure of thousands of skilled, younger pro-Bolshevik workers from the factories to either the front or work in various party and administrative agencies, and the exodus to the villages of tens of thousands of additional workers seeking to escape Moscow’s economic collapse.” (Hatch, pp. 560-561)

Because Bolshevik workers and party members had been sent to the front, “industrial party organisations” had become “woefully under-strength” (Hatch, p. 561)

Thus, it is quite evident that if there was any temporary weakening of bolshevism in the cities in 1918-21, it wasn’t because the people turned against them. It was because the Bolshevik party members and supporters were constantly being sent to fight and die at the battle fronts of the civil war.
