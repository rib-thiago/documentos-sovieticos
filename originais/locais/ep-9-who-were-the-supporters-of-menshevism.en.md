# Menshevik attempt to overthrow Bolshevism: Ep.9 – Who were the supporters of Menshevism?

This is the last part in my series about Menshevism (though I’ll probably discuss some related topics at some point in the future). In this final part we’ll examine who the supporters of Menshevism were. From previous evidence we can conclude that Mensheviks received support from the petit-bourgeoisie and non-workers, even from previous factory owners or managers.

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/martov-dan-1.jpg?w=600)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/martov-dan-1.jpg)

***Mensheviks Julius Martov (left) and Fedor Dan (right)***

Hardline anti-communist and Menshevik sympathizer Brovkin says that Mensheviks and SRs had their strongest base in smaller more rural provincial towns, which “unlike larger cities, where tens of thousands of workers were concentrated in huge plants… were dominated by merchants, kustar’ shops [handicraft shops], and trading peasantry.” (Brovkin, “Menshevik comeback”, p. 4)

However, as examples he mentions places like Tver’ where according to Smith the Bolsheviks won the elections in 1918, and Kaluga, where the Mensheviks dissolved the Soviet because Bolsheviks won the election in 1917, so even there the Menshevik support doesn’t seem very firm.

According to social-democrat Price:

“The Mensheviks were supported by the brain-workers and by the privileged working-class aristocracy, which, however, thanks to the very small development of Craft Unionism in Tsarist Russia, were an almost negligible factor in the land. The Bolsheviks were supported by the greater part of the skilled workers, by the sailors, by that section of the poorest unskilled labourers who were not under the influence of the middle-class intellectuals, and by that section of the soldiers who had the courage to stand up against their officers. The Socialist Revolutionaries represented the middle and upper layer of the peasantry, a section of the poor peasantry as far as they were politically conscious at this period of the Revolution, the profiteering middleman, small proprietor, the professional man of the country-side and the simple careerist.” (M. Philips Price, My reminiscences of the Russian revolution, p. 26)

“The Mensheviks, the Bundists (the separate organisation of the Jewish workmen) and the Social Revolutionaries, mainly representing the non-industrial unions and the small urban centres (of the industrial unions, the only one which constituted a firm bulwark of the Mensheviks… was the printers’ union)” (Sidney & Beatrice Webb, Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation, Third ed. in one volume, Longmans, Green and co. 1947, p. 127)

“If there was a ‘labour aristocracy’ in Russia prior to the revolution, then printers were its best representative. Perhaps more than any other urban working class group, printers, in their small-scale work environments and with their guild-like solidarity, were imbued with the pre-industrial values of an artisanal elite… [they worked in small companies:] their labour environment was of a smaller scale than that of most other industries.” (J. Hatch, “Working‐class politics in Moscow during the early NEP: Mensheviks and workers’ organisations, 1921–1922”, Soviet Studies Volume 39, 1987 – Issue 4, p. 568)

“The print workers, with their high rates of pay and their close ties with the intelligentsia, were the most likely candidates for ‘labour aristocracy’ emerging in Russia.” (Orlando Figes, A people’s tragedy : the Russian Revolution 1891-1924, London : Pimlico, 1997, p. 115)

“typesetters, highly skilled urban workers, were notorious for setting themselves apart from other workers and often rejected participation in a wider labor movement in favor of helping themselves.\*” (Koenker, p. 431)

\*“It is well known that the Federation of Book-Workers was one of the most reactionary, and that one of its divisions, up to the last “Congress of the Book,” defended a position of neutrality [between reds and whites]. But since the formation of a united All-Russian Federation, on the basis of a support of Communism, the local groups of a series of cities, such as Rybinsk, Orel, Tambov, Vladimir, Kinechma, Chonia, are reporting their adhesions. At Kozlov, the printers’ union has also been connected with the Yellows up to the moment of the incursion of Mamontov’s bands. But the latter definitely opened the workers’ eyes as to the real meaning of the pretended neutrality of the trade-union movement, and on September 9, they completely broke their relations with the Yellows, to join the All-Russian Federation.” (Communique on trade unions, Soviet Russia, Vol. II New York, February 14, 1920 No. 7)

The Mensheviks had some supporters among a section of railway workers in the early days of the October Revolution. The railway union Vikzhel, advocated a coalition government of Mensheviks, Bolsheviks and SRs. The leadership of the union was mostly Menshevik, while the rank and file were increasingly Bolshevik. There was also an SR and capitalist faction.

“Vikzhel was composed of three groups: a pro-bolshevik and an anti-bolshevik group, of about equal size, and a third non-party group, said to be pro-Kadet—B. & F., p. 153, note 31. The anti-bolshevik strength of Vikzhel was, however, considerably neutralized by the fact that, as events soon showed, it failed in many cases to enforce the decisions on the rank and file of railway workers” (Leonard Schapiro, The origin of the communist autocracy; political opposition in the Soviet state: first phase, 1917-1922, Footnote on p. 70)

The strongest base of the Mensheviks was the book printers’ union, but the Vikzhel was their only actually strategically important position (or would’ve been, if Mensheviks had fully controlled it), as Vikzhel actually played a somewhat influential role in the early days of the revolution, due to the importance of the railroads. Soon the Vikzhel too began drifting towards Bolshevism, until it was replaced by a new communist union [Vikzhedor](https://encyclopedia2.thefreedictionary.com/Vikzhedor).

Mensheviks had more support among older conservative skilled workers, because they were not happy with egalitarian wage policies. They wanted higher wages for themselves and not for others (Hatch, p. 562).

Besides book printers, the Mensheviks other support base was the chemical industry, because it had a “large number of clerical and technical workers… a substantial number of more [skilled], well-paid, male workers… These difficulties were compounded by worsening conditions in the industry caused by the social and economic disruption of the period” (Hatch, p. 563)

The worsening conditions caused by war were of course only temporary and accidental, but the more firm reason why there were more Mensheviks among these segments was of course, that they were privileged, and some of them were officials and not factory proletarians.

In the chemical industry union “well-paid, skilled male workers in the rubber industry… interested in defending their relatively privileged economic position (much like skilled printers), dominated the union and supported Menshevik trade union politics” and “the existence of large numbers of traditionally liberal or social democratic clerical and technical workers in the chemical industry helped to reinforce the Menshevik grip” (Hatch, p. 563).

However, eventually even in the chemical industry union the Mensheviks lost their popularity to the Bolsheviks. The Menshevik newspaper Sotsialisticheskii Vestnik claims the Mensheviks were defeated in the union by Bolshevik sabotage and repression, but Hatch quite correctly writes that “the claim made by Sotsialisticheskii vestnik may have been at least partially self-serving. In times past the Communists had successfully mobilised worker support” (Hatch, p. 564)

At the chemical workers union conferences “the pro-Communist bloc was almost exclusively composed of delegates representing ‘large’ and ‘medium’ enterprises. Nearly all of the delegates from the ‘small enterprise-employee’ category supported the Mensheviks” (Hatch, p. 565)

In other words, the Bolshevik supporters were more proletarian, collective and disciplined, while Mensheviks were more petit-bourgeois, labor aristocratic minded, and those who earned higher wages. Elsewhere Hatch says the Menshevik workers were more “craft-conscious” i.e. supported only their own craft, not the class as a whole, and not even the industry as a whole.

In craft unionism each separate kind of work even in the same field or industry is separated into its own craft organization, and those of higher skill defend their “elite” position. For example in the field of construction, carpenters might get higher wages than plasterers or brick-layers. The carpenters would have their own craft union, separated from the craft union of plasterers and brick-layers. They would negotiate work contracts on their own, and let the other crafts fend for themselves. Craft unionism is an older and more backward form of unionism and derives from petit-bourgeois medieval guilds.

The extremely anti-communist Figes agrees that Menshevik supporters and members were mostly petit-bourgeois, but he makes the absolutely ridiculous argument that “the Mensheviks were genuinely more democratic, both in their policies and in their composition, than the Bolsheviks. They tended to attract a broader range of people… more diverse types of workers, petty merchants and members of the intelligentsia — whereas the followers of the Bolsheviks tended to come from a narrower range (the vast majority were Great Russian workers and uprooted peasants). This broader social base may partly explain the Mensheviks’ inclination towards compromise and conciliation with the liberal bourgeoisie.” (Figes, p. 154)

It is the epitome of anti-communist “logic” to claim that a workers’ party is undemocratic by definition because it includes mostly people from the working class, while a party which includes more property owners, intellectuals and collaborates with capitalists, is democratic because it appeals to these other classes too.

Menshevik workers were also older, more conservative, Bolshevik workers were younger as Hatch says on p. 565. Hatch says that according to a Bolshevik source the Mensheviks received votes also from the previous factory directors (actual bourgeois) who had been ousted. Figes also writes that:

“Bolshevik activists in the factories tended to be younger, more working class and much more militant than their Menshevik or SR rivals.” (Figes, p. 457)

During the worst years of the civil war the Mensheviks and right SRs tried to weaponize the discontent of the most backward workers:

“Hunger riots began to multiply, and anti-semitism reared its head again. Even at the Putilov Works an SR speaker shouted out on 8 May, ‘Let’s throw the Y\*\*s [anti-semitic slur] into the Neva, get a strike committee going and stop work.’… The best elements among the workers were away fighting; those in the factories were precisely the less energetic, less revolutionary sections, along with the petty folk, yesterday’s small shopkeepers and artisans, who had come there to find refuge.” (Serge, Year one, p. 228)

Hatch states that the higher vote for other parties such as Mensheviks in Sokol’nicheskii Soviet (which was still as little as 4.5%) was due to “the large number of white collar workers employed in the district” (Hatch, p. 559). Mensheviks apparently received help from left-oppositionists in the Bolshevik party (p. 560).

Finnish Communist leader Sirola wrote during the civil war:

““To defeat the enemy one needs courage, courage and more courage!” To the revolutionary poor masses Marx emphasized this golden rule of Danton’s. And the communist vanguard of the Russian proletariat has shown by its numerous previous struggles and by its remaining in the ceaseless fire of the revolutionary period that it has taken this rule to heart.

But when the struggle becomes protracted and more stubborn we must remember also another slogan: resilience, resilience and more resilience! – That is what the revolutionary people of Russia need most of all. Because the current half-year – spring, summer, autumn – is the period of its final test. During this period the decisive events will take place.

Tenacity, resilience is now being asked of the proletariat which is loyal to the revolution to its very core, because there are groups whose faith is wavering. They are those who don’t understand the laws of war, but imagine that already in the course of the battle the fruits of victory should be in their mouths. Such are part of the peasantry and petit-bourgeoisie, who say: we’ve had enough, let us be satisfied with this, let’s not fight any longer! They do not understand what wage-workers have learned in the school of countless strikes: that to abandon the fight halfway means to lose everything. It is impossible to remain “here”, firstly because this is not any kind of utopian situation, and secondly: the enemy won’t allow us to remain here, but smashes into pieces the foundations of everything we’ve laid down, if we relent even for a moment.

There are even some groups of wage-workers, who have a petit-bourgeois mentality, and who do not see the toiler class as a whole. Such splitting mentality is seen e. g. among railway workers. So the Moscow Pravda tells us that in that region some groups of railway workers have held meetings where they shout “bread” and even threaten to strike. In organizing such things certain engineers, officials and other petit-bourgeois elements have been very active. They whisper provocational rumors and incite the less thinking elements to scream. They rely on demagogy, the irresponsible incitement of unconscious elements. They don’t explicitly incite against the soviets themselves, because they know that even the most conservative groups of workers don’t accept that. But they take advantage of temporary hardships for splitting purposes.

For example now the bread supply has diminished. After all, large battles are taking place on the fronts and the new harvest hasn’t ripened yet. The conscious workers remember last year and know the situation was worse then. It looked very grim. But we survived it. And now it is only a past hardship, which only steeled us. As we won last year, now we will win better! – But not everybody has the strength to think like that, and there the counter-revolutionary provocateurs strike. They scream “bread”, though it hasn’t even run out yet.

Additionally the railway workers have certain privileges compared to other workers. They have for example been excluded from military service both during the world war and the civil war. That was the reason for the “patriotism”, social-patriotism of some of their groups. Even during the civil war the railwaymen have enjoyed the same privilege. And now that they are asked to join in carrying their part of the burden of military defense, it arouses grumbling in some of their groups.

That must be overcome. In the revolution what is at stake are the interests of all working people. What is at stake is the progress of the whole country and people, all humanity. Splitting that front is the blackest provocation imaginable. The working class cannot allow it. Towards all splitters, individuals and groups, it acts just like it has gotten used to acting during strikes towards strike-breakers and those cowards who constantly whine that “we will never succeed, let’s give up”” (Yrjö Sirola, “Vaivojen kautta voittoon”, Vapaus, 27. VI. 19.)

Sirola perfectly understood exactly the reality of the situation, the scale of the difficulties, but also the strength of the workers, which would triumph despite the cowards and traitors.

**THE BEST MENSHEVIKS AND SRS BECAME BOLSHEVIKS**

As the workers abandoned menshevism, the best Menshevik activists and minds abandoned it and joined the Bolsheviks.

“The most revolutionary elements left the Mensheviks in large numbers, in groups and individually, and merged with the RCP” (В. А. Быстрянский, Меньшевики и эсеры в русской, p. 107)

***Georgy Chicherin***

[![](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/troyanovsky.jpg?w=738)](https://mltheory.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/troyanovsky.jpg)

***Alexander Troyanovsky***

Georgy Chicherin joined the Bolsheviks in 1918, Ivan Maisky did so in 1919, Vyshinsky went from Menshevism to Bolshevism in 1920. The famous philosopher Deborin also left the Mensheviks and later joined the Bolsheviks. Troyanovsky joined the Bolsheviks in 1923, as did the Menshevik leader Martynov.

Martynov “came to the conclusion that the Menshevist conception of Democracy and Dictatorship is a saccharine babbling of persons who do not know what is going on in the world, that it is just parlor theory… Some say, “The Bolshevik dictatorship is a regime wherein a minority imposes its will by force on the majority”. Martynov answers, “This is wrong. The Bolshevik Dictatorship is a regime of force by the revolutionary classes and layers of the population over the unrevolutionary classes”” (Frida Rubiner, “From Menshevism to Communism”, Soviet Russia Pictorial, July, 1923)

“The ‘left’ [group of Mensheviks who wanted to collaborate with Bolshevism] was led by O. Ermansky, B. Ber, Andrei Vyshinsky, and O. Domanevskaia.” This is not to be confused with Martov’s centrist pseudo-leftist anti-bolshevik trend. (Getzler, Martov: a political biography of a Russian social democrat, p. 184)

“many Mensheviks defected to the Bolsheviks in the spring of 1920. Martov lamented that defections had acquired the proportions of a flood. L. M. Khinchuk, the former chairman of the Moscow soviet who had denounced the Bolshevik seizure of power at the Second Congress of Soviets back in October 1917, Ivan Maisky, former CC member and a minister of labor in the SR-led government of the Constituent Assembly in Samara in 1918, and many many other prominent old-time Social Democrats now switched to the Communists.” (Brovkin, Behind the front lines, p. 244)

“the “Social-Democrat Internationalists”… [joined the Bolsheviks] in 1919, and similar groupings.

Among the important figures on the extreme left, besides Ermanskii, were Boris Ber, the Kharkov leader; Ivan Akhmatov, also in the Ukraine, later in Siberia; and Andrei Vyshinsky, who… became a member of the extreme left in 1919, and eventually a Bolshevik.” (David Dallin, in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 106)

“It was a major blow for the [Menshevik] Party when two members of its Central Committee, Troianovskii and Ermanskii, defected in the spring of 1921.” (Simon Wolin in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 252)

G. Gonikberg also broke from the Mensheviks in the 1920s and returned from emigration to the USSR (Simon Wolin in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 321)

“in 1947, Iugov in effect went over to the Communists.” (Boris Sapir in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 376)

“Many prominent [Menshevik] members withdrew from political work or went over to the victors; for example, M. S. Ralabanov, F. A. Bulkin, V. Galuzin, K. A. Gvozdev, B. I. Gorev, V. G. Groman, D. Zaslavskii, O. A. Ermanskii, V. M. Korobkov, I. M. Maiskii, A. S. Martynov, A. M. Naumov-Ginzburg, A. A. Pleskov, I. I Rubin, S. lu. Semkovskii, A. A. Troianovskii, A. M. Fishgendler, L. M. Khinchuk, A. B. Stern — intelligenty and workers, of the Menshevik right and left. All these, and all the other old S-Ds who left the Party, must be taken into account in assessing the loss in potential replacements for disappearing leaders. Far from all of these defected out of fear. Some were honestly disappointed with menshevism, and the best were attracted by the seemingly limitless opportunities for applying their talents to the reconstruction of the state.” (Boris Sapir in Leopold Haimson (ed.), The Mensheviks : from the revolution of 1917 to the Second World War, p. 378)

“Lev Khinchuk, a prominent Menshevik defensist at the Second Congress of Soviets, found that he ‘‘could not sit with arms folded while a proletarian revolution was going on.” Khinchuk left the Mensheviks abruptly and, to their utter astonishment, published a statement affirming that he had long been disappointed with menshevism.” (André Liebich, From the other shore : Russian social democracy after 1921, p. 79)

Only the miserable traitors and idealists, liquidators and social-imperialists like Potresov, petit-bourgeois waverers like Martov, and dishonest frauds like Dan remained Mensheviks to the bitter end, though never firmly or with principle, but always wavering and scheming. They emigrated to the West where they collaborated with capitalists and White Guards, bickered amongst each other, lost all support and contact with the masses.

“Martov wrote that the Menshevik party experienced more defections to the Bolsheviks in the fall of 1918 than ever before. The Mensheviks were undergoing a profound crisis of identity… their efforts had led to the consolidation of “reactionary forces” that had only used the socialist-led protest movements for their own advantage.” (Mensheviks after October, p. 287)

Even among the right-SRs the honest and revolutionary minded people went over to the Bolsheviks:

“Late in 1920 a party delegation to the Urals discovered that there were only a handful of SRs still active in Ekaterinburg. The author of the delegation’s report explained that most SRs and former SRs… “have in a majority of cases attached themselves to Soviet institutions and try with all their effort to make not only others, but themselves, forget that they were ever SRs” (Smith, Captives of revolution : the socialist revolutionaries and the Bolshevik dictatorship, 1918-1923, p. 237)

“A few SRs, such as Vasilii Sukhomlin, even joined the trickle of postwar “returnees” to the Soviet Union.” (Smith, p. 238)

According to GPU reports by the mid 1920s most liberals who pretended to be SRs had abandoned politics, while “most [real] SRs had “left the party long ago and are not carrying out any party work. In the vast majority of cases, they have already reconsidered their previous ideology and long since become Soviet…” He concluded that the thousand former SRs represented at the congress [of ex SRs in 1923] comprised only a fraction of those who could be enrolled in the movement… The dominant mood” at the congress was “forward, into the ranks of the Communist Party.”” (Smith, pp. 262-263)
