December 22, 1934.

Interrogation record

Of ZINOVIEV, Grigory Evseevich

QUESTION: The investigation again demands of you, as at your interrogation of December 19, that you tell everything you know about the organization among former participants in the so-called Zinovievist opposition in Leningrad, about the Moscow center of this organization, and about your role in all this.

ANSWER: In 1927–1928 there was an underground organization of Zinovievists in Leningrad, existing parallel to the Party organization, with its own districts, connections and so forth. But then the elements of the organization dwindled away, and after 1929 there was no organization left. What remained in Leningrad of the former Zinovievist organization could initially regard those in Moscow—myself, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and others—as a certain “moral” center. But in reality there was no Moscow center at all.

What happened during these years?

After the Fifteenth Congress of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), in connection with the Rightists' action, I had hopes of a future regrouping within the Party, of a discussion. Among other things, that was also why I wanted the young people among the leaderless group (RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV and others) to return to the Party and leave the path they had taken after breaking with the “Zinovievists.” It is possible that when I was in Leningrad in 1929 there was a conversation with the persons named during the interrogation (RUMYANTSEV, MANDELSHTAM, LEVIN and others); if there was a conversation, it was in the sense of the record found in SHVALBE's possession.

There were exhortations by myself, KAMENEV and others: “Join the Party, preserve your strength, it will be needed for the struggle against the Rightists.”

In 1929 KAMENEV's hopes and mine that we would be “called upon” to participate in leading the Party did increase in connection with the Party's struggle against the Rightists. This was, of course, a whole tangle of sentiments. On the one hand, we were almost without work, or in work that did not satisfy us, and learned about Party policy from the newspapers; on the other hand, only the course of collectivization and the growth of industry created the decisive conditions for striking at the Rightists. My political line generally coincided with the Party line at this time. But at this time I did not want simply to follow the Central Committee's leadership as a rank-and-file member, but to negotiate with the Central Committee as a “power”; hopes of possible participation in the leadership still did not disappear.

Only against the background of such sentiments did the incident with STEN become possible. My role seemed too petty to me if I went to “inform” on this. I reasoned as follows: for almost thirty years I had known everything happening in the Party, and considered that I had the right to acquaint myself with any material, since it would go no further than me. Of course, I was wrong. I met leading officials of the Party and the Soviet government repeatedly, before going to Kaluga, after returning from there, and after returning from Kustanai. The comrades knew of the underground methods of struggle with which I had previously been connected, but nobody asked me: tell us who these people are, how they worked, name them! Somehow it was tacitly assumed that if I submitted politically, all that fell away.

Everyone knew that in 1928–1929 the persons mentioned in the testimony visited me and KAMENEV in Kaluga. Of course this was an almost open continuation of factional work. But after my return nobody asked me about it. I do not consider myself blameless. But the matter is not, after all, merely my individual traits; it is that a reasonably painless transition from my former role in the Party to the position in which I later found myself was difficult and almost impossible. Poisoned by awareness of the role I had played before, I tried, despite everything, to remain in the thick of politics and did not understand that after my struggle against the Party and my colossal political mistakes, only a transition to the position of a rank-and-file Party member was possible. I was not alone in this state.

To a greater or lesser degree this applies to the entire group of former leaders, the Leningrad “old-timers” who were in opposition with me. A whole little group of such people formed. This was based partly on personal connections, but chiefly on a common position and dissatisfaction with that position. The greatest outbreak of these sentiments among us after the Fifteenth Congress was the Rightists' action. “We said so, we warned the Party, we suffered for nothing,” and so forth: those were my sentiments and those of others then.

I did not understand that exposing BUKHARIN, RYKOV, TOMSKY and others as opportunists did not at all mean that I could be politically amnestied, much less elevated. I did not understand that on the question of how, when and by what methods the Rightists could be defeated, the Central Committee had proved entirely correct, rather than me. My assignment to Tsentrosoyuz naturally did not satisfy me; initially I thought it was a question of a few months before I would be summoned to leadership work. At first, in 1929, I was not the only one with such sentiments. Repeatedly I swore loyalty to the Party and, generally, fully shared the general line from 1929 onward, while I myself nevertheless retained until 1932 the psychology of someone who still feels himself somehow, at least partly, in the right.

That is the essential point.

Such sentiments could not be secret to the various opposition groups within the Party, who counted on these people still “showing themselves.”

The sentiments about which I testify above remained with me until 1932. Naturally, with such sentiments both the Party leadership and Comrade STALIN personally were abused. But since returning from Kustanai I have had no unhealthy sentiments. I met nobody, held no anti-Party conversations, know of no organization and likewise know nothing of the existence of a center.

QUESTION: To the record of your interrogation of December 19 you added a note that a number of persons, such as RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV, MANDELSHTAM and others about whom the investigation questioned you, had broken with the group of the former Leningrad opposition already after the Fourteenth Congress and then formed a special group of the “leaderless” or “Leftists.” Explain this to the investigation.

ANSWER: These persons, the investigation told me, are members of the center of the Leningrad organization to which I allegedly belonged. I considered that one argument against this could be to point out that these people had broken with me and those sharing my views. At the time of the break they were much closer to the Trotskyist group, with which we too had broken, than to the group to which I then belonged.

QUESTION: The testimony of RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV and MANDELSHTAM, to whom you refer in your addition to the record of December 19, establishes that the further struggle against the Party and the Soviet government conducted by the persons listed erased their hostile disagreements with you. Your own letter of June 13, 1928, addressed to RUMYANTSEV, confirms that the disagreements were eliminated and that this group of so-called “leaderless” people reestablished its connection with you.

What testimony can you give in this connection?

ANSWER: I never saw any of them again. My letter addressed to RUMYANTSEV confirms, on the contrary, the existence of disagreements (otherwise why would I have had to persuade him?), and since I know absolutely nothing about what happened to them afterward, what struggle they conducted, I can say nothing.

QUESTION: You say that these people split away after the Fourteenth Congress and that thereby contact automatically ceased, yet you establish contact with RUMYANTSEV again. The investigation notes an obvious inconsistency here.

ANSWER: You are asking about the letter. I am saying that I knew some of them personally… I then considered RUMYANTSEV a valuable person, a young worker who had lost his way. I tried to persuade him. There were no meetings with him afterward, and I was not interested in what he became later.

QUESTION: RUMYANTSEV interested you not in himself, but because he was connected with a group of young people. As appears from RUMYANTSEV's testimony, your letter to him was precisely intended to keep him and the group of young people connected with him under your influence. RUMYANTSEV's subsequent return to the Party likewise did not take place individually. He conducted negotiations with the former Central Control Commission jointly with TARASOV and KOTOLYNOV, who headed the youth group of former Zinovievist oppositionists. They were directed personally by KAMENEV, who incidentally edited their statement to the Central Control Commission, which, as they confessed, was duplicitous in character. In this connection your letter to RUMYANTSEV pursued clearly duplicitous aims. Do you confirm this?

ANSWER: Anything is possible; I admit that they may have acted duplicitously, but I called RUMYANTSEV into the Party without any ulterior motive. I persuaded many people to return to the Party.

QUESTION: Did you say anything to GORSHENIN about the Zinovievist organization existing in Leningrad, and when?

ANSWER: I might have said something in 1928, but if we are speaking of later events, then no. At the end of 1928 [1], I admit it.

QUESTION: GORSHENIN's testimony of December 19 has established that at the end of 1928 you told him of the existence in Leningrad of a clandestine organization of former members of the so-called Zinovievist opposition, headed by Vladimir LEVIN and Vladimir RUMYANTSEV. You described this organization to GORSHENIN as militantly disposed. Do you confirm this?

ANSWER: No, I cannot confirm it.

QUESTION: Where does GORSHENIN get this from, then?

ANSWER: There was no such moment at all. This did not happen even in the worst times of the anti-Party struggle.

QUESTION: On the basis of all the materials at the investigation's disposal and the materials of your interrogation, your organizational connection with LEVIN, RUMYANTSEV and others is completely obvious. The investigation establishes that not only with your knowledge but on your instructions an organization of former participants in the so-called Zinovievist opposition arose and took shape in Leningrad.

The investigation insists on a truthful answer about your actual role in creating an organization of former participants in the so-called Zinovievist opposition.

ANSWER: Today I said in detail that I had no organization and could not have had one, and did not participate in one; that at the turn of 1928–1929 there were indeed hopes of returning to leadership work in the Party, and on this basis there was a good deal of talk. My sentiments and my attitude to this are best reflected in the record later seized from SHVALBE [2], which is in your hands. There was hope of a new discussion; we even counted on the possibility that my comrades and I might be sent back to Leningrad to fight the Rightists. Sentiments in this sense could exist, but it was a long way from creating any organization.

QUESTION: Do you know Mikhail Semenovich REM?

ANSWER: I do.

QUESTION: REM's testimony of December 22 establishes that in 19<28> [3], on the question of the need for former participants in the so-called Zinovievist opposition to return to the Party, you personally told him: “The Bolsheviks in their time entered such a pigsty as the Duma. BADAEV kissed the cross, and you want to look squeaky-clean in the struggle.”

Do you confirm this conversation with REM, and acknowledge that it was a directive to return to the Party with the aim of deceiving the Party?

ANSWER: I do not remember the conversation with REM at all; I cannot even say now whether he was among those who did not want to return to the Party. As for my using such a comparison, comparing the Party with a pigsty, with kissing the cross—no, I think that even in the worst days of my anti-Party actions I did not do this.

QUESTION: In the record of your interrogation of December 19, in response to the investigation's question based on EVDOKIMOV's testimony, you answered that you had conducted no negotiations with anyone about drafting and submitting an anti-Party letter to the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) concerning the supposedly difficult economic situation in the country. BAKAEV's testimony of December 21 has established that in the summer of 1932 you personally discussed with him the expediency of drafting a collective letter to the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) about the supposed difficulties in the country and the need to demand that the Central Committee inform Party members about them.

The investigation asks you to give a truthful answer to this question.

ANSWER: This did not happen and could not have happened.

QUESTION: Do you maintain that there was no conversation with BAKAEV?

ANSWER: There was not and could not have been such a proposal on my part. Nor was there one because this would have been a method of 1927. I exclude it completely.

QUESTION: Did you see BRAVO in 1932, where exactly, and what was the nature of your conversation with him?

ANSWER: I saw him at Ilyinskoye. What I remember most was his talk about his impressions of the countryside (he had gone on a grain-procurement mobilization). These were typical conversations of that year: difficulties and so forth.

QUESTION: Did BRAVO pass you information about the situation in the countryside?

ANSWER: Why put it that way—“information”? He recounted his impressions of what he saw. I do not remember exactly where he had been.

QUESTION: Did you make an assessment of the situation in the countryside, and what exactly was it?

ANSWER: I do not remember. Probably I asked about his impressions.

QUESTION: BRAVO's testimony of December 19 has established that in August 1932 he and EVDOKIMOV visited you at your dacha at Ilyinskoye, where they found you with KAMENEV, KUKLIN and one other unknown person. As a result of your conversation with BRAVO, who worked in the Procurement Committee under the STO [4], you assessed the situation in the country negatively and in particular concluded that a general deterioration of the food situation in the country should be expected. Do you confirm this?

ANSWER: I cannot confirm it. What sort of expert am I in food difficulties? Of course, in 1932 the difficulties were serious.

QUESTION: What conclusions did you draw from this, then?

ANSWER: In 1932? What special conclusions could I draw? Probably I viewed it as everyone did: it would depend primarily on the future <prospects> of the harvest, and then on the successes of collectivization.

QUESTION: Describe the nature of your conversation with GORSHENIN in August 1932, after his return from a trip to Western Siberia.

ANSWER: I do not remember whether he went to Western Siberia; I have difficulty recalling the conversation.

QUESTION: GORSHENIN's testimony of December 21 has established that after returning from his trip to Western Siberia he informed you about its results. In conversation with GORSHENIN you expressed the opinion that the policy of industrialization and collectivization of the country was being carried out at enormous unproductive cost to the country, which was enduring unbearable privations that could have been avoided under different leadership.

Do you confirm this?

ANSWER: I recall that he spoke rather pessimistically about what he had seen, but I could in no circumstances have disparaged the policy of industrialization and collectivization on that account. In 1932 I did not and could not in general doubt the policy of industrialization and collectivization as pursued by the Central Committee. I did not understand individual measures, about which I shall speak separately.

QUESTION: GORSHENIN's testimony establishes that at the end of 1933 you personally explained to him the fascist coup in Germany and HITLER's accession to power by the incorrect policy of the Comintern and the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), which had facilitated HITLER's accession to power.

Do you confirm this?

ANSWER: I can in no circumstances confirm this. I wrote enough about the fascist coup and Hitler's accession to power; I was considered to a certain extent a specialist on this question and did indeed set out what I thought about it.

QUESTION: The testimony of the same GORSHENIN establishes that in 1934, after the Vienna uprising and during the events in Spain, you gave him a negative assessment of Comintern policy as incorrect and inflexible.

Do you confirm this?

ANSWER: I can refer to the fact that I wrote in the greatest detail about the Vienna uprising. This did not happen.

QUESTION: In the record of December 19 you spoke of your meeting with SAFAROV. Describe the place of your meeting with him, the nature of the conversation, and whether you proposed that SAFAROV establish systematic contact with you.

ANSWER: The conversation took place at Ilyinskoye; as for its nature, I remember he described a trip to one of the national republics, Kazakhstan I think; he spoke of the enormous excesses in collectivization there. He spoke about this in a very agitated tone. We said very little about the past, about the past struggle. Generally the conversation was saturated with the subjects of 1932, that is, difficulties. I admit that I might have suggested he visit me, but there was no talk of any “connection.”

QUESTION: SAFAROV's testimony of December 20 establishes that in the summer of 1932, when meeting you at your dacha at Ilyinskoye, you told him that the country felt anxiety in the face of difficulties, that elements of dissatisfaction were growing in the country, and spoke slanderously about the Party's orientation, the situation in the country and so forth.

What can you say about this?

ANSWER: Everything depends on the formulations. Of course the difficulties of 1932 were discussed, but not in the context that the policy as a whole was wrong—this did not happen. That particular reports from particular districts caused anxiety, that there were certain instances of bungling that the Party was correcting—this I admit; but to say that the leadership's policy was wrong—this did not happen. I do not mean by this that in 1932 I was altogether blameless. I had individual unhealthy sentiments, vacillations connected with the difficulties the country was then experiencing.

G. ZINOVIEV

[1] Erroneously 1923 in the text.

[2] Shvalbe, Filipp Petrovich, Kamenev's secretary. “F. Shvalbe returned to Moscow in June 1933 after exile in Minusinsk and was readmitted to the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in 1934. Unlike many Zinovievists who lost their lives, in December 1950 he was alive and even headed a workshop at a consumer-goods factory… Mikhail Shvalbe lived to see the Twentieth CPSU Congress.” (Panteleev, Mikhail. “Agents of the Comintern: Soldiers of World Revolution.”) However, in this instance G. Zinoviev means Filipp Shvalbe's brother, Mikhail Shvalbe, who, having been arrested for keeping secret Party documents in his home, revealed during interrogation a cache of documents hidden inside the hollow legs of a bed. Among these documents were letters from G. Zinoviev of July 13 and 14, 1928, in which he shared his thoughts with L. Kamenev after reading L. Kamenev's record of his conversation with N. Bukharin. For the letters see “How the NEP Was Broken,” vol. 4. International Democracy Foundation. Materik Publishers, Moscow, 2000, pp. 694–698.

[3] Here and later in this sentence the publisher refers to poorly legible text in the document where omissions occur. The corresponding testimony of M. Rem records: “Later, in January 1928, on the question of the need to enter the Party, ZINOVIEV personally told me…”

[4] Council of Labor and Defense: an extraordinary supreme body of the RSFSR and subsequently the USSR. It was responsible for directing economic construction and defense, guiding the activities of economic commissariats and departments. Established in April 1920, it was abolished by a resolution of the USSR Central Executive Committee of April 28, 1937, transferring its functions to the Economic Council and the Defense Committee under the USSR Council of People's Commissars.
