INTERROGATION RECORD

Of TIMOFEI DMITRIEVICH DMITRIEV,

December 24, 1934.

DMITRIEV T. D. — born in 1887, village of Degtyarka, Gzhatsk district, former Smolensk province; father a middle peasant. Russian, citizen of the USSR. Rostov-on-Don, Voroshilovsky Avenue, building No. 54/101, apartment 134. Head of the labor economics and personnel department of the Azov–Black Sea canning trust. Wife: Nadezhda Georgievna SLAVINA, secretary of the Agricultural Department of the Azov–Black Sea Territorial Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in Rostov, a party member. Salary. Elementary education. Party member since 1915. Expelled from the party by its Fifteenth Congress for factional work. Readmitted by the Central Control Commission in August 1928. Registered with the Rostov Military Commissariat. 8-a.

Question: What do you know about the existence of the Zinovievist organization?

Answer: The ideological and political defeat of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc did not, however, end the existence of the Zinovievist underground. The statements we submitted following our expulsion from the party by the Fifteenth Congress (myself included), beginning with renunciation of factional work, continuing with recognition of the correctness of the party line, and ending with condemnation of our positions of principle and pledges of loyalty to the party leadership — all this, from beginning to end, was a maneuver designed to return to the party, preserve the Zinovievist cadres and continue our counterrevolutionary work against the party under the new, changed conditions. These considerations guided us when, after our expulsion from the party, we drafted these statements in Moscow, chiefly at GERTIK's apartment. These propositions were formulated by ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, LASHEVICH, BAKAEV, GERTIK and others and transmitted as a directive of the Zinovievist center. After arriving in Leningrad (following the Fifteenth Congress), I conveyed this directive to Vladimir LEVIN, MYASNIKOV, SHURYGIN, KOSTRITSKY and others, who fully approved it.

Implementing this directive, working practically to consolidate our cadres, establishing connections among Zinovievists in Leningrad, Moscow and other cities, and bringing Zinovievist groups into contact with the Moscow leaders, particularly ZINOVIEV personally, by approximately 1929 we had reestablished the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization that existed until recently.

Question: Who belonged to the leading center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization in Leningrad?

Answer: The formation of the leading center of the Leningrad counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization dates to the period after the Fifteenth Congress. The center included Vladimir LEVIN, Nikolai MYASNIKOV, SHURYGIN and myself, DMITRIEV. REM and KOSTRITSKY were close to the center. Its meetings took place partly at my home (I was then living at the Astoria), but chiefly at Vladimir LEVIN's on Nadezhdinskaya Street.

In 1930 I was transferred to Rostov-on-Don. Living in Rostov, I continued to maintain written and personal contact with center members LEVIN and MYASNIKOV until 1934. From this contact I know that the Leningrad center still exists.

Question: What can you testify about the Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization and its connection with the Leningrad organization?

Answer: The Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization exists with the following membership: ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN and GERTIK. Until my departure from Leningrad (1930), the Leningrad organization's connection with the Moscow center was maintained chiefly through me and Vladimir LEVIN. During this period I traveled to Moscow twice and was received by ZINOVIEV: once at the Tsentrosoyuz and once at his apartment in Kaloshinsky Lane [1]. Upon returning from Moscow, I informed Vladimir LEVIN of the instructions received from ZINOVIEV, which chiefly amounted to the need to preserve cadres and accumulate strength.

My trips to ZINOVIEV were facilitated by our working in the same system: I at the Sevzapsoyuz in Leningrad, and he at the Tsentrosoyuz.

Question: What instructions did you receive from the Leningrad center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization before leaving for Rostov in 1930?

Answer: Before my departure from Leningrad for Rostov (this was in October 1930), LEVIN and I agreed to maintain personal and written contact. LEVIN told me that some active Zinovievists were in Rostov, particularly Nikolai GORDON, whom he recommended that I contact in order to develop work locally.

Question: Which members of the Moscow center did you see before leaving for Rostov, and what directives did you receive from them?

Answer: Before leaving for Rostov, I stopped in Moscow and visited center members ZINOVIEV and KUKLIN. I went to ZINOVIEV as an authoritative leader for advice and a directive. He received me at his apartment in Kaloshinsky Lane. I told him that I was being sent to work in Rostov. He expressed approval and advised me not to lose contact with him and to strengthen connections with like-minded people generally. I do not remember the details of his instructions, but their meaning was: “We must sit tight and wait.”

For the same purpose as my visit to ZINOVIEV, I subsequently visited KUKLIN at his apartment on Plyushchikha. He approved my move to Rostov and told me that a group of active Zinovievists was there (in Rostov): Nikolai GORDON, GLEBOV-AVILOV and PEKAR-ORLOV, whom he recommended that I contact on arrival. There and then KUKLIN and I agreed to maintain contact with Rostov through me.

I will give additional testimony about the Rostov group's activities in 1930–1934, its connection with the Moscow center and, additionally, the activities of the Leningrad counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization in 1934.

Correctly recorded from my words, read by me —

T. DMITRIEV.
