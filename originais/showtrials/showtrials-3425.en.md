24/XII/1934.

Copy

To Comrade Ya. S. AGRANOV.

Comrade AGRANOV!

You asked me to set out what I know about the activities of the so-called “Leningrad group” in recent years. This fully accords with my own desire. I must only stipulate that, since a number of facts date back many years and, on the other hand, I am deprived of any written documents, errors or confusion in the sequence of events may creep into my account. Please do not hold this against me. I write with complete sincerity and with the desire to set out everything I remember.

The question of the future conduct of the “Leningrad opposition” arose categorically toward the end of the Fifteenth Congress. TROTSKY and his group were effectively taking the road toward a second party. The Leningraders hesitated. I resolutely advocated ending all underground activity and submitting to the party. Heated arguments took place. Until then I had played a secondary role in determining opposition tactics, first because I had been absent from the USSR for many months (in Italy) and returned just before the congress. Second, because I held “Right” positions, while the entire opposition, under the influence of TROTSKY and ZINOVIEV, who had submitted to him, was in a “fighting,” “offensive” mood. The roles changed after the defeat at the congress. My “capitulationist” viewpoint clearly found sympathy. The first task was to “steady” ZINOVIEV, who clearly feared the prospect of a “second party,” yet at the same time could not bring himself to draw all the conclusions from this, feared losing influence among the “Lefts,” and therefore hesitated. He soon adopted my position, however. Looking back now, I of course understand that my position then meant submission to the party, rather than merging with it. I had not yet concluded that our political line was fundamentally wrong, only that defending it by the methods of factional organization and underground activity was hopeless and harmful. But even this viewpoint could not unite the “Leningraders.” A split occurred. SAFAROV, NAUMOV and SARKIS declared us “capitulationists” and “traitors.” Among the Trotskyists, only PYATAKOV adopted our viewpoint. I remember that my principal argument was the following: we had failed to change the party line in the direction we desired through open struggle, but if we remained in the party and submitted to it, we would undoubtedly eventually gain the opportunity to influence its policy. Once, in the heat of an argument with the “Lefts” and the Trotskyists, I said that after everything that had happened (I meant underground activity, taking to the streets, and so forth), I would consider it an entirely satisfactory prospect if the party listened to our advice, even without entrusting us with any official posts, and that for this purpose we must abandon the so-called “platform.” Within the opposition this theory received the ironic name of the “theory of unofficial advisers,” provoking indignation and mockery among the “Lefts.” I sincerely believed in it: that if we submitted to the party and renounced the platform, the party, encountering inevitable difficulties, would turn to us for help and eventually restore our role in it. It had not yet occurred to me that our political role in the party was irrevocably over. From this standpoint I then advocated a decisive break with “Lefts” of the SAFAROV–NAUMOV type, renunciation of the platform, and considered it useful for ZINOVIEV and myself to leave Moscow, giving the party a chance to forget our “exploits.”

Kaluga, where ZINOVIEV and I moved, became a center of visits by our like-minded associates. “Lefts” came who, after hesitation, had decided to join the “statement of the twenty-three”; Trotskyists came who had decided to break with TROTSKY; supporters of the “twenty-three” also came to learn how we assessed the situation. Among the visitors I remember EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, PYATAKOV, ZALUTSKY, FEDOROV, KAREV, KAPITONOV, BABAKHAN [1] and MAGYAR. We did not reject these visits. The chief subjects of conversation were analysis of the reasons for our defeat and defense of our position of “capitulation.” Our general mood, and mine in particular, can be depicted in the words of I. I. KHLESTAKOV, who expected couriers to arrive and say: “Ivan Ivanovich, please come and run the ministry” [2]. Personally, it seemed to me that the party could not manage without us and that, since we had renounced the platform and underground factional activity, there were no obstacles to this. The difficulties the party was experiencing sustained this conviction. Grain procurement was proceeding poorly, and I remember following economic developments, particularly grain procurement, very closely and compiling a whole study with figures and diagrams proving that things could not continue this way, that the kulak was not delivering grain to the state, and that some change of policy was required. I had no positive program. I no longer believed in the remedies featured in the “platform” (a grain loan, a horse for the horseless), but could devise nothing myself either. There was another important circumstance that sustained our conviction, and mine in particular, that the party would “summon” us. We avidly seized upon rumors of disagreements in the Central Committee brought by visitors. But these were all rumors and gossip, difficult to trust because they came second- or thirdhand. There was, however, one fact to which I attached more serious symptomatic significance. I no longer remember the time or occasion, but I remember that the Politburo had instructed BUKHARIN to edit one of our statements. At his direction I came (I do not remember whether alone or with ZINOVIEV) to his Kremlin apartment with the manuscript. That same morning, “Pravda” had carried a telegram from a district of Western Siberia stating that Comrade STALIN was there and that, on his instructions, strong pressure was being exerted on kulaks who were not delivering grain. During discussion of this document [3], which BUKHARIN was to edit (another Politburo member may also have been present, perhaps RYKOV; I do not remember clearly), I said: “Well, things with the kulak are not going as smoothly as you preach,” citing the telegram in “Pravda.” In response to my remark, BUKHARIN could not restrain himself and snapped with evident irritation: “That is all STALIN. We will not allow it.” It was said in passing, and my relations with BUKHARIN, who had just fiercely attacked us from the congress rostrum, were not such as to permit continuing a conversation on such subjects. But to me his words signaled some disagreements in the Central Committee and strengthened the hope that we might yet be returned to political life. It was therefore no complete surprise when, in Kaluga (in mid-1928), I received a note from SOKOLNIKOV summoning me to Moscow and, on arriving, learned that BUKHARIN wished to see me.

What BUKHARIN told me is known, and I will not repeat it here. But what he said seemed to open certain prospects of escaping the impasse in which we found ourselves. Neither I nor those around me believed in illegal, underground methods of struggling for influence over the party, and we had broken with those methods. We had not yet worked our way toward accepting the idea that we must become ordinary rank-and-file party members. Under these conditions, it seemed tempting to think that the Rightists' struggle against the Central Committee would induce the latter to expand the activities of each former oppositionist, and would once again bring us, ZINOVIEV and myself, who had only recently participated in leading the party, into leading work. Proceeding from this, ZINOVIEV began, both in conversation and even on paper, to construct all manner of combinations, including a new Politburo membership with us included. I regarded all this more coolly, considering it premature and idle. I knew that we had no power to influence the course of events and that our further participation in politics depended on one factor alone: whether the Politburo, and Comrade STALIN in particular, would consider it necessary and useful to enlist us in the struggle against the Rightists. I was therefore interested in how firm the Rightists were in their determination to pursue their own line. This was what I tried to clarify for myself when TOMSKY invited me to his dacha in the winter of 1928–29. This was my second and last meeting with the Rightists. Apart from these two meetings, during that period I saw RYKOV only a couple of times, approaching him on official business as chairman of the Council of People's Commissars. But during these meetings RYKOV was very reserved, entered into no general conversations and only laughed ironically: “What sort of chairman of the Council of People's Commissars am I! They keep me as long as they consider necessary, and soon they will throw me out altogether.” I found BUKHARIN at TOMSKY's. We had no frank conversation. I think this was because they sensed that I regarded them as people whose struggle could only clear the ground for the goal I sought, rather than as allies in the struggle. That goal, as I have already said, consisted in finding, on the basis of the Rightists' struggle against the Central Committee, a path and an opportunity to cooperate with the Politburo majority. This goal was dictated by three considerations: 1) Comrade STALIN's political line seemed in essence to me, to ZINOVIEV and to others much more correct than BUKHARIN's line, with its “kulak growing into socialism,” distrust of industrialization, and so forth; 2) despite all the clashes, irritations and “grievances,” I considered Comrade STALIN far more worthy of the post of party leader than any of the Rightists (I think this was not merely my personal opinion even then, but it was more pronounced in me because of my old friendship with Comrade STALIN); 3) I never believed in the Rightists' “victory.” There was no reason to think that BUKHARIN, RYKOV and TOMSKY would “defeat” the leadership that had just utterly defeated TROTSKY and ourselves. Of course I said none of this to TOMSKY and BUKHARIN, but I think they understood or sensed that approximately such was my position, and therefore nothing serious came of the conversation. On the main question that interested me, “How far do the Rightists intend to go in their struggle?”, I gained the impression that they intended to be very cautious and wished at all costs to retain their places on the Central Committee. I know that ZINOVIEV also met TOMSKY. He gained the same impression.

Soon the Rightists were definitively defeated. It also became clear that the Central Committee had needed none of our help to defeat them ideologically and organizationally, and that our position had not changed one iota as a result. For me this was a decisive moment. I understood that the leadership which had utterly defeated, in succession, the Trotskyists, ourselves and the Rightists had finally, so to speak, “stabilized”; that there was no hope of our group returning to leading work as a result of an internal party “brawl”; that these hopes must finally be abandoned and an attempt made to return to the party in reality as well as formally, by gradually earning the leadership's trust. I no longer attached any serious importance to individual groups and little groupings (“Leftist,” “Right-Leftist,” and the like), regarding them as bubbles appearing on the surface of the water where a great and serious whirlpool had recently been. I described my meetings with STEN, SHATSKIN [4] and LOMINADZE during interrogation. In 1930, after the Sixteenth Congress, I no longer nourished hopes of returning to politics, took less and less interest in so-called “internal party questions,” since in our milieu they increasingly assumed the character of empty and fruitless “gossip,” and increasingly concentrated my attention on literature, wishing to retreat into this field from “politics.” I continued living in that milieu partly from inertia and partly because there was no other. Party members justifiably avoided us.

At this time ZINOVIEV's personal qualities played a fatal role. He continued to enjoy enormous authority among the Leningraders.

ZINOVIEV and his line of conduct, or rather his absence of any line, had a fatal significance for this group. Long observation allows me to say that ZINOVIEV is characterized by lack of will, lack of consistency, and an ability, under the influence of chance impressions, now to become fired with unfounded hopes and optimism, now to fall into the gloomiest pessimism. In all these years he never once demonstrated an ability to think a situation through, make a firm decision and carry it out. Just as in his writings he always proceeds from “quotations” rather than independent consideration of a question, so in his actual conduct a fact of the day, a chance meeting or conversation, can change his assessment of the situation. The best formula for his behavior during these years is: “He wants to, but it stings, and Mama won't let him.” He wanted to reconcile with the party, yet was stung by the knowledge that henceforth he could play only the role of a rank-and-file member. He understood the danger of preserving old connections with his like-minded associates, yet feared being left “alone.” He acknowledged the correctness of party policy, yet one sensed resentment that this policy was being carried out without him. Hence his readiness to seek weak points and vulnerabilities in every party measure. Hence his avid attention to all rumors and “political” gossip, his willingness to hear out anyone who approached him, and his inability to give a precise, decisive and categorical answer to doubts and vacillations. This must have had a corrosive effect on those who listened to him. I personally had long since ceased to do so, knowing from experience how easily his opinions changed and how much they always contained that was personal, subjective and a passing mood. From the moment we no longer shared a common political goal, the relations established between us weighed upon me. We have different characters, different tastes, different ways of life and different attitudes toward people. I knew and felt that I could more easily make the journey of a real return to the party alone, rather than arm in arm with ZINOVIEV. But probably for that very reason I considered it indelicate and ungrateful to abandon him and go my own way. I acknowledge that this was foolish and politically harmful, and now bear responsibility for it. His behavior toward me in connection with the “Ryutin” document and the subsequent exile to Minusinsk freed my hands in relation to ZINOVIEV. These facts are known to the party, and I will not repeat them. But even before leaving for Minusinsk I learned that, terrified of responsibility, he had not bothered to absolve me of that responsibility, though he could easily have done so. Already in Minusinsk another circumstance became clear to me. When I left for Minusinsk he was in hospital. We then had a conversation about whether we should submit statements to the Central Committee before departure. We agreed that this would be fruitless at that moment and therefore should not be done. I left without submitting a statement, and only in Minusinsk, four or five months later, did I learn that he had submitted one, without telling me a word, thus placing me in a false position before the party. But independently of these facts, and even before I learned of them, our relations were such that when he asked me, in choosing a place of exile, to seek to have us settled together (as in Kaluga), I decided under no circumstances to pursue this and told the comrades responsible directly that I did not want to be exiled to the same town as ZINOVIEV. From Minusinsk I corresponded with him hardly at all. On returning from Minusinsk, I decided to break all personal connections with my former associates in the Leningrad opposition and carried this out. To my great sorrow and misfortune, I did not break personal connections with ZINOVIEV. My desire was nevertheless so evident that, after my return to Moscow, Olga RAVICH came to me with a special request: to support ZINOVIEV and not break with him; she cited his illness, loneliness and the like. Partly under this influence, partly because of everyday circumstances, and partly because, after his and my speeches at the Seventeenth Congress, I no longer saw any political danger in associating with him (which, as I have only now become convinced, was an enormous mistake), I did not break my personal connections with him. This was a fatal error, a weakness for which I am now paying so terribly.

In any event, I confirm once again that from the moment of my return to the party I had nothing in common with the former Leningrad opposition, avoided meetings and conversations with its former members, and knew absolutely nothing about the “Leningrad group.” I considered that I had emerged from the filthy swamp of oppositions and antiparty struggle and was proceeding firmly along my chosen road of honest work in the sector entrusted to me by the party, hoping to regain its trust. Now, when everything has already been decided and nothing can be changed, I wish to state that since my arrival from Minusinsk I have not, by deed, word or thought, betrayed a single word of what I wrote to the Central Committee and Comrade STALIN. I wrote sincerely then, spoke just as sincerely at the Seventeenth Congress and honestly fulfilled the commitments I had undertaken, and it seemed to me that a straight road lay before me. Now, when everything has turned to dust and everything is hopeless, I state once again: I did not play with words; in my letters from Minusinsk to the Central Committee and Comrade STALIN, in my articles in “Pravda” and in my speech at the Seventeenth Congress, I expressed all my thoughts fully and without reserve, and have not changed them one iota since.

24/XII/1934.

L. KAMENEV

Certified true: A. Svetlova

Comrade AGRANOV, I was hurrying, and the result is uneven. Perhaps something should be added. I shall gladly do so if necessary. Please summon me.

[1] In the text, erroneously: “Babakhar.”

[2] L. Kamenev is mistaken, for some reason calling this Gogol character Ivan Ivanovich instead of Ivan Alexandrovich. However, in prison, awaiting the swing of the “proletarian axe,” such an error is entirely forgivable.
