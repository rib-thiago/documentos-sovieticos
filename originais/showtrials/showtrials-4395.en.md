INTERROGATION RECORD

Of BAKAEV, Ivan Petrovich, dated January 10–11, 1935.

QUESTION: Name the principal facts of the activity of the Moscow center of the Zinovievist organization throughout its existence.

ANSWER: I shall really be able to indicate only the principal facts of the center's activity, insofar as my memory permits. The entire period from 1923 to 1934 was so abundant in events and actions in which I had to participate that it is impossible to remember everything.

The task of all our work during the period under consideration was gradually to gather and preserve the old ties with f\<ormer\> participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc, wherever they might be, around ZINOVIEV and ourselves, the narrow group of people who put his ideas into practice.

We carried out this task through illegal meetings and gatherings and by disseminating information, counterrevolutionary in character, about the activities of the party, the Comintern and the Soviet government.

We exaggerated the real difficulties of socialist construction; we portrayed the most important positive facts of the economic activity of the party and government as manifestations of the party leadership's inadequacy, pointing to the gravest consequences to which they would lead; on this basis we expected to be brought into the leadership.

Whether you take our discussions of disagreements within the CC of the VKP(b)—a subject with which we systematically concerned ourselves throughout our activity—or other questions, they were all permeated with this idea.

The foundation of the Zinovievist organization was laid at a meeting in ZINOVIEV's apartment held literally on the second day after the Fifteenth Congress of the VKP(b). As far as I remember, besides ZINOVIEV, those present were KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, LASHEVICH, myself—BAKAEV—FEDOROV, SHAROV, KUKLIN, GERTIK, GESSEN, NAUMOV. We summed up the congress, recognized that its decisions provided half-measures in resolving the questions posed in our factional documents, that they had been adopted under our pressure and as a result of a compromise between the CC majority and the group of BUKHARIN, RYKOV and others, that difficulties with grain and a number of other national economic questions were approaching, that further struggle within the party was inevitable, and that it was necessary to return to the party to participate in that struggle.

There and then the composition of the center took shape which, with minor changes, existed until recently. These were ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, myself—BAKAEV—FEDOROV, SHAROV, KUKLIN, GERTIK, LASHEVICH—until his death—KHARITONOV—until 1932; GORSHENIN later became a participant in the center.

Also in 1928 a meeting of center members took place at ZINOVIEV's apartment, at which we discussed the CC of the VKP(b)'s decision to expel TROTSKY from the territory of the Union. I reacted to the decision by demanding that we protest against the expulsion; I do not rule out that it was I who interjected: “Well, we are not going to wait until they start hanging people.” The other center members present at the meeting—ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, I believe EVDOKIMOV, KHARITONOV—spoke against my proposal, and the matter ended there.

Next comes the so-called Kaluga period in the organization's work—the end of 1928 and apparently part of 1929. F\<ormer\> oppositionists from various towns and localities began making their way to ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, who were in Kaluga; I and, I believe, ZALUTSKY visited them. In Moscow, where I arrived from Vyatka, where I had permanent work, I met BOGDAN, ZINOVIEV's secretary, who brought me together with ZINOVIEV, then in Moscow, and with him I went to Kaluga, where KAMENEV met us. I had a number of conversations with ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV on current political questions in the spirit of our customary attacks on the leadership of the VKP(b) and the government, whose content I find difficult to recall now.

After the return from Kaluga to Moscow I know that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV organized two seminars: a political and an economic one, the first led by ZINOVIEV and the second by KAMENEV, at which questions of party policy were discussed in the terms customary among us; only our own people attended the seminars, and their aim was to prevent our like-minded associates from drifting away from politics; I know that these seminars did not last long.

In 1929 LASHEVICH's funeral took place in Leningrad; a small group—ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, myself—BAKAEV—KUKLIN and someone else among our comrades, I do not remember exactly who—received permission from the CC of the VKP(b) to travel to Leningrad and attend the funeral. ZINOVIEV told me that he had used this trip to Leningrad to meet a whole series of like-minded associates; at SHAROV's apartment he held a meeting with the young people who remained loyal to him; in addition he conversed with a number of other persons.

In 1928 and for a considerable part of 1929 I lived in Vyatka, as I have testified. Frequently, on notification from comrades, I went to the station and met individual participants in the organization passing through Vyatka on their way to the Urals, Siberia and the Far Eastern Territory; I usually met them with KOSTINA. When LASHEVICH was going to Harbin to work, he notified me by telegraph to meet him at the station. From LASHEVICH I received information on a number of political questions in our particular interpretation. In the same manner I met ROTSKAN, who was going to Khabarovsk, as well as a number of other comrades. During this period I corresponded with a number of political friends; I wrote to KORSHUNOV, corresponded with ROTSKAN and EVDOKIMOV, who was then in Ulyanovsk, wrote to PEKAR-ORLOV in Novosibirsk, and also to ZINOVIEV.

I visited Moscow once every two months; I usually stayed at GERTIK's apartment, called on ZINOVIEV, KUKLIN, SHAROV and other comrades; I received information from them on the situation in the country, the party and the Comintern, discussing party policy together with them.

To this period belong the negotiations of center participants with various anti-party groupings for the purpose of cooperation in the struggle against the party and, consequently, against Soviet power as well.

The first meeting with the Right took place in 1928; KAMENEV and BUKHARIN took part; it was held at BUKHARIN's apartment. BUKHARIN told KAMENEV about disagreements within the Politburo, spoke sharply against STALIN and was clearly seeking KAMENEV's sympathy. ZINOVIEV told me of this conversation between KAMENEV and BUKHARIN during one of our meetings in Moscow; KAMENEV recounted it in more detail in Kaluga when I went there. KAMENEV told me that ties with the Right had to be maintained to obtain information on what was happening within the Politburo; I asked: “What, is he proposing a bloc?” KAMENEV replied: “There is no talk of a bloc yet.”

The second meeting with the Right, also in 1928, was between ZINOVIEV and BUKHARIN; ZINOVIEV personally told me about it; it took place at BUKHARIN's apartment. BUKHARIN spoke in detail about the growing disagreements within the CC of the VKP(b), made sharp attacks on STALIN, said that he had been removed from work in the IKKI and that ZINOVIEV's group was now in a more favorable position than BUKHARIN and his associates, who, he said, were being criticized mercilessly. ZINOVIEV replied to BUKHARIN in sympathetic terms.

I asked ZINOVIEV whether a bloc was being discussed; he denied it and said that “apparently things are so difficult for them that they are looking for someone to whom they can pour out their feelings; they regard us as beaten and are certain that these conversations will not become known to the party”; ZINOVIEV confirmed that ties with the Right had to be maintained for information.

The third meeting with the Right, in 1929, took place at TOMSKY's or SHMIDT's dacha in Moscow; KAMENEV and TOMSKY and, I believe, SHMIDT took part; whether there were other participants I cannot now say—I do not remember. I have information about this meeting from ZINOVIEV. TOMSKY complained about the party leadership and STALIN, said that their relations with the CC majority had become extremely strained and that matters were heading toward an open denouement. I do not now remember KAMENEV's reply to TOMSKY; I assume it was given in the same sympathetic terms as above. ZINOVIEV's summary regarding the meeting was: “Our group will have to play a significant role in this conflict.”

The fourth meeting with the Right occurred in the same year, 1929, in Sochi; ZINOVIEV, TOMSKY and FEDOROV Grigory were present. I learned of this meeting from FEDOROV, after which I approached ZINOVIEV for confirmation. ZINOVIEV said that in Sochi he and FEDOROV had met TOMSKY; that TOMSKY pointed to the Right's difficult position, expressed sympathy for ZINOVIEV and his group and said that the situation could not improve without a change in the balance of forces within the Central Committee; that the Right had decided to begin an open struggle against STALIN and his supporters; that ZINOVIEV and his group should support the Right if they still wanted to participate in the country's political life. ZINOVIEV replied to TOMSKY that he could give no answer without first consulting his comrades.

ZINOVIEV told me I had arrived at an opportune moment and that TOMSKY's proposal had to be discussed. The meeting took place at ZINOVIEV's apartment; as far as I remember, besides ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, those present were FEDOROV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, SHAROV, GESSEN, myself—BAKAEV. FEDOROV gave the report and ZINOVIEV supplemented it. It was decided to reject TOMSKY's proposal. ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV behaved indecisively; ZINOVIEV advanced considerations for and against the bloc and refrained from expressing his own viewpoint. I was left with the impression that our leaders were not satisfied with the meeting's decision. Toward the end of the meeting ZINOVIEV said: “Let them fight it out among themselves, and then we shall see.” I remember that Grigory FEDOROV was to convey our decision to TOMSKY.

QUESTION: Did you tell any of your like-minded associates about the negotiations with the Right, apart from the persons who participated in the meeting?

ANSWER: I told KOSTINA Anna about the first meeting with the Right and about the last meeting. Whether I told anyone else, I cannot recall now.

It is entirely obvious that all these meetings and negotiations with the Right can be explained only by the utter lack of principle of our group and the leaders' constant readiness to barter their ideas provided they could achieve a leading position in the party.

During the opposition period and later we always boasted that we were irreconcilable fighters against the Right and that we fought for the purity of Leninist principles above all on this point.

In practice the opposite picture emerged: we were actually pushing the Right toward struggle against the party and thereby supported their ideas of capitalist restoration in the name of carrying out our plans.

A continuation of this profoundly unprincipled attempt at cooperation with anti-party groupings was the negotiations conducted in 1931–1932 by EVDOKIMOV with the leftist Lazar SHATSKIN; the negotiations took place in Moscow at the “Paris” hotel, where both lived; I received information about this from EVDOKIMOV. L. SHATSKIN, in a conversation with EVDOKIMOV on political questions, spoke in sharply anti-party terms about party policy; EVDOKIMOV replied in the same terms and did not conceal from him his political character or the group to which he belonged; from EVDOKIMOV I know that in these negotiations SHATSKIN spoke on behalf of the leftists.

SHATSKIN proposed establishing a mutual exchange of information. EVDOKIMOV reported this proposal to ZINOVIEV in my presence; the latter approved EVDOKIMOV's actions and instructed him to maintain ties with SHATSKIN.

In 1932, while in Gagra, KAMENEV also negotiated with the leftists L. SHATSKIN and LOMINADZE, which I know from KAMENEV himself. I do not now remember the content of the conversation; I know it was anti-party in character. KAMENEV told me that he had arranged an exchange of information with the leftists and considered it correct and necessary; it is entirely obvious that the proposal to exchange information essentially veiled a proposal for a bloc.

Apart from these sources, ZINOVIEV was well acquainted with LOMINADZE's political attitudes; ZINOVIEV told me he knew from STEN of LOMINADZE's anti-party attitudes and that the latter disagreed with a number of the party's most important decisions on domestic policy questions.

In 1932 EVDOKIMOV informed me of a meeting with SMIRNOV I.N. and a conversation with him of an anti-party character; EVDOKIMOV said he supposed that the Trotskyists were seeking rapprochement with the Zinovievists and that SMIRNOV had proposed that he meet MRACHKOVSKY.

This meeting took place on the day of MRACHKOVSKY's departure from Moscow, at the station in a railway carriage, but the conversation did not take place, either because MRACHKOVSKY did not want to begin it or because there were many people in the carriage whom EVDOKIMOV did not know.

I know of one more attempt by our group to establish ties with anti-party groupings. I mean ZINOVIEV's negotiations with STEN and his obtaining from him the so-called Ryutin platform; on ZINOVIEV's part these negotiations were in the nature of probing and obtaining information about an anti-party, essentially counterrevolutionary formation. Of course, the negotiations with STEN were no accident, since they followed entirely from our practice of seeking ties with anti-party groups.

What do all these attempts at blocs, establishment of ties and exchanges of information with disparate anti-party and counterrevolutionary groups over more than four years mean?

All this, more decisively than any other proof, testifies to the unheard-of lack of principle of our group and to the greatest depth of our fall, we leaders of the Zinovievist organization.

I want to go back a little in order to continue my account in answer to the question put to me at the beginning of the record.

In the years 1929, 1930 and 1931 our group led an active political life, discussing emerging questions of domestic and foreign policy in a spirit hostile to the party.

I regularly visited ZINOVIEV at his apartment; constant visitors were EVDOKIMOV, SHAROV, KHARITONOV, KUKLIN, GERTIK, FEDOROV Gr\<igory\>, GORSHENIN; sometimes a certain GRINBERG visited in our presence, supplying ZINOVIEV with a variety of literature; at one time he had been secretary either to ZINOVIEV or to KAMENEV.

I participated in the celebration of New Year 1930 or 1931 at KAMENEV's apartment; I was in Moscow on an official trip from Vyatka. There were all sorts of people, predominantly our like-minded associates; the gathering had a particular character, although there were no political speeches or statements, only hints directed at the CC. KAMENEV proposed a toast “to better times”; the gathering aimed to bring our cadres closer together, rallying them around the old banners. I retained nothing more definite in my memory from that gathering; the money for holding it was provided by KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV; besides ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, SHAROV, KUKLIN, GERTIK, GORSHENIN, myself—BAKAEV—and a number of other persons were present.

Since May 1931 I have lived continuously in Moscow; moving to Moscow helped establish even closer ties between myself and the comrades in the organization. I can name a number of participants in our organization in 1928, 1929 and 1930. They include NALIVAIKO Foma; ALEKSANDROV Aleksandr, an active Komsomol member who recently worked at Lenoblsnab; RUTENBURG, director of the Leningrad branch of the Thermal Engineering Inst\<itute\>, who was once an active oppositionist; LEVIN Mikhail, who was linked with GERTIK; MUSHTAKOV Andrei, who visited ZINOVIEV and EVDOKIMOV; TSVIBAK, whose first name I do not remember, who taught social sciences in Leningrad and was close to EVDOKIMOV, GERTIK and KUKLIN—TSVIBAK is a person with firm anti-party attitudes; YAKOVLEV Moisei; RUSANOV, Ivan Mikhailovich. In 1928, 1929 and later SHLYAKHOV, he\<ad\> of the Leningrad branch of Gidroelektroproekt, belonged to the organization; an old, staunch Zinovievist, he visited ZINOVIEV together with REM.

I recall a remark by ZINOVIEV, either in 1931 or 1932, in connection with the publication in the newspapers of a number of government transfers. ZINOVIEV said: “If we were in power, I would work in the Comintern, Lev Borisovich in the Sovnarkom, Aleksandr Sergeevich (KUKLIN) would be our SHVERNIK, and you, Ivan Petrovich, would be our GPU or TsKK.”

Although ZINOVIEV said these words with a chuckle, it was clear that he had not said them without reason: they indicated to us, his like-minded associates, what prospects awaited us and, on the other hand, testified to the plans he was nurturing.

As is known, the Seventeenth Party Conference adopted a decision to raise the material standard of living of working people two to three times. From GORSHENIN I know how ZINOVIEV assessed this decision: he considered it impracticable and demagogic.

In 1932 ZINOVIEV had a conversation with me about submitting a statement to the CC on behalf of the group concerning the difficulties the country was experiencing. Assessing the state of affairs in the country, ZINOVIEV pointed to particular problems with the progress of grain procurements and workers' supplies, and to the fact that the party did not have correct information about the real state of affairs.

ZINOVIEV said: “I have talked here with some comrades; they propose sending a letter to the CC requesting that the party be given detailed information.” I disagreed with ZINOVIEV, pointing out that party circles knew of the situation and that submitting a statement on behalf of our group would be regarded as a group action; this ended the episode of submitting the statement.

Recorded correctly from my words, much has been read —

BAKAEV.

INTERROGATED BY:

Assistant Head of the EKO of the GUGB of the NKVD of the USSR — DMITRIEV.
