INTERROGATION RECORD

Of GORBACHEV, Georgy Yefimovich,

dated January 4, 1935.

In response to the questions put to me by the investigation about the practical activities of the Zinovievist organization, I additionally testify:

The task of the Zinovievist organization was counterrevolutionary struggle against the party and its leadership, that is, thereby also against Soviet power. Defeated on the front of direct political struggle, with no opportunities for open action there, the Zinovievist organization became intensely interested in various sectors of the theoretical front.

For those of us (Zinovievists) who worked specifically on the ideological front, an opportunity appeared to avenge our general defeat on that front. In the milieu with which I had contact, the solidarity of the Zinovievists with one another in hostility toward the party leadership and in the struggle against it found vivid expression. But even those Zinovievists who had not previously dealt with particular theoretical questions displayed an interest in them after the 15th Congress and throughout all the subsequent years. This includes ZINOVIEV’s interest in literature, which he displayed in conversations with me in 1928 and 1929, not only in terms of rallying writers around him, ZINOVIEV (the proposal to publish through Tsentrosoyuz books on cooperative subjects by writers connected with me), but also in terms of interest in the progress of the struggle on the literary studies front, particularly my statements against PEREVERZEV and the RAPP leadership, with the aim of using all this in the struggle against the party. When, after the defeat of the Menshevizing idealists-Zinovievists, KAREV began working at the Academy of Sciences, M. YAKOVLEV and I organized his seminar for the study of Hegel, accompanied by KAREV’s constant mockery, with our support, of the new philosophical leadership; that is, we used the seminar to struggle against the party line on the theoretical front (1930–1931). Members of our organization, the economist YAKOVLEV and the historian TATAROV, became intensely interested in the literary struggle, giving me advice and instructions and intervening (TATAROV) directly in literary polemics, that is, pursuing the general Zinovievist line here.

All this demonstrates that the Zinovievists put themselves forward as a cohesive group in various sectors of the ideological front, with mutual protection and responsibility for one another.

On these questions the Zinovievists generally adopted anti-party positions (KAREV in philosophy; myself, organizing together with LELEVICH and VARDIN the “Napostovist Left,” that direct literary reflection of the Zinovievist political line, or organizing “Litfront” as a literary bloc of the Zinovievists with the Right-Leftist group). We Zinovievists considered our people in every sector of the theoretical front representatives of truly revolutionary and progressive thought, vanguard fighters for a supposedly correct political line against genuinely party-minded thought on the question, which from our point of view was official and stereotyped. It is characteristic that, considering themselves the Left in the party and fighters against the Right deviation and bourgeois influences, the Zinovievists also acted supposedly “from the Left” in every field of literary, historical, and other ideology, “signaling” bourgeois influences and fighting the Right (the “Napostovist Left,” “Litfront,” statements by TSVIBAK, TATAROV, and NOTMAN on theoretical questions).

In recent years the central point in the struggle against the party by Zinovievists working on the ideological front became hostile criticism of the line of the Culture and Propaganda Department of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), both in its general party orientation and in individual aspects of its work.

The starting point in our struggle against the party was our slanderous assertion that, in the ideological field, the party line was incorrect, uneven, stifling initiative and creative thought; that the party smashed valuable cultural forces at sharp and unexpected turns; and that all this had supposedly led to the degradation of theoretical thought in the country. These propositions were propagated by myself, LELEVICH, YAKOVLEV, TATAROV, TSVIBAK, and VORONSKY (1931–34).

Specifically, these counterrevolutionary positions manifested themselves in the Zinovievists’ practice in various ideological fields.

This activity proceeded from our tactic: beaten in politics, we would supposedly show what we could do in our specialized fields.

This was the application, in a new and necessarily narrower field, of the Zinovievists’ tactic after the 15th Congress: winning leading positions for their own people, that is, counterrevolutionary struggle for their own leadership against the party leadership.

Facts relating to this:

My transformation, in 1930–31, of the Institute of Modern Russian Literature of the Academy of Sciences into a base and refuge for Litfront members, with M. YAKOVLEV’s support in the Academy of Sciences collective; conversations between myself and YAKOVLEV, myself and TATAROV, about tactics in Litfront, in which a policy was developed for LELEVICH and me to seize influence in Litfront.

My assessment, in a conversation with SAFAROV and YAKOVLEV at the latter’s apartment in the autumn of 1934, of the writers’ congress as predominantly a “parade,” with speeches about universal literary achievements in the absence of serious debates on creative questions or criticism of the shortcomings of contemporary literature. Both my interlocutors expressed solidarity with this typically Zinovievist counterrevolutionary assessment of the congress as official and loudly proclaiming exaggerated achievements.

Numerous conversations in various combinations of interlocutors (YAKOVLEV, myself, LELEVICH, TSVIBAK, TATAROV), especially during the discussion of Comrade STALIN’s letter to the editors of “Proletarian Revolution” and, in particular, in connection with my expulsion from the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) for Trotskyist contraband, to the effect that the party leadership employed the method of “beating people over the head”; that this led to the disorganization of theoretical thought, to a desire to remain theoretically silent; that its unexpectedness, sharpness, and political and organizational conclusions corrupted young people, fostering the emergence of opportunists who did not care whom they struck or whom they raised on a pedestal, not only in particular but also in the most general political questions.

In literature we continued our struggle against the party leadership even after the liquidation of RAPP. When the party leadership, having liquidated RAPP, took all literary policy directly into its hands, we (myself and LELEVICH) began with renewed vigor to discredit the new leadership, describing it as bureaucratic, official, and weakening the political sharpness of literary creation. I conveyed these propositions in conversations with TSVIBAK, YAKOVLEV, and TATAROV, thereby also contributing here to discrediting and disrupting the party line. For us Zinovievists, the slogan of “party leadership” in various ideological fields meant, above all, an attempt to use it in the interests of our counterrevolutionary organization.

Written by me in my own hand — GORBACHEV.
