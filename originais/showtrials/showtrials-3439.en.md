INTERROGATION RECORD

Conducted by the head of the Fourth Section of the SPO — L. KOGAN, 25/XII/1934.

ZILBERMAN Aleksandr Ilyich, born in 1900, member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) since 1917. Assistant manager of the “Soyuzkhimmontazh” trust, Moscow.

In response to the investigation's questions, I testify:

How could it happen that I, who considered myself an honest party member, found myself in the dock alongside White Guard–fascist terrorists? Was this accidental, and who is to blame?

I answer with complete certainty: it was not accidental, and I myself am to blame.

To show how this happened, I must begin my confession with the Fifteenth Party Congress.

When ZINOVIEV and his group submitted a statement to the congress, the group of the so-called Leningrad “Left” did not follow Zinoviev. It considered: 1) that ZINOVIEV continued to maneuver, which it considered no longer permissible; 2) that the party congress resolutions were an unstable zigzag to the left — the entire Leningrad opposition had held this view before the congress, and ZINOVIEV had convinced us of it; 3) that our road did not lie with TROTSKY, since, having been expelled from the party, any activity outside it would lead to the formation of a second party and open counterrevolution. I too belonged to this group. When SAFAROV and SARKIS were exiled, several people remained in Moscow (myself, KOTOLYNOV, TARASOV, A. ALEKSANDROV, OSINSKAYA, MAGYAR and DOMBSKY), who formed a single group (albeit with internal contradictions), maintained written contact with SAFAROV, SARKIS, NAUMOV and VUYOVICH, and direct contact with the Leningrad group of the “leaderless.” Circumstances made me the center of this group. Our orientation was against factional struggle and in favor of waiting, since, supposedly, party policy was a maneuver and one could not maneuver with classes in this fashion, so sooner or later a situation would arise in which we would enter the party without renouncing our views. On this basis we devised naive, homegrown theories, corresponded, exchanged opinions and informed ourselves about the situation among the Zinovievists and Trotskyists, although we considered it impermissible to enter into any political relations with them. During this period I myself wrote a number of letters to SAFAROV, SARKIS and NAUMOV and received replies containing the same homegrown theories. We discussed the replies in the group mentioned above.

Soon, however, the utter worthlessness of this position and the complete fallacy of the basic thesis concerning the party's “maneuver” became clear to me. Others reached this conclusion too, and we firmly decided that our road led back into the party. The Moscow group reached this decision earlier, and we, particularly A. ALEKSANDROV and I, traveled to Leningrad to persuade the Leningrad group, which contained many opponents of this course. During this same period A. ALEKSANDROV, RUMYANTSEV and others visited Comrade YAROSLAVSKY several times, informing him of our views, our decision to request admission to the party and our independent path of returning (we continued to consider Zinoviev's path insincere). On his advice we returned to the party and drafted statements, in whose editing I participated. We sent this statement to SAFAROV so that he would submit it together with us. It turned out that, without waiting for or informing us, he had submitted a statement himself, only afterward sending it to us with a recommendation to join his statement. We did not do so. We submitted our own statements to the Central Control Commission, and when SAFAROV returned we received him coldly; I saw him once or twice and have not met him since, because I thought that SAFAROV, as a leader, could not act this way, abandoning us, politically weak as we were, to our fate.

When I and the others were readmitted, our group naturally broke up.

ZINOVIEV, whom I saw during this period, told me: “You see, I was right; we should have submitted together.” During this period I resumed my visits to GERTIK, whose apartment I visited fairly often. Political conversations were constantly going on there; one person or another would arrive, political “news” and gossip would be passed on, all sorts of combinations devised, and so forth.

I was no longer entirely “one of their own,” but a renegade. This on the one hand, and the evolution of my views in another direction on the other, led me to visit less and less often. Soon I left Moscow, and when I returned a couple of years later, I continued occasionally to visit GERTIK. Among other former Zinovievists, I visited V. LEVIN and BASHKIROV when coming to Leningrad, and NAUMOV and BLINKOV in Moscow. I met the others only by chance, in the street or at the theater (once I met ZINOVIEV at the theater, but there was no political conversation). As for NAUMOV and BLINKOV, they had broken personal relations with the Zinovievist milieu. (BLINKOV completely, while NAUMOV, I believe, met only SAFAROV, who was hostile to ZINOVIEV during this period.)

Each lived his own party and working life — so it seemed to me, but I was profoundly mistaken.

As for myself, I submitted my application to the party with complete sincerity. One question remained unresolved for me then: that of the leadership. I understood that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV had gone completely bankrupt; I accepted the party leadership intellectually, but not yet in my soul. This acceptance came gradually, and around the time when the party celebrated Comrade STALIN's fiftieth birthday, I fully understood and felt Comrade STALIN's role; from that period I considered myself a Stalinist.

But I was a poor, worthless Stalinist. I did not sever my connections with the Zinovievists. I met them, although we had fewer and fewer points of contact, especially from the moment when ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV's attempt to meet with the Rightists became evident. I listened to their conversations, sometimes objected and sometimes did not, did not bring these conversations to the party's attention, and treated them in a conciliatory, intellectual fashion; thereby, I acknowledge, I unconditionally covered up their counterrevolutionary essence. From GERTIK I heard gossip about disagreements in the Politburo; I also heard from him that ZINOVIEV was being exiled unjustly, that he was ill and unlikely to return, and other slanderous insinuations directed against the party leadership and Soviet government. And I lacked the Bolshevik qualities to draw Bolshevik conclusions from this, although all of it was already alien to me. I make these qualifications not to diminish my guilt but, on the contrary, to increase it and show how far my ideological position diverged from practice and what a worthless party member I was. I lacked what is fundamental in a Bolshevik: irreconcilability in all fields, not merely theory; I therefore cannot characterize these actions otherwise than as lack of principle and organizational double-dealing. This characterization cannot be nullified by the fact that I thought I was sincere with the party in every question, without even a hint of disagreement or doubt. Nor can it be nullified by the fact that I did not conceal my connections from the party, that during my purge I said I met GERTIK, LEVIN, BASHKIROV and NAUMOV, and that I said the same at my first arrest in January of this year.

When, on 17/XII of this year, the day before my arrest, I read in the newspapers the first report that Comrade KIROV's murder was the work of the dregs of the former Zinovievist antiparty counterrevolutionary group, it came as a bolt from the blue. That very day I wrote a statement to the Central Committee and Moscow Committee saying that, although I did not know the vile murderers, responsibility also fell on me as one of those who had once sown the seed that produced such poisonous shoots. But even on December 17 I did not realize that this was merely a declaratory statement, that I was responsible not only for that past but also for failing, in recent years and right up to these days, to expose the counterrevolutionary essence of the Zinovievist group.

This little group existed as a closed little world, without any political program, ideologically impoverished; it preserved cadres and prepared itself to lead the party. As a closed little world, as a caste, it rotted and inevitably produced the rotten milieu from which the fascist–White Guard terrorists emerged.

And I, who considered myself an ideologically honest party member, who had the political impudence to consider myself a Stalinist, maintained so-called “personal” relations with individual members of this circle. And I, who of my thirty-four years of life had spent almost eighteen in the party, that is, my entire conscious life, had learned nothing and failed to discern behind these “personal” relations their counterrevolutionary essence. Yes, I fully deserved the name of thrice contemptible given by “Pravda”; yes, I proved unworthy to bear the title of party member, for whatever personal, subjective or psychological justifications may be advanced, the fact remains that the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization existed until the last days, and I, who maintained “acquaintances” with it and did not expose it, objectively can be called nothing other than a participant in this counterrevolutionary organization. I cannot escape this verdict of the party-Soviet court and my own conscience.

That is why it was not accidental, that is why I myself am to blame for finding myself in the dock alongside inveterate counterrevolution in its fascist-terrorist form.

It was hard to understand and acknowledge this. Now that I have understood it with my whole being and told my party, I feel relieved. Every individual fact I remember that will help the party see better how counterrevolutionary filth is born, that will help the party prevent such possibilities in the future at their very inception, I will draw from my memory and recount with the utmost conscientiousness, not to gain leniency for myself — I do not deserve it — but for the party, for whose sake alone I lived.

I have no right to ask for anything except the severest revolutionary punishment. And I also ask you to believe that I am now speaking with complete honesty: my dream is, even before the lawful and just punishment is carried out against me, even at the last minute, to have the right to be called an honest Bolshevik, an honest Stalinist.

I ask for the severest revolutionary punishment.

Written in my own hand — A. ZILBERMAN.

INTERROGATED BY — L. KOGAN.

Certified true: Kuznetsova
