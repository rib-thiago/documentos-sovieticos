INTERROGATION RECORD

Of the accused KOZHURO Anna Yevgenyevna,

January 8, 1935.

QUESTION: In your previous testimony, you informed the investigation that you were a member of the underground Zinovievist organization and that, by your position in the organization, you were closely connected with members of the Moscow Zinovievist center. Describe in greater detail the center's personnel and the organization's structure.

ANSWER: The personnel of the Moscow Zinovievist center: ZINOVIEV G. E., KAMENEV L. B., EVDOKIMOV G. E., BAKAEV I. P., SHAROV Ya. V., and KUKLIN A. S.

Two others also played a central role in the organization: GORSHENIN I. S. and GERTIK A. M., but they did not belong to the center and occupied a somewhat special position; they were the center's trusted persons and served as responsible informants and executors of various assignments originating chiefly from the organization's head, ZINOVIEV.

The organizational structure of the Zinovievist underground was built with a view to struggle within the Party and seizing responsible positions in the Party apparatus and in Soviet and economic institutions and enterprises.

In this sense, the organization's task was the gradual, steady restoration of old connections from the past factional struggle, the extension of the organization's influence to other cities of the USSR, and the obligatory preservation of its cadres within the Party.

Organizational connections were strengthened and preserved through clandestine meetings, usually covered, for reasons of secrecy, by family tea gatherings and parties. Former Zinovievists previously marked out for involvement in the organization were often invited to these “parties” (the matter concerns consolidating connections); the same was done with Zinovievists arriving from other cities, who were supplied in Moscow with information and instructions orienting them toward continuing the struggle against the Party.

QUESTION: Describe your organization's political instructions.

ANSWER: Systematic criticism of the policy and decisions of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), taking place at meetings of members of our organization, proceeded chiefly along three lines:

1\) Questions of the Comintern and international relations;

2\) Questions of domestic socialist construction;

3\) Questions of the internal Party order.

Along all these lines, definite views common to the entire organization took shape, amounting to the following orientations:

1\) The present Party leadership, concerning itself chiefly with domestic questions, ignores questions of the international proletarian revolution and relegates them to the background. Hence, as we considered, the inability to make use of the heated revolutionary atmosphere of the capitalist countries, the Comintern's poor work, and partial defeats of the proletariat in various countries.

These propositions were supported by highly distorted information about the situation in the Comintern, with which the Comintern official MAGYAR supplied the organization.

Members of the Moscow center considered that leadership of the Comintern was not assured. This proposition was illustrated at our meetings by numerous tendentiously selected facts of poor Comintern work and incorrect leadership of fraternal parties.

If our criticism concerning the Comintern is deciphered, it amounts to the old, worn-out assertion about the Party's national narrowness, socialism in one uyezd, and the sacrifice of the interests of international revolution to the official policy of the People's Commissariat for Foreign Affairs.

2\) On questions of domestic economic construction: first of all, responsibility for all the difficulties the country had to experience and overcome was placed on the Party leadership. It was considered that the adopted rates of industrialization and collectivization were entirely unbearable, that these rates exhausted the country, had led to famine and a sharp deterioration in the material position of workers and collective farmers.

Living reality was made to fit these instructions given by the Moscow Zinovievist center through its deliberate distortion. Zinovievists arriving from the provinces, maintaining, so to speak, contact with the localities, supplied tendentiously selected material about disintegrating collective farms, senselessly begun and mothballed construction projects, starving workers, and so on and so forth (ELKOVICH, GESSEN, GORDON, and others).

GORSHENIN, returning from an official trip to Siberia where he had gone with a Central Committee inspection brigade, brought figures selected in such a way that, distorting reality, they confirmed the same conclusions. PERIMOV, who worked for several years on the construction of a meat-processing complex, spoke during his visits of the senselessness of this project. On his last visit, KAPITONOV related how they were evicting from their own districts (Novosibirsk) nomads who, seeking to escape death from starvation, tried to move into areas of settled population.

During this same visit, I remember, KAPITONOV cited figures for sums supposedly senselessly expended on constructing the Palace of Soviets, millions of rubles, which had to be mothballed.

We tried to use not only “our” material but material “from outside” to substantiate our counterrevolutionary orientations, not to mention that, on instructions from the organization's center, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, GORSHENIN and KAPITONOV, using their official positions in the Gosplan of the RSFSR and USSR, systematically selected numerical data discrediting capital construction under the first and second five-year plans.

We brushed aside the Party's colossal achievements and all the successes of socialist construction and collected only difficulties, failures, and temporary delays into one whole. And when, however, under the influence of facts, we were compelled to acknowledge this growth and these successes, statements of approximately the following kind were made: there are successes, of course, but at what price? At the price of millions of lives that could have been saved under different conditions. And again we came to the same conclusion: the Party's policy is wrong, the adopted rates of construction are unbearable.

If all these assertions are deciphered, if one examines why these deliberately selected facts distorting reality were needed, the old familiar thesis of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist platform concerning the impossibility of building socialism in our country appears again.

3\) We members of the organization assessed the internal Party regime as a regime of repression and terror. It was considered established that there was no freedom of expression at Party meetings, that standardized resolutions were voted on, that self-criticism was a fiction, and so forth. By virtue of our double-dealing, which had become our second nature, we placed ourselves in a position where at meetings we had to watch carefully that our anti-Party intentions did not slip into our speeches.

Our organization grew ideologically and organizationally on these ideas and sentiments supplied by our “leaders.” Usually everyone arriving from the localities sought to see someone from the center, primarily ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, or BAKAEV, whose instructions were then carried out in the localities.

It seems to me that recently the role of EVDOKIMOV and BAKAEV as authoritative leaders grew particularly among us; their opinions were considered as binding as those of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV.

The political orientations of our organization indicated above possessed a ruinous power of attraction for all of us, its members. Even if healthy Party sentiments and thoughts sometimes appeared in one of us, they were immediately crushed by collective efforts and by the authority of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, or other members of the center.

I remember, for example, that Comrade STALIN's speech to collective farmers made an enormous impression on me. In this speech, the path along which the Party, with a skillful and caring hand, leads toward socialism the peasant not yet freed from proprietary instincts emerges with the utmost simplicity and clarity. I shared my thoughts with GORSHENIN, and he seemed to agree with me. But one only had to speak with EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, GERTIK, and others for different assessments to appear, and Comrade STALIN's speech was assessed extremely negatively. In general, after conversations with ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and EVDOKIMOV, I noticed that anti-Party thoughts and sentiments were enormously strengthened in all of us.

I remember that, having read ZINOVIEV's latest article in “Bolshevik,” I reacted critically to it, though insufficiently and one-sidedly. It seemed politically wrong to me in our time, when the capitalist world was preparing intervention, to emphasize ENGELS's defeatist sentiments so much without somehow connecting the past with the present, without especially strongly emphasizing that we are now all defenders of our socialist homeland. GORSHENIN, too, found no objections to my arguments. But after speaking with EVDOKIMOV, he declared: “Yeremeich considers the article correct,” and with that the question was exhausted.

The authority of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, and other members of our organization's center was undoubtedly placed above the authority of the entire Party.

QUESTION: In which clandestine meetings of the organization did you participate, where and when did they take place, and what was discussed there?

ANSWER: Our organization's clandestine meetings began after the Fifteenth Party Congress. They took place fairly regularly, once or twice a month, at GERTIK's apartment, mine, and those of BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, VUYOVICH, SHAROV, and KUKLIN.

At all our meetings and gatherings, the principal subject of our conversations was discussion of the Central Committee's policy and decisions and the establishment of unity of views among all members of the organization.

I personally participated in the following underground meetings of the organization:

1\) A number of meetings at the apartment of GERTIK Artyom Moiseyevich, considered the organization's headquarters, held fairly regularly until 1933.

2\) To the same period, 1929, belongs an attempt to organize political and economic seminars. I learned, I believe from GERTIK, that I had been assigned to the political seminar (at that time GERTIK was generally my immediate leader). I was given some assignment: either to collect literature or compile lists of literature. On this question I was summoned to ZINOVIEV, who sharply reprimanded me for my slowness in the work.

3\) A large meeting, twelve to fifteen people, in 1932 at my apartment in Plotnikov Lane, 6/8, attended by KAMENEV, his wife, GESSEN, EVDOKIMOV, ZORIN, KAPITONOV, and others. Owing to personal circumstances, I was not present at this meeting.

4\) A meeting at BAKAEV's in late 1932, attended by EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KOSTINA, GORSHENIN, myself, and others, at which the question of how to behave at Party meetings in connection with ZINOVIEV's banishment to Kustanay and KAMENEV's banishment was discussed. It was decided to vote for banishment, but KOSTINA then decided nevertheless to speak in ZINOVIEV's defense, considering that there was a convenient reason for this, since ZINOVIEV had received a harsher punishment than some coauthors of the document.

5\) Before ZINOVIEV's exile to Kustanay, I visited him together with GORSHENIN. ZINOVIEV then asked GORSHENIN to obtain newspapers from MAGYAR for forwarding to Kustanay through his, ZINOVIEV's, son. GORSHENIN carried out this assignment throughout ZINOVIEV's entire time in exile.

6\) Shortly before the murder of Comrade KIROV, GORSHENIN and I were at ZINOVIEV's apartment. GORSHENIN provided information there about planning projections.

7\) A meeting at VUYOVICH's apartment, attended by VUYOVICH, BUDZINSKAYA, GORSHENIN, myself, and others, at which BUDZINSKAYA gave an extremely sharp characterization of the Polish Communist Party's work. I do not remember the details. This was in 1933.

I am unable to recall all the meetings and encounters; as I have already testified above, they were regular and took place until recently, that is, until December 1934.

QUESTION: What do you know about the existence and activity of the underground Zinovievist organization in Leningrad?

ANSWER: From individual statements by members of the center and questions discussed at our meetings, I know that our organization had substantial cadres in Leningrad. I know for certain that LEVIN Vladimir and DMITRIEV came from Leningrad fairly often. In addition, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, SHAROV, and KUKLIN frequently traveled to Leningrad, where they contacted like-minded associates.

QUESTION: What aims did your organization set itself?

ANSWER: The aim of our organization, despite the absence of a definite formulated program, was to make maximum use of particular difficulties experienced by the country and on that basis create a movement within the Party in favor of returning ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV to the Party leadership.

QUESTION: Do you acknowledge your organization's activity to be counterrevolutionary?

ANSWER: Yes, unquestionably.

I sign in confirmation of the correctness of my testimony; the record has been read by me:

A. KOZHURO.

Interrogated by the deputy head of the SPO of the GUGB of the NKVD — STROMIN.
