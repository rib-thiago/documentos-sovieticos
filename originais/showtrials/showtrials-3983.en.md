INTERROGATION RECORD

OF SAFAROV G.I.

January 2, 1935.

In response to the questions put to me, I testify.

After returning from exile in mid-1928, I visited Leningrad, hoping to convince those who refrained from submitting statements acknowledging their guilt of the need to return to the party. For this purpose, a meeting was convened, attended by YEMELYANOV Kondraty, GORODSKOY Pyotr, MAKSIMOV Nikolai Aleksandrovich, KASHCHEYEV, I think SAMARETS, and others. A considerable portion of the assembled members of the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc were in an anti-party mood. I believe SAMARETS spoke particularly resolutely against returning to the party, depicting the offensive against the kulaks as a passing maneuver that would quickly give way to a turn to the Right. SAMARETS later went over entirely to the Trotskyists, among whom he worked actively. In my report, I posed the question as follows: the offensive against the kulaks and capitalist elements had unfolded. To remain outside the party meant to evade this struggle and become an ally of the kulak. Inner-party democracy, I said—meaning by this what the Trotskyist-Zinovievists called “inner-party democracy,” that is, freedom to struggle against the party line—would come in the wake of the unfolding socialist offensive. At present, refusing support for the CC meant Trotskyism, a struggle for a second party. I condemned ZINOVIEV for faintheartedness and said it would have been better to remain in exile for six months so as not to be forced to “betray like-minded comrades,” as the Zinovievists did. In favor of returning to the party, I advanced anti-party arguments, which could be nothing other than counterrevolutionary double-dealing. Most of those assembled held an even more anti-party and anti-Soviet viewpoint, and, while doing vile work, I thought I was “helping the party” by securing the return of its enemies to its ranks.

Mikhail KOVALENKO, formerly employed by “Krasnaya Gazeta,” was an active Trotskyist who worked in close collaboration with KARPOV and used his extensive newspaper and literary connections for this purpose. In 1928–1929, he adapted skillfully like a chameleon while continuing his counterrevolutionary work. According to Vladimir MATVEYEV, KOVALENKO had recently taken up literature, but had retained his hostility to the party and socialist construction as before.

His close friend—Nikolai Pavlovich BASKAKOV—was also an active Trotskyist. To deceive the party, in 1926 and partly in 1927 he even pretended to be fighting the Zinovievists; a spreader of counterrevolutionary gossip and slander, a publishing specialist who surely used his “knowledge and experience” to help the Trotskyist organization with printing and so forth. MATVEYEV said that he was now in Saratov, of course “with his old sentiments.”

ABIK—a Trotskyist reinstated in the party in 1930 and subsequently expelled during the purge. Previously worked at Sots. EKGIZ \[1\]; now works at the “Stary Bolshevik” publishing house under Ilya IONOV's patronage. At meetings I had with him on SOTSEKGIZ business in 1931–1933, he usually shared counterrevolutionary gossip and rumors and indicated in every way that he did not support the party leadership and “joyfully remembered the days of the old struggle.” At the same \<time\>, officially he tried to express his solidarity with the party line “more loudly than anyone.” In his whole disposition, he is a petty chameleon clinging to a comfortable position but ready to do mischief on the sly. A great friend of VARDIN's \[2\] in recent years.

LENKOV—a very active Trotskyist who worked at “Treugolnik.” I think he was arrested and exiled in 1928. In 1927, TROTSKY flaunted him as a “representative of the Leningrad workers” wherever he could. In Leningrad in 1928, I was told that he was a man not only of active anti-Soviet orientations, defending “work stoppages” at enterprises, but also generally kept close to the “Decists.”

TUZHIKOV—a worker at “Putilovets,” an active participant in the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc. In 1928, he was sharply indignant over the submission of statements seeking return to the party, demanding continuation of the struggle and citing TROTSKY as an “example.” In 1929, he remained in the same counterrevolutionary mood and undoubtedly influenced a number of other workers of the former Narva district connected with the Zinovievists and Trotskyists.

LEOSHKO—a Zinovievist from the former Narva district; in 1928 and 1929, he moved in Zinovievist circles and maintained old connections and acquaintances. I do not know what became of him afterward.

Written in my own hand—G. SAFAROV.
