CENTRAL COMMITTEE OF THE AUCP(b) —

to Comrade STALIN.

Great and wise leader!

I kneel before the party and you, its great leader, and ask for mercy and help. You were infinitely right in foreseeing the opposition's degeneration into the dregs of counterrevolution. You were exceptionally patient in explaining the opposition's counterrevolutionary essence and repeatedly attempting to return to the party path us people who had broken away from the party and working class, become the vanguard of counterrevolution, and turned into an amalgam of betrayal, double-dealing, lies, slander, cowardice, and sordid political intrigue. You were extraordinarily lenient toward former oppositionists who declared their withdrawal from the opposition, hoping they could reform and carry out the party line, while they, as it turned out, entered the party under a banner inscribed: “a lie may bring salvation.”

In blind malice and with bestial fury, the Zinovievist opposition advanced the slogan of the degeneration of the party leadership, maintaining it constantly in its ranks; yet on examination it emerged that complete degeneration had overtaken the opposition itself and that precisely it, and it alone, had been cast to the very bottom of White émigré excrement, choked in that excrement, spread its aromas, and served as the mouthpiece of all White Guard, bourgeois-restorationist sentiments! Stewing in its own vomit, the Zinovievist opposition concocted counterrevolutionary slander against you and the party, harping on all sorts of rumors and gossip about supposed disagreements in the Central Committee, searching for a crack into which it could thrust its swinish snout.

Since the Fourteenth Party Congress I have traveled the entire counterrevolutionary path behind ZINOVIEV and EVDOKIMOV, subordinating everything else to its tasks, and bear full responsibility for it. But even I was horrified when I heard everything that was said at the trial. It emerged that the organization leaders' double-dealing and lies were twofold: they lied vilely not only to the party but also to their closest associates. Although I was closely connected with EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and others, only at the trial did I first hear about the negotiations with TOMSKY, MRACHKOVSKY, I. N. SMIRNOV, SHLYAPNIKOV, and SMILGA, about SAFAROV's procuring, and so on. This was done to avoid losing the last remnants of their cadres: they feared not only the Central Committee, not only the party, but also their own associates.

And that is why I stated at the trial that, while considering EVDOKIMOV's assessment of the counterrevolutionary path of the Zinovievist opposition, with its exposition of our counterrevolutionary views, the most complete and correct, I did not join him, as a whole series of defendants said they did. It seems to me that agitation prevented me from developing my thought.

I no longer want to join anyone except the party; I no longer want to follow anyone except STALIN. In equal measure, in the sharpest and most resolute manner, I condemn, reject, and curse both my anti-party, counterrevolutionary past and my former leaders and chiefs whom I followed: ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and others. In court, ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and others dragged ever more facts of their counterrevolutionary work out of the hiding places in their dirty underwear, facts they concealed even from their associates, each dirtier and more vile than the last, all taken together literally forming a cesspool of political unprincipledness and filth.

I say this not to lessen my guilt: my guilt is as great as everyone else's, and since I was an active member of the organization I bear full political responsibility for everything that was done, and the sentence passed by the court is unconditionally correct. I was closely and systematically connected with the leading members of the center. I conducted counterrevolutionary work; I informed members of the center of everything I knew about the condition of the country from the materials I had at the Procuracy. The party entrusted me with responsible political work; I betrayed the party's trust in the interests of the counterrevolutionary organization, and there can be no mitigation of my role. I cite this only to characterize the entire abyss into which we had fallen.

I disagree with BAKAEV's assertion that “the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground was growing stronger.” It was not growing stronger but disintegrating, rotting alive, and dying politically. There were and are no causes in the Soviet Union by virtue of which the counterrevolutionary underground could grow stronger. Precisely because it was disintegrating and rotting alive, the dregs of the organization took the path resorted to by the frenzied and dying class enemy, a path undoubtedly applauded madly by all the fascist scum, all the inveterate enemies of the working class.

The speed of this disintegration and final decay was inversely proportional to the growth of the party's strength and the Soviet Union's might. And now, when the party's strength and unity and the Soviet Union's might have risen to unprecedented heights, the dregs of the Zinovievist organization have emitted the poison of their last gasp, and the entire Zinovievist organization has collapsed finally and forever, choking on the poison of its own excrement. Nor is it accidental that this occurred immediately after the historic decisions of the November Central Committee Plenum, which clearly demonstrated the results achieved by the party under your leadership in building socialism.

My personal guilt is aggravated by the fact that in recent years, seeing and feeling the party's victories, understanding that the Stalin path had proved the only true and correct one, I did not find the strength to break with the organization, continuing to belong to it and conduct counterrevolutionary work. The logic of the struggle and personal ties compelled me with irresistible force to continue rolling down the path of counterrevolution and double-dealing.

The counterrevolutionary work of the Zinovievist organization has cost the party and country far too dearly.

It is clear to me that the entire party experienced these two months heavily and painfully, as did you, its great leader. This follows directly from your entire struggle against the opposition, in which you tried in every way first to persuade, explain, and prove, rather than cut off and destroy, since you wanted to retain in the party everyone capable of reforming, capable of fighting for socialism.

The news of who had killed reached me in the Kharovsk district, where I was conducting the district congress of soviets. And however hard it was for me, two days before my arrest, in my concluding speech closing the congress, I described the counterrevolutionary path of the Zinovievist organization and stated that those directly responsible for the most vile crime were ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and others. At my proposal [1], the congress sent telegrams about this to you and to the territorial committee secretary, V. IVANOV. But I lacked the strength then, as at my arrest, to tell Comrades AKULOV, YEZHOV, and VYSHINSKY immediately that, since I had been an active member of the organization, I too must answer in equal measure.

What frightens me most now is that I have been cast overboard from life, that I am deprived of the opportunity to prove in practice, through work, that I can atone for my crimes against the party even to a millionth part, and that only association with sworn enemies of the working class lies before me.

At the age of nineteen I began working in the Leningrad organization, was brought up by it, and followed its leaders first along the revolutionary and then along the counterrevolutionary path. Until 1935 I had never appealed to you, never told the Central Committee about my path.

I want to live and work; I still have sufficient strength for work. I implore you to help me, once finally torn from the captivity of the counterrevolutionary swamp, to atone in practice, through work, at least a little for the harm I have caused. I implore you to give me the opportunity to live and die not among the enemies of the working class, but in struggle for the party, for the working class, for the great and wise leader, Comrade STALIN.

B. SAKHOV.

January 18, 1935.
