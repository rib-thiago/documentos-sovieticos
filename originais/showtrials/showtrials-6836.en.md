TO THE PEOPLE'S COMMISSAR FOR INTERNAL AFFAIRS OF THE UNION OF SOVIET SOCIALIST REPUBLICS —

Comrade YAGODA

Before the proletarian court pronounces its revolutionary word on my counterrevolutionary actions against the Party and Soviet power, I consider it necessary to address the following statement to you.

Returning to the Party after statements of full solidarity with its policy at the end of 1929, considerably later than Zinoviev and his counterrevolutionary group, with the pitiful remnants of the former “leaderless” group, for a time I thought that I had acted more honestly toward the Party by returning after genuinely acknowledging my political mistakes and counterrevolutionary actions. In reality this was not so at all. I imagined that on a number of questions the Party, in its struggle against the Rightists, had adopted the views of the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist opposition, and that this enabled me and those signing with me to write a statement of solidarity with Party policy. The results of this counterrevolutionary double-dealing were not long in coming: some of the people in Leningrad, headed by EZHOV, soon resumed counterrevolutionary work and received their deserved punishment; others, together with me, renewed connections with ZINOVIEV and his group and once again sank into that group's criminal anti-Party, anti-Soviet, counterrevolutionary work.

Throughout 1930–1932 I maintained contact and took an active part in the work of the Moscow Zinovievist group. Throughout this time the supporters of the former Zinovievist opposition who remained in Moscow tried in every way to preserve personal connections with one another, giving their counterrevolutionary work the most varied forms.

The principal task consisted, on the one hand, of boundlessly exaggerating the difficulties encountered on the path of the Party's general line and attributing them to its allegedly incorrect line, and, on the other, of discrediting the Party leadership and Comrade STALIN in particular by every possible means.

Any entirely correct maneuver by the Party, arising from the balance of class forces at a given stage, was presented as a departure from Leninist policy, and on this basis distrust and hostility toward the Party leadership were sown.

The active members of the former Trotskyist-Zinovievist counterrevolutionary opposition grouped around Zinoviev, as well as those gathered around SMIRNOV I. N. and other persons close to the counterrevolutionary TROTSKY, did not believe in the correctness of the Party's general line.

On the country's industrialization, we exaggerated the difficulties of mastering technology and new construction, denied the facts of the Bolshevik pace of industrialization, and expected the collapse of this grandiose construction, unprecedented in world history, initiated at Comrade STALIN's instigation and carried out under his iron Bolshevik leadership. Exaggerating and distorting isolated negative facts, we presented them in a spirit that differed in no way from their presentation in the White Guard and Menshevik press.

The group also disbelieved in the victory of collectivization in the countryside, taking a counterrevolutionary position on this question too toward particular mistakes and difficulties. The group, inspired by the counterrevolutionary ZINOVIEV, looked upon him as a springboard that could help it return to the leadership. Therefore, in 1932 ZINOVIEV sought allies both in the former group of Right counterrevolutionaries around RYUTIN and in the group of former “Left” counterrevolutionaries around SHLYAPNIKOV, cherishing the hope that temporary difficulties in the countryside would lead to the collapse of collectivization and that his bloc with SHLYAPNIKOV and RYUTIN would strengthen his position in the Party. The vileness and counterrevolutionary nature of such an attitude toward this great undertaking of the Party of Lenin–Stalin, on the path toward overcoming the contradictions between town and countryside and eliminating classes, differ in no way from the assessment of collectivization by the worst class enemies of the land of the Soviets.

On questions of internal Party democracy, the group continued to look at matters in the old way, spreading the vilest slander about the regime within the Party. The Party's enormous political growth, the unprecedented cohesion of its ranks around the Central Committee and Comrade STALIN, its unshakable political unity, and the incomparable growth of its influence throughout the country — all this remained a book sealed with seven seals. The group continued to speak of the absence of the Party and Central Committee and depicted Comrade STALIN's role in the Party in such a way as could arouse only extreme bitterness and hatred toward the man under whose leadership the Party and Comintern had ensured the unceasing victorious advance toward socialism of the proletariat and collective farmers in the USSR and laid the foundations for the victory of the proletarian dictatorship throughout the world.

In our downfall we went so far that ZINOVIEV, in a conversation with me in the autumn of 1932, quite openly expressed his wish that Comrade STALIN should no longer be in the Party leadership, whatever means might bring about his removal.

The group underestimated the enormous growth of the Comintern section in the capitalist countries, their Bolshevization, and the consolidation of a strong Communist Party leadership in a whole series of countries; it regarded the resulting greater independence of the fraternal Communist parties, foreseen in their time by LENIN and STALIN, as a manifestation of national narrowness on the part of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and its withdrawal from the struggle for world revolution.

All this shows that the group remained on the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist platform, systematically deceiving the Party, making the method of counterrevolutionary double-dealing the basis of its attitude toward the Party and creating an atmosphere in which criminals who raised their hand against Comrade Kirov were inevitably born. At different stages the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist opposition fought the Party and Soviet power in different ways, always fulfilling the class enemy's social commission.

At the moment of extreme bitterness among the remnants of the kulaks and the international bourgeoisie in the face of the grandiose construction of socialism and the victorious advance of the land of the Soviets toward a classless society, the dregs of the former Zinovievist opposition did not shrink from the vilest murder of Comrade Kirov, continuing to fulfill the class enemy's commission.

Responsibility for this murder, however, falls not only on the immediate perpetrators and not only on the leading core of the former Zinovievist group, but also on every member of the group, active or inactive, who participated in its counterrevolutionary activity or, knowing about it, concealed it from the Party.

Such an atmosphere created a complete blunting of Party spirit and responsibility toward the revolution. The best proof of this is my conversation with ZINOVIEV in SHLYAPNIKOV's presence in the autumn of 1932: when I reported counterrevolutionary rumors about an alleged attempt on Comrade STALIN at a Party meeting, ZINOVIEV replied, “People talk about what they wish for”; neither SHLYAPNIKOV nor I responded in a Party-minded way, either during the conversation or afterward.

I consider it my greatest crime against the Party that I did not immediately report this conversation to the TsKK, since it is quite evident that similar conversations by ZINOVIEV with other members of the group, if they took place, could have played a definite role in creating or sustaining terrorist sentiments and plans in the circle around the group and contributed to the commission of the crime — Comrade Kirov's murder.

At the same time I concealed from the Party that the meeting between SHLYAPNIKOV and ZINOVIEV, of which I happened to be an accidental witness for a short time, was in the nature of restoring a political bloc to struggle against the Party by exploiting temporary difficulties in the countryside, although this was quite clear to me.

Instead of exposing this counterrevolutionary activity and openly fighting it, I confined myself to breaking personal connections with the leading core of the Zinovievist group, while retaining personal relations with a number of members of the former Zinovievist opposition (ZORIN, LELEVICH, OSINSKAYA, GERTSBERG, POZDEEVA, NAUMOV, and MAGYAR). After the Ryutin affair I intended to deepen this break, but did not do so, and, as subsequent events showed, this was no accident.

In the spring of 1933 the Party gave me a warning by removing me from Comintern work and sending me to work in Tashkent. I did not understand this warning and failed to use it for a fundamental reassessment of my values and correction of my oppositionist, counterrevolutionary past. Having failed to do this, I quite inevitably failed to carry out my intention of breaking all personal connections with former oppositionists and setting about the work to which the Party had assigned me in Tashkent in a Bolshevik manner. Thus in Tashkent I associated chiefly with SMILGA I. T. and SHATSKIN L. A., and on my visits to Moscow continued to meet the persons mentioned above. I took the help the Party had given me as an allegedly unjust and unfounded removal from political work, and this in turn contributed to my failure, even in the new circumstances, to behave as befitted a Party member.

It is true that during the past two years, far from the Moscow Zinovievist group, amid the enormous Bolshevik work carried out by the Party in Central Asia, I managed to review my views on all the fundamental questions of Party and Comintern policy. For a time it seemed to me that I had no disagreements with the Party.

But the first new trial showed that I was profoundly mistaken and that the poison of the counterrevolutionary activity of the former Trotskyist-Zinovievist opposition was deeply lodged within me.

It is true that during my few meetings with the former Zinovievist oppositionists listed above over the past two years, I openly said that the Party had been right on every question and that I fully shared its line. This elicited corresponding behavior from those people, who did not wish to lose me from their circle and understood that, until I drew all the Party-minded, Bolshevik conclusions from my political position, they could with full right consider me one of their own.

I failed to draw these conclusions either at the beginning of 1933 or after Comrade Kirov's murder.

I remained at liberty for about a month after the vile role of the dregs of the former Zinovievist opposition, the organizers and perpetrators of Comrade Kirov's murder, became known, and when the counterrevolutionary, treacherous role of the entire Zinovievist-Trotskyist group in this affair was clear to everyone. I had every opportunity to consider the conduct of the group as a whole and my own personal conduct after returning to the Party. Yet I failed to draw the only correct conclusion: immediately to tell the Party everything I knew and give a political assessment of the counterrevolutionary actions of the entire group and of my own. Thus I myself placed an equals sign between my counterrevolutionary, double-dealing conduct in 1930–1932 and my profoundly anti-Party and anti-Soviet conduct in 1933–1934.

One can and must break with one's counterrevolutionary past, but only completely and irrevocably; otherwise, as my example proves, sliding back into the counterrevolutionary swamp is inevitable.

It is now completely clear to me how enormously we harmed the cause of revolution, and I will accept any punishment from the punitive organs of the proletarian dictatorship as fully deserved.

I know how the Party regards statements by people who have deceived it so many times, and how right it is in this. Nevertheless, I cannot end this statement, which brings the vile counterrevolutionary period of my life to a close, without telling you, the Party Central Committee, and the Party Control Commission under the Central Committee that I have understood fully and broken completely with my counterrevolutionary past, and that the harsh lesson I have received will serve as the starting point for a new revolutionary life.

My crimes against the Party are very grave. They can neither be forgotten nor forgiven. I will undergo my deserved punishment, but the heaviest of all punishments is expulsion from the Party, being outside the ranks of the enormous army of builders of socialism who, under the leadership of the Party and the great STALIN, are victoriously building a classless society. My past gives me no right to speak of trust. But I ask to be given an opportunity, in the nearer or more distant future, through practical work as an ordinary worker in the great construction, to earn and win anew the right and high honor of becoming an active fighter in the ranks of the foremost vanguard of the world proletariat, in the ranks of the Bolshevik Party of LENIN–STALIN.

V. VUYOVICH.

February 16, 1935.

I earnestly ask you to bring this statement to the attention of the Central Committee and the Party Control Commission under the Party Central Committee.

Certified true: M. Belkin
