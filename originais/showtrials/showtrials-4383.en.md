TESTIMONY

OF ALEXANDROV, Alexander Ivanovich —

10 January 1935.

In response to the questions put to me by the investigation, I testify:

How did it happen that, on the one hand, after my reinstatement in the party, sincerely convinced already by late 1929 that I had no disagreements with the party, sincerely rejoicing with everyone and admiring those truly grandiose successes and victories which the Bolshevik party achieved and achieves in the shortest time under the leadership of its greatest genius and leader of the world proletariat, Comrade STALIN, and, on the other hand, despite all this, I nevertheless became a participant in the counterrevolutionary organization of former adherents of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc, whose vile member on 1/XII-34 fired the treacherous shot into the party's heart, ending the life of the greatest revolutionary and Bolshevik S. M. KIROV, Comrade STALIN's closest friend and comrade-in-arms.

Having thought everything through seriously, attentively, and honestly to the end, I declare that the answer to this question can and must be direct and definite.

The reason for this transformation, the transition from party-mindedness to counterrevolution, lies in inner division, duality, and double-dealing.

There were two ALEXANDROVs. One a party member, proletarian, Bolshevik, seemingly sincerely making every effort to dissolve into the general party membership, to return organically to the party with his whole being, seemingly taking energetic measures to defend his Bolshevik party-mindedness zealously and steadfastly against anti-party elements and encroachments.

The other ALEXANDROV — rather, the same one, but from the reverse side — is ALEXANDROV, a former active oppositionist who for over two years actively fought against the party and its leadership. ALEXANDROV, in whom substantial traces and imprints remained of schooling and education under the leadership of the former “chiefs” and “leaders” of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc. ALEXANDROV, a product of that bloc's milieu.

In 1928, in Kaluga, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV sit it out awaiting the end of the period for receiving party cards. In Kaluga, ALEXANDROV, ALEXANDROV of the “leaderless ones,” visits the “leaders” about three times. The leaders strongly recommend returning to the party quickly in order to preserve the opposition cadres. I demanded of them, admittedly very timidly, at least “some” criticism of their mistakes. Unfortunately I received no articulate sounds from them on that score.

After submitting an application in July 1928, I left for Leningrad. The mood there was very bad. Confusion in people's minds was desperate. Conversations and “persuading” began. The workers' group from factories and mills went back into the party relatively easily. I remember that SUROV, TSARKOV, LUKIN, SOBOL, and others maintained their view (not to return). All the technical arrangements for submitting applications, their form and contents, were handled by RUMYANTSEV. I went back to Moscow.

1929. In January I received my party card. I work at Rezinotrest. I am connected with ZILBERMAN, BLINKOV, and BARANOV. The first somehow became the center for the group of “leaderless ones.” The latter two looked toward Comrade SARKIS, who then rejected any groups and “leaders.” He emphasized that the crux of the question was leadership: one must go precisely into the party and support the party leadership by every means. Among ourselves we discussed the party's general line, individual directives, and press articles. The question of the attitude toward the “leaders” and of their attitude toward the party's general line was not removed but remained on the agenda in one form or another.

In spring 1929 (talk had already begun about the “Rightists'” disagreements with the party), I called on ZINOVIEV in Kaloshin Lane; several people were there (I do not remember exactly who). I asked how the “leaders” would regard the party's struggle against the Rightists; again I received no articulate answers. Moreover, some time afterward I was told, by ZILBERMAN I think, that ZINOVIEV had declared: “I do not want to see ALEXANDROV. Get him away from me. The farther from trouble, the better.” I never saw him again.

1930. I work at the “Kauchuk” factory as its director. Director of the largest enterprise in the district, I am removed from the lists of District Committee members at the party conference. I swallow this bitter pill. I understand the reason. I continue working persistently. I receive a good assessment from the local party organization: “fought firmly for the party's general line,” and so forth.

Everything seems to be in order. It seemed to me that I had already grown organically into the party, dissolved into it, overcome my mistakes. But this was only “seemingly.” It only seemed so. In reality this was not the case; how, I shall indicate below. For even at that time, now and then something would slip in: so-and-so was transferred, this one was subjected to criticism, that one was pushed aside, and so forth.

1931–1934. In March–May, the Rubber Association, by agreement with the Leningrad Regional Committee, transfers me to Leningrad, to the “Red Triangle” factory, as deputy director of the whole combine. This alone shows that the Leningrad party organization received me very well, investing me with the most colossal confidence.

I work for over a year. Then I am appointed successively deputy manager of Regional Municipal Services, manager of a trust, head of the Leningrad food industry's administration, and finally deputy manager of Lenkozhtrest. In all these jobs, according to the organizations' assessments, I perform fairly well. Again everything seems good. A one-hundred-percent Bolshevik, a party man. But in practice? Far from it. Not at all.

For all the party's immense confidence in me I “repaid” it with “counterfeit opposition coin.”

Onto the scene comes ALEXANDROV, the active oppositionist, a product of the opposition milieu. This began with ordinary meetings with former participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc after my arrival in Leningrad in 1931. Frankly speaking, I did not seek these meetings, but they happened of themselves. “A fisherman recognizes another fisherman from afar.” I visited almost none of the oppositionists' apartments, except on one occasion of which I shall speak below, nor did I visit them at work. Nevertheless these spontaneous meetings connected me with the entire milieu of former active participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc.

Whom did I see in Leningrad and Moscow during this period? REM, RUMYANTSEV, KHANIK, TSEITLIN, SEREDOKHIN, TOLMAZOV, LUKIN, SOBOL, SUROV, VASILIEVA, POPOV, NOVIKOV, DEMIDOVA, MINAEV, LEVIN, SOSITSKY, NALIVAIKO, LEOSHKO, KOSTRITSKY, ANDREEV, REPNIKOV, BLINKOV, BARANOV, ZILBERMAN, MANDELSHTAM, DMITRIEV, BRODSKY, SOKOLOV, ABRAMSON, BOGRACHEV, GRIGORIEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KOSTINA; I probably met others, it is difficult to remember now.

Were they glad of these meetings? Yes. They welcomed me as “one of ours,” “family.” What questions did they raise, what interested them? Of course, by 1931–1934 questions such as “is the party's general line correct or incorrect?” or “who came over to whom?” were no longer raised, but these questions were: 1) Where are ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and others, what are they doing, and how are they? 2) Why are they not brought into the leadership? 3) How are your visits? 4) Whom do you see among the “old ones”? 5) Drop in “for a chat.” 6) We ought to get together sometime. 7) Sometimes they complained that “we are pushed aside, not allowed to advance,” and so forth.

What are these, chance innocent questions? No. One does not put such questions to just any Bolshevik party member. They can be put only to a person of one's own milieu.

From KHANIK, TSEITLIN, RUMYANTSEV, and others I often heard complaints of being “pushed aside” and “not admitted” to the Komsomol organization, sometimes adding a “piece” of discrediting the Komsomol leadership: “who, they say, is writing Komsomol history?” “so-and-so has become a Komsomol leader too,” and so forth.

Once, in the Regional Committee's administrative section (with Baltramovich and Orlov), in the presence of someone else, KHANIK related somewhat ingratiatingly that at a meeting, admittedly with official Provincial Committee representatives participating, the question of candidates for awards in connection with the Red Army anniversary had been discussed; among other candidates mine, ALEXANDROV's, had been proposed as one of the first organizers and military commissars of the Komsomol combat detachments of 1917–1919, and despite KHANIK's defense of my candidacy it had been withdrawn because of “the odiousness of the figure.” Naturally, all this was said in a spirit of discontent.

At the same place, but at another meeting in 1933, in the presence of PAVLOV, ZHESTYANNIKOV, and someone else, that same KHANIK related that at a Komsomol anniversary meeting in Moscow, when orders were being awarded to individual Komsomol leaders, almost in reply to the speech by the party CC representative, SMORODIN P. declared: “Well, we did not receive our orders here; we won them in the battles at Kronstadt,” and immediately came the addition: “But of course, they did not even remember us.” The spirit of the information is, of course, clear.

An incidental remark: the Party Regional Committee's Administrative Department, in BALTRAMOVICH's time there and then ORLOV's (who, if I am not mistaken, I do not know for certain, was secretary of the bureau of Komsomol combat veterans' fellowships), became a peculiar kind of clandestine meeting place; at any rate it was rare for me to call there without finding one or another oppositionist Komsomol member.

One more feature of “kinship” and “cliquishness.” True, I never attended any meeting of the Combat Veterans' Fellowship despite repeated invitations. I do not know when or how it was organized. I obtained my partisan card through the general procedure in Narva District, having certificates from P. SMORODIN, with whom I had been in a detachment in 1917–1918, from Istomol, and, I think, from the late Comrade GAZA.

Nevertheless, once PANOV dragged me there, persuading me that I absolutely had to complete a questionnaire for the Central Commission and also help them establish whether there were any hangers-on. Naturally I was passed through without waiting in any queue: “Why ask? He is ‘ours,’ ‘one of us,’ ‘we know him’”; and some time later, I think, KHANIK obligingly handed me an extract from the CC resolution restoring the rights of former Red partisans and Red Guards.

Sometimes I expressed sharply negative views of individual oppositionists' “little conversations” and “whispers.” Perhaps this serves to excuse me in some measure? No, it cannot. On the contrary, it increases the guilt. It is easier for the party and working class to identify a hostile element when he is always clearly for or against, and worse when this element is ambiguous; that is one side. On the other side, what does “sometimes” mean? Is this how a genuinely authentic Bolshevik party member should approach resolving these questions arising in a definite opposition milieu? Of course not. He would always have a sharply negative attitude toward these anti-party, hostile conversations. He would castigate and expose that milieu. He would bring the specific bearers of evil into the open, drag them to the appropriate authorities, and so forth.

And now, if one compares the first ALEXANDROV with the second. What did he do with the party confidence shown him? He squandered it. Instead of strengthening revolutionary class vigilance, he lost it.

I did not castigate all these little conversations, did not say: “You, like me, committed the gravest crime against the party; it forgave you, trusted you, and you occupy yourselves harming it and its leadership.” Instead I treated this conciliatorily.

I informed the party organizations about the meeting at SUROV's apartment in 1932 (about which I have already testified), which does not diminish my guilt in the slightest.

What is the outcome, the conclusion? It seems to me that from all the above a conclusion clearly suggests itself. I did not break with this harmful milieu. Even if I did not seek it, it found me. But it did find me. I came under its power, became its captive. I not only failed to expose it but clearly contributed greatly to consolidating these forces. I helped cultivate the sentiments described above, and thereby I turned out to be, or became, a collaborator of the counterrevolutionary organization of former participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc, a member of which committed an unheard-of crime, tearing from the party and working class their beloved leader, an unbending, firm Bolshevik, Comrade STALIN's closest friend and comrade-in-arms.

What can I ask of the party and its leadership? Only that I be held to the strictest account. After all, damn it, I am still flesh of the flesh and blood of the blood of the working class and its party. What else can I ask? I am only horrified at how I could have sunk to “a life like this,” to such a low point. But one cannot escape the facts. Any punishment, however severe, I must accept and will accept without the slightest murmur.

I cannot, dare not ask for anything more; but if the party finds it possible or necessary someday to place me again in its fighters' ranks, then, tearing the serpentine counterrevolutionary sting out of my being by the roots, I am confident I will prove in practice that I can be a worthy and faithful soldier of it.

Written in my own hand:

ALEXANDROV.
