STENOGRAPHIC TRANSCRIPT.

CONFRONTATION RECORD

Between ZINOVIEV, Grigory Evseevich, and

SAFAROV, Georgy Ivanovich,

January 11, 1935.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Tell us about your negotiations with ZINOVIEV in 1932.

ANSWER: I must begin with the fact that before meeting ZINOVIEV I met a number of representatives of anti-party groupings, and these prior meetings prepared the way for the meeting with ZINOVIEV. Starting approximately in the spring of 1932, there was a great revival among all anti-party and anti-Soviet groupings, which were trying to exploit the temporary difficulties to struggle against the party and against the party leadership. I had not met ZINOVIEV since the Fifteenth Party Congress, when I withdrew from the Zinovievists with the anti-party group of the “leaderless.”

Approximately in the spring or early summer of 1932 MRACHKOVSKY, Sergei Vitalyevich, came to Moscow; I had personally been very close to him. My relations with him had broken off after my return to the VKP(b). Now we met at his apartment on Tverskoi Boulevard and began a conversation on anti-party subjects. I expressed the following views to him: that the party leadership had no line; that we faced the threat of a rupture of the alliance between the working class and the peasantry.

At the same time I spoke with MRACHKOVSKY about the errors of the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc on the question of building socialism in one country and on Thermidor; MRACHKOVSKY received my references to the errors sourly, assenting vaguely. But he supported wholeheartedly my practical conclusion that a struggle against the leadership had to be waged, and I then advanced the idea that a platform document should be drawn up setting out our views, and that this document should be submitted to the CC with the signatures of some twenty old party members.

This proposal aroused no sympathy in MRACHKOVSKY, but he immediately raised the question: was I seeing ZINOVIEV? I said: “You know yourself that our relations broke off.” He replied: “But you must see ZINOVIEV and EVDOKIMOV, because such matters must be settled with them.” After this MRACHKOVSKY brought me together with Ivan Nikitich SMIRNOV, a Trotskyist, and the conversation with SMIRNOV proceeded along the same lines.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Where did MRACHKOVSKY bring you together with SMIRNOV?

ANSWER: We met at SMIRNOV's apartment on Krymskaya Square. The conversation concerned the same thing—the practical form of action against the leadership. MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV regarded the form I proposed skeptically, but both at the same time insisted that I must see ZINOVIEV and EVDOKIMOV.

This insistence became more understandable to me after I went to see MRACHKOVSKY off in the autumn of 1932. I went to his railway carriage and unexpectedly saw EVDOKIMOV there. There was a small company of Trotskyists: MRACHKOVSKY, SMIRNOV, Shura SAFONOVA, VAGANYAN \[1\], Pyotr PEREVERZEV and EVDOKIMOV. MRACHKOVSKY had concealed this connection with EVDOKIMOV. He himself felt awkward and said: “Do not be embarrassed, Georgy; everyone here is one of us.” At our first meetings he had not mentioned his closeness to EVDOKIMOV. Before meeting ZINOVIEV I also met SMILGA.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Where was the meeting?

ANSWER: At SMILGA's apartment; this was before the meeting with ZINOVIEV. On June 15, 1932, I was called up into the army, and this occurred before that moment. SMILGA stated that there was a threat of a rupture of the alliance between the working class and the peasantry; everything in the economy was upside down; construction figures were inflated and, as he said, inflation was actually taking place, which would wreck all state attempts to regulate the national economy: a very acute moment was approaching. He added: “I know your temperament; please do not hurry\! Again he said: “Why do you not see ZINOVIEV?”

Ivan Nikitich SMIRNOV and MRACHKOVSKY also gave me these same arguments about the gravity of the situation to justify my meeting with ZINOVIEV.

In his conversation with me, SMILGA developed his view of the general situation in connection with my proposal for an appeal to the CC signed by twenty old party members. In this connection he said: “Well, here we must wait until they fall out. There are two groups in the CC fighting each other; let them fall out, and then we shall act.” He tried to give his assessment of the state of affairs in the country, the working class and the party. On the economic side I have already described how matters stood. Explaining why the views of counterrevolutionary, anti-party groupings found no support in the party or the working class, he developed a theory of the “peasantization” of the working class. He said that as a result of enormous construction a huge mass of new people—“muzhiks,” “country bumpkins”—had poured into construction work and factories; they had “flooded” the core cadres of the working class, and this affected the situation in the party. “The muzhik is overpowering the worker.”

On parting from me, SMILGA remarked: “You see, I meet Lev Borisovich—a most intelligent economist—and with the country in such a difficult situation he remains outside the leadership\!” SMILGA reminded me that I must see ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV.

Before meeting ZINOVIEV I also met VARDIN, who often visited me. He had previously belonged to the same anti-party “leaderless” grouping as I had. VARDIN at this time was also casting about in search of a platform and was striving to cobble together a counterrevolutionary bloc of all anti-party groupings.

In particular, in my testimony I reported an incident that occurred with LOMINADZE. MAGYAR told me that LOMINADZE and STEN were writing some platform. I passed this conversation on to VARDIN, who is known for his talkativeness. He immediately went to LOMINADZE and told him about it. LOMINADZE rushed to my home and began asking where I had obtained such information, trying to assure me that there was no platform yet. When I began setting out my anti-party assessment of the situation, he received it with full approval and said: “Let us keep in contact.”

VARDIN met me after this conversation. I scolded him for his talkativeness, since MAGYAR had asked that LOMINADZE not be told about his reports, and this had caused a misunderstanding. VARDIN also asked why I was “not seeing Grigory.”

MAGYAR played the role of an agent of the Zinovievist organization in the Comintern, collecting and spreading all sorts of gossip and suitably presented counterrevolutionary “information.” He himself is an intriguer and gossip, a typical expression of that spirit of internal emigration which permeated the whole underground “life” of all counterrevolutionary anti-party groups and, particularly and especially, the Zinovievist organization. MAGYAR said: “Grigory wants to see you,” insisted on this meeting and demanded that I go to ZINOVIEV's dacha, indicating the exact time and place—I believe half past three in the afternoon, in July.

The meeting took place between July 10 and 15. I remember these circumstances well because at that time I was undergoing a month and a half of refresher training through the 4th Directorate of the RKKA. I arrived at ZINOVIEV's in military uniform, toward the end of the refresher training.

At half past three I was in Ilyinskoye and called at ZINOVIEV's dacha. KAMENEV was absent; we went into the garden, sat on a felled tree stump and had a long conversation there. I set out my conception, dwelt on affairs in the Comintern, argued that the Comintern leadership was no good and that the parties were entirely unprepared to solve the tasks facing them. ZINOVIEV assented, and the conversation that followed was so prolonged that I left between seven and eight. The substance of the conversation was as follows: I set out to ZINOVIEV my proposal to address a document to the CC. ZINOVIEV said: “Well, you know, we are sitting in trenches; stick out a finger and they will shoot it off. It is like being at the front.”

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Who is “we”?

ANSWER: We means everyone belonging to anti-party groupings. Since this was said after I had set out my credo, it is entirely understandable that ZINOVIEV had every reason to enroll me in the ranks of his counterrevolutionary organization.

ZINOVIEV said: “The difficulties are growing and becoming more acute. This leadership cannot survive either internal or international complications in the existing situation, with the struggle within the leadership. It will fall apart; a leadership crisis is inevitable, but we must not hurry too much; we must take the circumstances into account and consider the situation.

Having said this, he immediately added: “Well, these are such times that writing a platform is simply in the air.” In reply I remarked that a platform evidently ought to be written. He said: “You know, in my opinion a great deal in the old platform is not outdated, and with much of what it contains one can now come into the open without fear.”

I then objected that we had been wrong on two questions: building socialism in one country and Thermidor; that these were Trotskyist tricks and we had broken our heads over them. ZINOVIEV replied: “Why talk about this when you yourself see what they have made of it—of building socialism in one country; you see what is coming of it.”

On Thermidor he said: “Well, this leadership is the cause and source of all misfortunes and difficulties.” By this he meant not only the CC but the whole leadership. “This leadership,” he said, “represents a bureaucracy cut off, isolated and alienated from the masses, and therefore naturally has no connection with living reality.” He then turned to a report he had received from Leningrad. In Leningrad, he said, the workers' food situation was very difficult, and the workers' situation generally very difficult. He spoke of a bureaucratic, dismissive and contemptuous attitude toward workers, toward old workers, the established Leningrad cadres. “The old workers in the enterprises feel like strangers.”

I speak about this in detail to portray the spirit of internal emigration, of complete counterrevolutionary degeneration, which entirely dominated us. In general, one must call things by their proper names.

ZINOVIEV said: “A worker cannot say anything now, because some appointed secretary or snot-nosed Komsomol member will immediately subject him to criticism; the old worker feels like a stranger in production.” From him I heard a more developed repetition of the theory of the peasantization of the proletariat.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: You heard this from ZINOVIEV?

ANSWER: Yes, precisely\! “Construction has expanded the ranks of newcomers from the countryside,” said ZINOVIEV. “The bureaucratic leadership has pushed aside the old proletarian activists; the muzhik has arrived, above the muzhik stands the superstructure of bureaucracy, and on top of that there is the enormous role of young people.” Here he referred to what KUKLIN had related about Leningrad, citing as an example the attitude of EMELYANOV's family, of himself and his sons.

ZINOVIEV must remember this—these are precise and concrete facts. ZINOVIEV spoke of the rejuvenation of the proletariat: “What are these Komsomol boys and girls?\! They are bought with cinema, pleasures, an easy life, the opportunity to enter a higher educational institution after spending a couple of years at a machine. One can do anything—rise to secretary, and so on.”

On relations between the working class and the peasantry, ZINOVIEV spoke as follows: “LENIN wrote that ten to twenty years of proper relations with the peasantry were needed to achieve victory, but STALIN wants to accomplish this in two years.” Hence the threat of a rupture of the alliance; hence what leads directly to an immediate threat to the dictatorship of the proletariat.

I told ZINOVIEV of my conversations with various representatives of anti-party groupings and conveyed my conversations with MRACHKOVSKY and Ivan Nikitich SMIRNOV. ZINOVIEV seized on this—he knew that MRACHKOVSKY and I were old friends—and said: “Yes indeed, they have learned a great deal; one can reach an understanding with them.” ZINOVIEV said that relations with them must be continued, that MRACHKOVSKY was “an energetic man, an old Bolshevik.”

I told ZINOVIEV that I had met SHLYAPNIKOV and related my conversation with SHLYAPNIKOV, who to my question about a platform had said: “We have the old platform.” And when I asked him: “What old platform do you have?” SHLYAPNIKOV replied: “The old one, the one with which we fought against LENIN.”

ZINOVIEV remarked: “Well, as for the old platform, that is nonsense, but he is an old Bolshevik; we must not lose sight of him.”

Then the conversation turned to the meeting with LOMINADZE, with whom I had reconciled after breaking off relations over a book I had published in which I criticized him. ZINOVIEV said: “Look, LOMINADZE and STEN are intelligent men. STEN is very talented. This is the best of Stalin's youth, and how furiously they are now turned against this leadership, against STALIN. Lev Borisovich KAMENEV once saw TOMSKY; TOMSKY visits them (in the CC)—he is a CC member. He says it is utter wretchedness, a pitiful spectacle. He fully agrees that this leadership cannot lead the country out of its difficulties.”

Then the conversation turned to other groupings. ZINOVIEV remarked: “I no longer know how the misunderstanding with BUKHARIN arose. But take KOMAROV and UGLANOV. What remains of our former hostility toward them? They are real Bolsheviks, after all.”

I told ZINOVIEV about the conversation with SMILGA; ZINOVIEV spoke very approvingly of SMILGA: “An old, genuine Bolshevik, he is with us. We must think what to do.”

From ZINOVIEV's statements about the peasantization of the proletariat, their literal coincidence with SMILGA's statements, and SMILGA's constant connection with KAMENEV, I concluded that all this was no accident and that an agreement existed. They declared the proletariat degenerated to justify their own counterrevolutionary degeneration.

In the conversation ZINOVIEV indicated: “STEN has written a platform against the leadership, for inner-party democracy. Meet Lev Borisovich. He has this platform. It would be good if you familiarized yourself with it and expressed your views on this question.” Thus the issue was my attitude toward the Sten–Ryutin platform. ZINOVIEV did not mention RYUTIN's name then.

At this meeting with ZINOVIEV, consequently, a formal agreement with me took place, and afterward the whole Zinovievist organization in Moscow and Leningrad was proclaiming that “the madman has joined us.”

When I visited GERTSBERG a few days later, he greeted me: “Reconciled with Grigory?\! Well, congratulations.” EVDOKIMOV also greeted me accordingly.

When I had a conversation with TOLMAZOV and SEREDOKHIN in the Comintern in 1933, they expressed satisfaction that “everyone has come together.”

These are, approximately, the principal and most important points of the conversation with ZINOVIEV.

I touched on Comintern affairs briefly because when I began speaking about them in more detail, ZINOVIEV interrupted: “I know from MAGYAR what is happening there.”

In conclusion ZINOVIEV said: “We must meet more often. It would be good for you to see Lev Borisovich. You must also see EVDOKIMOV; you have Leningrad connections too.” He had in mind above all the following: among the “leaderless” group were former Komsomol workers who later proved to be counterrevolutionaries, White Guard terrorists.

On parting, ZINOVIEV emphasized that “we need to maintain permanent contact and discuss the matter in practical terms.”

I have outlined the main points of my testimony on this question. If there are questions for me, I shall supplement it.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Specify from whom the initiative for assembling a bloc of anti-party groups came.

ANSWER: To sum up the matter, in my view the initiative for assembling the bloc came from ZINOVIEV, from the Trotskyists and from me, and from ZINOVIEV no less than from me. I have fully acknowledged my crime against the party and Sov\<iet\> power. That the initiative also came from ZINOVIEV is apparent above all from the fact that when I subsequently met EVDOKIMOV, EVDOKIMOV considered that everything had returned to what it was in 1927, when I belonged to the leading center of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: What prevented you from realizing this bloc?

ANSWER: I shall return to this. ZINOVIEV and the Zinovievists wanted to cover up their direct relations with MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV and pretended that I was the Zinovievist plenipotentiary for an agreement with this Trotskyist grouping. In fact, as became entirely clear to me, EVDOKIMOV had direct ties and closeness to MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV no less than mine, if not greater. As far as I know, MRACHKOVSKY had from very long ago been a man, in matters of anti-party conspiracy a man \[2\] far more astute than ZINOVIEV.

He does not admit people to his group whom he does not fully trust. And in his carriage, on his departure in the autumn of 1932, were Shura SAFONOVA, Pyotr PEREVERZEV, SMIRNOV, VAGANYAN and EVDOKIMOV. This was no chance gathering. I stated in my testimony that when MRACHKOVSKY was leaving he told me: “If anything turns up among you concerning a platform, write to me,” and gave me Shura SAFONOVA's telephone number. He indicated that PEREVERZEV would be going first, in a few days, and the document could be sent through him. A few weeks later Shura SAFONOVA would be going to the Far East and could serve as a carrier.

On the whole, taking the principal points of assessment and the conclusions from the standpoint of political content, the platform was ready. On the question of the appeal to the CC which I envisaged, I found myself alone. In talking with me ZINOVIEV even said: “Writing is not difficult. We must reach agreement, then sit down at the table and write. We are all writers, and it is not difficult for us to do this.” I remember this phrase about writers very well.

Why did this fall through? It is entirely clear that it was because the Sten–Ryutin group was broken up. STEN, who was the pivotal figure in this whole chain, was arrested. This caused a corresponding depression in spirits. Then ZINOVIEV himself was exiled. After this happened I visited EVDOKIMOV at the 27th House of Soviets, and he said that “now we must wait, because things did not turn out as expected.”

That is what EVDOKIMOV said, while I was deeply indignant that they had made such a fuss and once again produced only a squeak, referring to ZINOVIEV's statement at the TsKK. EVDOKIMOV replied: “Well, all the same ZINOVIEV did say something: that uncertainty and anxiety are felt in the country.”

I objected that “this is once again a rehearsal of the attempt to put an ear to the ground.” “Now we must say that the matter is postponed,” replied EVDOKIMOV.

Then I visited Ivan Nikitich SMIRNOV. This was before going to EVDOKIMOV, because there was a certain interval between STEN's arrest and ZINOVIEV's exile. When STEN and RYUTIN were arrested, I went to SMIRNOV's apartment and told him: “Are we really going to be imprisoned over a kulak platform? What if the OGPU arrests us today or tomorrow? What shall we say?” He replied: “You see that things have become complicated; the date has shifted. Let each collective farm decide for itself.” He called his organization a collective farm. He said a collective farm or “each holding.” By “holding” was meant one or another counterrevolutionary anti-party grouping or the corresponding organization. The conversation with SMIRNOV ended there.

It is entirely clear to me, and was clear to everyone, that the formal unification of people in a counterrevolutionary bloc had in essence taken place, but no platform written on paper emerged because the OGPU broke up the Sten–Ryutin organization and ZINOVIEV was exiled.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm SAFAROV's testimony?

ANSWER: I shall confirm what is partly true, and shall also say what is untrue. It is unclear to me: SAFAROV said he visited me between July 5 and 10.

SAFAROV: No, between July 10 and 15.

ZINOVIEV: I wanted to establish that the whole affair of the Ryutin platform was considerably later, and I could not have had this platform in July.

SAFAROV: Do you deny that I visited you in military uniform?

ZINOVIEV: I do not remember in what uniform.

SAFAROV: You were accustomed to seeing me in a jacket; nor was I in the habit of going about with a Nagant revolver; I was then in military uniform because during that period I had been called up for military exercises in the Red Army.

ZINOVIEV: Let it be military uniform; I proceed from the date SAFAROV himself named.

I ask the investigation to establish—you possess all the information about this—that at that time RYUTIN's platform was not in my possession. I have told the investigation more than once that my recollection is that I received the platform toward the end of summer, toward autumn, and in July I could not have known of it. This is of enormous importance in this whole case.

SAFAROV says here that between July 10 and 15 I sent him to KAMENEV to familiarize himself with this platform and that in this platform STEN speaks about inner-party democracy. Absolutely none of this happened, just as STEN did not write the Ryutin platform. What does he understand by this platform, that is, Ryutin's? He—SAFAROV—himself said that it was a kulak platform, not a platform whose most characteristic feature was the demand for inner-party democracy. In July, in my opinion, I could not have had the Ryutin platform, and could not have said that STEN was writing a platform.

As for the first part of SAFAROV's account, how various persons directed him to me, I cannot judge; possibly that is how it was, that SMIRNOV and SMILGA pushed him toward me.

I have already set out in my testimony that in 1932, in connection with the difficulties, rumors circulated about my anti-party attitudes, that a number of people were directed to me, most likely because it was considered that KAMENEV and I had standing from our past political activity and that it was necessary to involve me and KAMENEV in one or another action. I cannot deny or dispute this.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: During his visit did SAFAROV tell you about negotiations with the Trotskyists, with LOMINADZE and others?

ANSWER: With LOMINADZE I do not remember; he spoke about SHLYAPNIKOV, but I attached little significance to it.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Did SAFAROV tell you about negotiations with MRACHKOVSKY and SMILGA?

ANSWER: I allow that he spoke about SMILGA, although I also met SMILGA myself.

SAFAROV and I spoke about the country's economic situation, but I made no proposal about a platform and did not say that we must enter the struggle against the leadership; I criticized the general line and a number of economic measures then being carried out, and said there was no system.

As for my meeting with SAFAROV, if he says it was through MAGYAR, I allow this; it seemed to me that EVDOKIMOV proposed that I see SAFAROV; possibly it was through MAGYAR too.

A good half of the conversation with SAFAROV was spent on his recounting his impressions of one of the republics—either Uzbekistan or Kazakhstan—where he had been, and describing the situation there in the darkest colors. He said there was a terrible famine, corpses lay about in the streets, and one could not travel by car without encountering a corpse. I heard about Kazakhstan from other sources in the same vein.

As for other attitudes, they were wavering, but I said none of what SAFAROV has said here. For example, I did not speak of the peasantization of the proletariat, nor did I say that far too many had been admitted to the party. I was always accused of the opposite, of wanting to admit too many to the party.

SAFAROV: That was when there was no five-year plan\!

ZINOVIEV: I could not have said that; as for the collective letter, I do not remember.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: You constantly refer to poor memory and forgetfulness; you must give precise answers.

ANSWER: If this letter had existed, I probably would have spoken against it, because I did not want struggle or organized action. I spoke of a set of doubts on the peasant question, but there were no views differing from the party's on the course of collectivization as a whole; likewise, concerning young people, I do not admit that I could have spoken of their excessively great role.

I may have complained that old cadres were not being given opportunities—that is true—that old workers were not trusted; that may have happened, I may have said that.

Further, SAFAROV speaks of connections with SMILGA and MRACHKOVSKY; here SAFAROV's account does not work well: I wanted to present him (SAFAROV) as a “plenipotentiary” before MRACHKOVSKY and SMILGA, but why would I need to do this when EVDOKIMOV could be regarded as my “plenipotentiary”? This is incomprehensible.

All I remember about this matter is what EVDOKIMOV told me before my departure for Kustanai. He told me that I.N. SMIRNOV had proposed establishing contact and conferring with SMIRNOV, MRACHKOVSKY and, as far as I remember, VAGANYAN. I do not know whether SMIRNOV could be believed or not, but in his conversation with EVDOKIMOV he stated that he did not at that moment consider himself a Trotskyist, that Trotsky had slid into the counterrevolutionary camp, but that SMIRNOV and his people were dissatisfied with the state of affairs in the party and the country and wanted to ascertain “our,” that is, the “Zinovievists',” views on the situation. I spoke against this, EVDOKIMOV gave a negative reply, and no conference took place. I do not know about EVDOKIMOV's meeting in the carriage with MRACHKOVSKY and others; it must be ascertained whether this meeting was before my departure or afterward.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Did EVDOKIMOV have any grounds for concealing from you his visit to MRACHKOVSKY in the carriage?

ANSWER: I do not think so. Perhaps this meeting was before my departure, but I do not remember.

SAFAROV: It was before the departure, because there was not even any talk of STEN's arrest then.

ZINOVIEV: EVDOKIMOV did not tell me the actual fact of the meeting in the carriage, but he did tell me that SMIRNOV had approached him with the proposal mentioned.

As for Leningrad connections, there was no mention of this in the conversation with SAFAROV and could not have been, since for a number of years I had maintained no ties with Leningrad. In 1929 I considered that there would be people in both Moscow and Leningrad, and even in considerable numbers, who would support me in the event of a party discussion. But now these ties did not exist. Let SAFAROV say what connection, what was discussed, and so on.

SAFAROV spoke of the meeting involving PEREVERZEV Pyotr and Shura SAFONOVA, but I must say that I saw PEREVERZEV in 1927, whereas I did not see SAFONOVA or hear this name in connection with EVDOKIMOV.

As for the fact that a number of conversations revolved around me because I could supposedly unite a new bloc and set myself against the party, that is true. That I had vacillations in 1932 is also true; they were then connected with the peasant question. That I expressed a number of anti-party considerations to SAFAROV is true, but as for a platform, that “we shall sit down and write”—there was no talk of that. I left Moscow on December 12, and the meeting with SAFAROV was in July. If a platform was to be drawn up, who prevented us from beginning to write?

At that time I did not regard SAFAROV as a representative of any group, but as an old comrade who had been connected with the Leningrad opposition, and so on. It is true that it gave me some pleasure that SAFAROV, who after the Fifteenth Congress had called me and oth\<ers\> capitulators, traitors and so forth, had had to acknowledge that these accusations were wrong.

He says that I praised KOMAROV and UGLANOV, that these were “our people,” and so on. SAFAROV put this unclearly, but I know that I had nothing in common with KOMAROV and UGLANOV. If I maintained ties with such Rightists as BUKHARIN and TOMSKY, these were very old ties; for a time there was personal and mutual respect, since they had once been Politburo members, and so forth. As for UGLANOV, my attitude toward him had been negative even during LENIN's lifetime. I considered KOMAROV a Rightist, and I could have had no personal or political contact with him, nor could I have praised him.

I confirm that in 1932 I had strong anti-party vacillations, but there was nothing coherent, no coherent views that would have expressed themselves in a desire to draw up a platform or write a letter to the CC. There was a desire to hear everyone out and wait to see how events would develop.

SAFAROV says that at the TsKK, during the consideration of the Ryutin affair, I supposedly behaved demonstratively and stated that the country was experiencing a feeling of anxiety. I stated that “a vague idea of retreat is in the air”—that did happen.

If one takes what SOLTS was saying, what the Right were saying at the time, and takes the vacillations I myself had regarding the pace of collectivization and what SAFAROV related about Kazakhstan, then it was a vague idea of retreat which I rejected and toward which I was at the same time sliding. There were no demonstrations on my part at the TsKK; my error consists in not having set out before the TsKK all the names of those who tried to cobble together an anti-party bloc, what had occurred between EVDOKIMOV and SMIRNOV, and so on. This was my crime, but I staged no demonstrations at the TsKK.

SAFAROV: I recall that I had two meetings with ZINOVIEV. The first meeting—the detailed one—of which I spoke. The second was when I returned from vacation; I had been in Kazakhstan, and the account of the situation in Kazakhstan of which ZINOVIEV spoke relates to this.

I returned by September 1. During August I was in Kazakhstan. ZINOVIEV asserts that SMILGA set out no platform to me but only criticized, and that he, like ZINOVIEV, had “strongly vacillating attitudes.”

These assertions are entirely incredible, because the conversation was among people sufficiently politically developed to understand that definite views were being set out with a certain consistency, that views developed around a definite core and added up to a definite system of counterrevolutionary views.

It is not for me to explain to ZINOVIEV that SMILGA had such a system of views, so that, as ZINOVIEV said, “one could sit down at the table and write down a platform.”

I am astonished that it can be maintained that there was no assembling of a bloc, no attempt to unite all the groupings. I categorically insist that ZINOVIEV said quite precisely: “Look at Stalin's activists—at STEN and LOMINADZE. I have now become more closely acquainted with STEN, and STEN cannot speak calmly of this leadership.” Those are his literal words. As for “a kulak platform and inner-party democracy do not fit together,” I do not think it necessary to explain here why ZINOVIEV's assertion is wrong.

ZINOVIEV said that “STALIN is doing in two years what LENIN proposed doing in ten to twenty years. Hence an almost accomplished rupture of the alliance with the peasantry.” Is that not from a kulak platform?

All our conversations, discussions and debates amounted to our expressing the attitudes of the bourgeois-restorationist reaction against socialism. You say “vacillations”? That is when one wavers in different directions.

Point to even one positive idea in our arsenal, even one idea that did not come down to “STALIN went too far there, went too far here; here one must be gentler on the turns, here a slower pace is needed\!” That is precisely reaction against socialism.

I believe that you think one thing and tell the investigation another. I cannot suppose that you have lost the capacity for political generalization. The issue was that the platform of the whole counterrevolutionary bloc was being cultivated.

I remember very well what I myself said. It is useless for ZINOVIEV to evade. I myself said: “What STALIN is doing toward the muzhik, the Trotskyists would not have done—in the sense of excesses; what is being done toward the workers, even TOMSKY would not have done.” You answered me with an account of KUKLIN's impressions, reports about EMELYANOV and about how the workers felt, how the established Leningrad worker felt; you developed the theory of the degeneration of the proletariat.

I have no need to prove that if in 1925–1927 the counterrevolutionary opposition favored expanding the party's limits, this in no way means that the same was said during the first five-year plan. It is time now to understand that we fought against that five-year plan. We were representatives of bourgeois counterrevolution, exponents and gatherers of the hatred and spite of the exploiters being crushed. Under the conditions of the proletarian dictatorship, a very strong dictatorship, counterrevolution employs the methods it can employ. Counterrevolutionary gossip, provocations and “platforms” serve this purpose.

This weapon is of very great importance to counterrevolution, because when these hostile elements are politically and economically crushed by the might of the proletarian dictatorship, they seek people who could generalize, express and propagandize their attitudes and desires in disguised form, in order to strike the heaviest blow against the party and Soviet power.

This is precisely the issue. The issue is the political responsibility of all of us who grouped together against the party and organized the struggle against the party, the struggle that led to the shot against KIROV. That is what is at issue.

ZINOVIEV states: “SMIRNOV told EVDOKIMOV that TROTSKY had gone over to counterrevolution.” I categorically maintain that this is not so. I am not so close to SMIRNOV; I am very close to MRACHKOVSKY. I remember that in my doctrinaire literary blindness I reasoned thus: “If I acknowledge that building socialism in one country is possible and that the Trotskyists are mistaken, this guarantees that I myself will not be a counterrevolutionary.”

This shameful and vile duplicity was characteristic of the entire Zinovievist organization. Verbal statements supposedly constitute grounds for saying that we were not counterrevolutionaries\!…

When I spoke to MRACHKOVSKY about building socialism in one country, Thermidor and Trotsky, he replied: “I am not for Trotsky or for the Right. We shall reach an understanding with each other because we have known each other well for many years. We are people known to each other and we shall reach an understanding.”

Thus the counterrevolutionary bloc was assembled, with diplomacy and omissions, using personal attachments and connections, and this was characteristic of its lack of principle.

The Leningrad connections were known to ZINOVIEV. When I broke away from their group with the group of the “leaderless,” former Komsomol workers who later became fascists, RUMYANTSEV was in that group. When I submitted a declaration to the TsKK in 1928, RUMYANTSEV and others were “indignant” that I had “given too much.” Hence, from 1928, the rapprochement of these Leningrad “leaderless”—TOLMAZOV, SEREDOKHIN, KOTOLYNOV and others—with the Zinovievists and their entry into the Zinovievist organization, which had preserved its entire organizational network.

EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, GERTIK and SAKHOV regularly visited Leningrad not to see the Lord God, but to see quite definite persons engaged in common counterrevolutionary work. The issue was day-to-day contact maintained, in particular, through SAKHOV, who was in the Procuracy.

Volodka LEVIN came here frequently enough; it was with him that RUMYANTSEV first became close; EVDOKIMOV, GERTIK, KUKLIN—all were part of this connection. When a Zinovievist came to Moscow, he visited either Ivan BAKAEV, GERTIK or EVDOKIMOV.

Our meetings in Ilyinskoye look like an anecdote in ZINOVIEV's account. One must tell the investigation the facts truthfully and precisely, conscious of the serious political responsibility, utterly tragic, which rests on my shoulders and yours.

It turns out that ZINOVIEV met SAFAROV because SAFAROV returned to the party in 1928. It turns out that because, when he was “leader of the leaderless,” SAFAROV called ZINOVIEV a “capitulator, traitor and so on,” therefore in 1932, when SAFAROV was precisely experiencing a fit of anti-party, anti-Soviet “fury,” he met ZINOVIEV to say that “ZINOVIEV was not a capitulator.” This is an anecdote\!

I remember that when I spoke to ZINOVIEV about the platform I pointed out that we must hurry with this, because “we may be imprisoned any day now; what shall we say then?” I said that we must hurry and proposed an appeal with a platform to the CC. ZINOVIEV replied: “How can one hurry here? How many years did it take for you and me to come together on this stump?”

And you do not remember even this, if you “do not know” about the Leningrad connections?\!

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Answer ZINOVIEV's remark that in July he could not have been told about the platform on STEN's authority. Do you confirm that at your first meeting ZINOVIEV told you about this platform?

ANSWER: Yes, I do.

ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm that there was a platform in July?

SAFAROV: You then expressed yourself in your particular language, saying that there was a “sketch.” You and I understood equally what this “sketch” meant. It was a platform.

ZINOVIEV: I did not even say that there was a sketch. The Ryutin platform came into my hands at the end of summer. And I had no idea that it was being worked out.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: At the previous interrogation you testified that the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization systematically displayed an acutely hostile attitude toward the leadership of the VKP(b), that this inflamed terrorist attitudes among the organization's participants. Do you confirm this?

SAFAROV: I confirm it. In today's account of the conversation “on the stump” at ZINOVIEV's dacha I passed over this question because I concentrated on political and organizational questions, touching on matters that should above all refresh ZINOVIEV's memory.

The question of the attitude toward the leadership. In this conversation in July in Ilyinskoye, ZINOVIEV spoke bitterly against the CC, against STALIN, about the need to remove this leadership. I shall remind him of a phrase which, incidentally, was constantly repeated in our center of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc during 1926–1927. “You know this man (here he made a bitter counterrevolutionary attack against STALIN); he is capable of pouring poison into an ear unnoticed.”

ZINOVIEV: I have already had read to me this lying phrase of yours which you attribute to me.

SAFAROV: That phrase was first uttered in 1926 by Lev Borisovich KAMENEV. At one meeting of the center you said: “See how excellently KAMENEV expressed the essence of the matter.”

That phrase speaks for itself, enabling a corresponding assessment of all this.

I can remind you of something else too: I said then, in this same conversation with you: “Would it really be better without STALIN? Without STALIN it would be worse.” But you replied: “Well, this leadership is incapable of doing anything. Who leads in Leningrad? KIROV, CHUDOV\! KIROV is more occupied with hunting; a worker cannot get to him.”

All this was said by ZINOVIEV in support of his thesis about a “bureaucracy standing above classes, alien to the working class.”

Then your account of the conversation between KAMENEV and TOMSKY, that “the CC is a pitiful spectacle?\!”

This is a system of inculcating fascist attitudes of internal emigration throughout one's circle, a system that inevitably had to nourish, and did nourish, terrorist attitudes and tendencies.

It is entirely clear that the party treated us too considerately, not compelling each of us—including you and me—to disarm genuinely, that is, to reveal the organizational network with which we were connected and with which you were connected all along.

It was precisely this organizational network that proved to be the casing in which the bullet that killed Comrade KIROV was nurtured. It is long past time to understand this.

This system was a continuation of the whole system of struggle against the party in 1925–1927, a continuation of the system of preparation for the Fourteenth Party Congress. You have probably forgotten: “First at a quarter voice, then at half voice, then at full voice” \[3\]? You spoke at half voice about the leadership and incited people against it. RUMYANTSEV spoke “at full voice” and organized the murder.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: What can you say about this?

ZINOVIEV: In my previous testimony I said in what sense and why moral and political responsibility for Comrade KIROV's murder rests on me as well. I have already said this, but it seems to me that every word must be weighed; details must not be confused.

SAFAROV: It is deeds that must now be weighed, not words.

ZINOVIEV: The issue is ten years of struggle. If there was a pilgrimage of visitors to me, as SAFAROV says, then when and in what year?

SAFAROV spoke here of abuse directed at Comrade STALIN. If this relates to 1926, both KAMENEV and I may have said worse at that time.

Of course, sharp counterrevolutionary attacks against the party leadership took place during the struggle in connection with preparations for November 7, 1927, and immediately afterward; in particular, in connection with China there were the most desperate, counterrevolutionary statements. This history is known to everyone.

The issue is what happened after that.

How can what happened before the Fourteenth Congress, “first at half voice and at the congress itself at full voice,” be compared with what was said later?

It is clear that these counterrevolutionary facts must not be confused with what happened in 1932, 1933 and 1934.

SAFAROV says that I never broke counterrevolutionary ties with Leningrad; can he name any instance connected with Leningrad? Can he say that in 1932, 1933 and 1934 I maintained ties, that I gave directives, that people carried out any assignments from me?

He creates the impression that he acknowledges everything and I do not.

I know what responsibility I must bear, especially after what the investigator told me about the trial. I am ready to say everything I know, but I cannot say what did not happen.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Whenever you are asked a concrete question, you always refer to your forgetfulness, your loss of memory, or deny it altogether. You must answer the investigation on the substance of the questions put to you.

ANSWER: Of course, over ten years, with my past, it is difficult for me not to be forgetful.

Can SAFAROV say at what time there was a pilgrimage to me? Can he say it was in 1933, 1932 and 1934?

He may have conjectures, but there are no such facts.

Of course, people traveled to me in 1927, 1928 and 1929, but this could not have occurred later.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm the conversation about the party leadership in 1932 at your meeting with SAFAROV?

ZINOVIEV: Of course there was a conversation about the leadership, but not in the style SAFAROV recounts. There could not have been such sharp conversations as SAFAROV recounts.

The five-year plan clearly showed that in the main the party line was vindicated; of course we doubted and vacillated, since we had never even dreamed of the five-year plan.

Thought worked as if in a mousetrap: there is a poor peasant in the countryside, he must be supported, he must be given a horse; but I had not even dreamed of this path—the path of the five-year plan.

It is clear that in 1932 there were difficulties that aroused in our group hostility toward individual party measures. This created unfriendly attitudes among us.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: What does “unfriendly attitudes” mean?

ANSWER: Hostile, or rather anti-party. From this it followed that I did not report STEN, did not manage to speak openly about attempts to assemble an anti-party bloc, about negotiations with the Trotskyists, and so on. But we had no organization in Leningrad.

SAFAROV: If there had been none, there could have been no shot at KIROV either.

ZINOVIEV: The shot at KIROV was the work of a fascist group, but in my previous testimony I said why and in what sense I am answerable even for it, since in the past I was connected with six persons.

At the beginning of the investigation I protested indignantly that no, under no circumstances was I guilty of the shot at Comrade KIROV; but when I saw that of the fourteen persons printed in the newspaper I knew six personally, that they had belonged to the anti-party group in which I too had been, and when they referred directly to the platform of 1925–1927 in whose drafting I participated, then I must say that I bear moral and political responsibility.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Which six persons are you speaking of?

ANSWER: I was given the newspaper to read and found six persons known to me in the indictment, namely RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV, TOLMAZOV, LEVIN, MYASNIKOV, MANDELSHTAM; I do not know the others.

When I saw this I understood that historically things had indeed developed such that moral and political responsibility rested on me too.

I hope that the investigation must see the objective facts in the case of Comrade KIROV's murder.

But it did not happen that I abused STALIN in a conversation with SAFAROV in 1932 and that the shot followed from this. One must see how it all really happened.

SAFAROV mentioned two meetings here. It is unpleasant for me not to remember, but apparently that was so. There are two months between July and September. Why then was the platform which we supposedly wanted to draft not drawn up in those two months, and what did SAFAROV and I discuss at our second meeting in September?

In my opinion, if you establish the facts, you will be convinced that in July I had no platform from STEN; in September I allow that I had already read this platform, but I could not have referred to it in discussion as “Sten's” platform.

Of STEN I said that he was the best among Stalin's youth; I may have expressed satisfaction that someone who had fought against me was now with me, and that it had supposedly turned out I had been partly right.

It is true that, looking back now, I am compelled to say that what took place in 1932 was anti-party collusion, counterrevolution.

I may also have spoken to SAFAROV about EMELYANOV. I have known him a long time, and you know what role he played in the past. He was an old worker who really did tell me that he was not being given opportunities. I may have referred to this, but that worker did not participate in our grouping. I know this.

SAFAROV: Both of EMELYANOV's sons were in the Leningrad opposition.

ZINOVIEV: One son of EMELYANOV was a definite Trotskyist. When he was arrested I openly interceded for him (I wrote, I believe, to Comrade MOLOTOV), since I had known him as a boy. The man may have gone too far, I thought. That is what I said about EMELYANOV, but not about maintaining ties with him.

There was no conversation in 1932 about opposing a platform to the CC's line, no conversation that the five-year plan was no good, and so on.

I had the deepest vacillations; there were elements of doubt about the correctness of the CC's line, but there was no talk of any platform.

SAFAROV: The point is that after the Fifteenth Congress the Zinovievist organization was adapted to conditions of particularly conspiratorial work “by a chain,” through visits, meetings, information and small gatherings.

I cannot produce notarized Zinovievist directives. But the organization worked precisely to leave as few written traces as possible. There was oral transmission, transmission along the chain. And the situation was this: what you said in Moscow would be heard in Leningrad. This is entirely exact, and individual facts can be cited.

In 1932 I visited Leningrad, called on KOTOLYNOV at home, and he told me of my assessments of Comintern work, attitudes and the state of affairs in the Comintern when I began telling him about these matters.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: What conclusion do you draw from this?

ANSWER: The conclusion that the conversation I had with ZINOVIEV, MAGYAR and EVDOKIMOV had become known to KOTOLYNOV.

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Was this in 1932?

ANSWER: Yes, in the summer of 1932. The matter concerned concrete things, not simply SAFAROV's negative attitude toward the situation in the Comintern. I maintained that “they do not apply united-front tactics when necessary” (I wrote about this in my testimony), that “there will be a sharp turn, a sudden blow, and they will slide back to the Anglo-Russian Committee.” KOTOLYNOV could not have picked these details out of thin air; they convey a definite conception.

I am simply disarmed by this specifically Zinovievist “ignorance” and “forgetting” of everything that happened.

The Leningrad people were directly connected with EVDOKIMOV. And who did not know that “all the news must be obtained from EVDOKIMOV, GERTIK, BAKAEV”? In 1932 I meet Nikolai DMITRIEV. I ask: “Where are you coming from?” He replies: “From GERTIK's; I visited EVDOKIMOV.” I meet Yakov ELKOVICH—he says the same thing.

Why should I speak of 1928–1929, when I was then sufficiently distant from you\! I am speaking of 1932.

Is this not an organization, if almost the very next day after talking with ZINOVIEV I visit GERTSBERG and he congratulates me on having returned to them? How did they learn this? There were no witnesses to the conversation\!

I must recall small details. ZINOVIEV had a domestic servant who had previously worked for us, and I said: “Why did you take on this old woman? Tomorrow everything will be known to the OGPU. Let us rather sit and talk in the garden.” Do you not remember even this?

Along your chain of connections everything spread like an underground radio. You are trying to portray the crime in the guise of an innocent sinner. According to you, RUMYANTSEV and KOTOLYNOV became fascists in 1934 because they “misinterpreted” what ZINOVIEV said in 1925?\!

In 1933 TOLMAZOV and SEREDOKHIN came to see me in the Comintern and said: “Now everyone has come together.”

QUESTION TO SAFAROV: Everyone who?

ANSWER: The Leningrad people.

ZINOVIEV: In 1933?

SAFAROV: Yes, in 1933\! One can go through dozens of instances pointing to a differentiated and developed organizational connection of the counterrevolutionary underground, whose threads led to ZINOVIEV. Here is another example.

After the exposure of STEN's group, KNORIN gave a report to a restricted circle of party activists in the Comintern. I told EVDOKIMOV. Afterward, at UFIMTSEV's apartment I met Sasha SIMASHKO, and she recounted this information of mine to me in every detail. The next day I told MAGYAR that it was impossible to deal with the Zinovievists—“if people are thinking of political activity, what sort of wireless telegraph is this? Today I spoke to EVDOKIMOV, and the next day others serve up my information to me.”

Dozens of such cases can be cited. Just as radio replaces newspapers, counterrevolutionary rumors and “information” can replace radio in the most conspiratorial anti-party work.

I am astonished that ZINOVIEV “does not see” all this.

Now the story of the group of former Komsomol members. This was a group that withdrew directly with me, splitting from the Zinovievist opposition.

What happens when I submitted a declaration to the party? It is entirely natural that others conclude that the old differences between ZINOVIEV and myself are being erased and that I have no grounds to call ZINOVIEV a traitor and capitulator.

Then RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV and others were indignant at my declaration to the TsKK, saying that I had “given too much.” In Leningrad, when I visited in 1928, I encountered indignation among the majority of the “leaderless,” and they condemned me. On this basis the migration into the Zinovievist organization began.

In 1929 rapprochement took place between RUMYANTSEV and Vladimir LEVIN. Previously RUMYANTSEV could not stand him and considered LEVIN a one-hundred-percent Zinovievist, doing only what the “leader” told him. And here “suddenly” they found contact in counterrevolutionary work\!

Why say things that do not correspond to reality?

ZINOVIEV states that I present everything “in a terrible light.” The issue lies not in my words but in the facts, in their political essence. All information was transmitted along your chain; my return to you was spread into every nook and cranny because this “raised the spirits” of participants in counterrevolutionary work, insofar as I enjoyed a certain authority in that field.

One must assume political responsibility for the crime, rather than chatter about “deep elements of doubt.” I do not know where there is a serious attitude toward the matter here. “Elements of doubt”\! “The CC is no good; it must be removed”—this is called “elements of doubt”\!

We have entirely opposite views of things.

The vile murder of KIROV by fascists forced me to think matters through to the end and assess our whole shameful path. It is shameful that only this tragedy made us take hold of our heads. But you have not thought this through. You do not want to call things by their proper names.

When meeting ZINOVIEV in September 1932 we spoke of Comintern affairs and Kazakhstan. On Comintern affairs ZINOVIEV said: “Hitler is advancing; the party is helpless before Hitler's advance; the party does not apply united-front tactics, and Hitler will crush us.” Hence a defeatist policy.

Of Kazakhstan you said you already had information.

ZINOVIEV: I asked SAFAROV to name facts about connections in recent years. He says that when he saw KOTOLYNOV in 1932 the latter conveyed information from SAFAROV's conversation with me. SAFAROV himself says that at that time he was connected with both MRACHKOVSKY and \<with\> SHLYAPNIKOV.

SAFAROV: With whom KOTOLYNOV was not connected.

ZINOVIEV: It follows further from SAFAROV's words that at that same time he was connected with SHATSKIN and LOMINADZE, who (that is, LOMINADZE) used to run to him and was interested in all Comintern questions. They had their own line of contact with f\<ormer\> Komsomol members, for they too had worked in the Komsomol. SAFAROV also had his own line of contact with the young people at that time, and was connected with the Trotskyists too.

If KOTOLYNOV knew what SAFAROV was saying about him, what follows—that I informed KOTOLYNOV along the chain? But this did not happen and could not have happened.

SAFAROV says that he himself saw KOTOLYNOV in 1932, having a direct connection with him. How many intervening meetings were there, do you remember, SAFAROV? Of course you do not remember. Why then does KOTOLYNOV's knowledge of my conversation with SAFAROV come from me?

I did not know where KOTOLYNOV was and had no idea about this for several years. SAFAROV says that he met people walking along the street from BAKAEV's. This must be true. Therefore SAFAROV knew of these connections.

I told the investigation that EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and oth\<ers\> had family ties with Leningrad people. People came to them from Leningrad and stayed overnight, but what connection has this had with me in recent years?

There is still a difference between people staying overnight and the existence of some organization.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: Your answer is not serious.

ZINOVIEV: People had been connected for decades when they had no connection with counterrevolutionary work. One must not confuse everything in one tangle. On the domestic, everyday and comradely level, of course there were ties. I allow that there were ties in Petersburg too, but what this has in common with an organization existing until very recently “by chains,” I do not understand.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: This is confirmed by dozens of your like-minded associates, who have testified how illegal contact operated along a chain and without a chain, while you reduce everything to everyday gossip.

ZINOVIEV: In recent years none of the Leningrad people was connected with me.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: It has been established that everything comes from you as the original source.

ANSWER: This cannot have been the case in recent years; I know this perfectly well.

QUESTION TO ZINOVIEV: What else can you add?

ZINOVIEV: Everything I knew, I have said. I can add nothing more.

The answers to the questions put to me have been recorded correctly:

ZINOVIEV.

The answers to the questions put to me have been recorded correctly:

G. SAFAROV.
