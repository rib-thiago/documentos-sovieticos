INTERROGATION RECORD

OF SAFAROV, G.I., January 1, 1935.

In addition to my testimony, I consider it necessary to draw the investigation's attention to the following points. After Fyodor KOMAROV was sent to work in Moscow and other representatives of the Right grouping were transferred to economic work, the conviction took root among them that they were “sidelined, wronged,” and so forth. Among the most prominent figures of this Right grouping are F. KOMAROV, Ivan Ivanovich KONDRATYEV, Taras KONDRATYEV, SERGANIN \[1\], AMENITSKY, DESOV, REKSTIN, KAROL, VOLODIN and MALETSKY. They all regard F. KOMAROV as their “head.” Their dissatisfaction with party policy and sense of collective grievance led to their covert rapprochement with the Zinovievists. There is no doubt that a number of Zinovievists—Nikol\<ai\> DMITRIYEV, ABRAMSON and other\<s\>—were able to return relatively easily to Leningrad to continue their counterrevolutionary work, which could not have been unknown to the Rightists, who knew all these Zinovievists personally very well, only by using their connections and acquaintances among the Leningrad Rightists. The Rightists skillfully and unobtrusively helped the Zinovievists in this matter, venting their bitterness against the CC. DESOV's vile libel against S.M. KIROV, which found support among the Zinovievists (P. ZALUTSKY), could not have appeared without the sanction of the entire Leningrad Right grouping, which always displayed great cohesion. One focal point of the rapprochement between the Rightists and the Zinovievists was A. KUKLIN's personal closeness to F. KOMAROV, the “old Vyborg comrades” and the “old Sormovo comrades.” LEBEDEV also played a certain role here; Ivan CHUGURIN hovered around, and old KAYUROV also associated with this group. F. KOMAROV's main connections are concentrated on the Vyborg Side, above all among former workers of the Arsenal, Phoenix and New Lessner factories. Fyodor KOMAROV, who was not inclined to advertise his connections, especially with the Zinovievists, very probably met those whom he considered closer to himself—first and foremost Aleksandr KUKLIN, then Pyotr ZALUTSKY and possibly EVDOKIMOV. The Sovnarkom dining room, in particular, could serve as convenient cover for such meetings. Besides, both KUKLIN and KOMAROV visited Leningrad and had many mutual friends strongly attached to them personally. I saw KOMAROV only once in 1932, at the book distribution center on Gorky Street. There was no political conversation, but he indicated that he sympathized with the “wronged” Zinovievists, considering himself wronged as well. KOMAROV had a long-standing closeness—both political and personal—to Aleksandr Petrovich SMIRNOV and UGLANOV, who also had connections of his own in Leningrad. I remember that at a meeting in Ilyinskoye in July 1932, ZINOVIEV lavishly praised F. KOMAROV and UGLANOV as “steadfast old Bolsheviks” and said: “What is left of our old enmity?” He also advised me to see someone from the Right, and I intended to call on UGLANOV, but he was arrested in the meantime. I know that Vladimir LEVIN was close to such representatives of the Right grouping as REKSTIN and SERGANIN and used their “information” about affairs in Leningrad to pass on to ZINOVIEV.

Written in my own hand—G. SAFAROV.

INTERROGATED BY:

DEPUTY HEAD OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD OF THE USSR—LYUSHKOV

HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD OF THE USSR—PETROVSKY
