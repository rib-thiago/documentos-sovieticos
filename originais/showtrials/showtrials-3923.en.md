INTERROGATION RECORD

OF ANISHEV Anatoly Isayevich, dated December 30, 1934.

In response to the questions put to me by the investigation, I give the following additional testimony:

1\. On what did the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization place its hopes, both in Moscow, its Moscow center, and in our Leningrad counterrevolutionary organization?

Not on the victory of socialism, since every success of socialist construction in the USSR postponed and made ever more doubtful the prospect of our being brought back into the leadership.

It was clear to all of us that we had no positive program. We did not even attempt to work one out, taking cover behind the argument, as GERTIK did in conversation with me and NATANSON, that without being in the leadership one could not develop such a program.

Our hopes were placed on difficulties, on the defeat of Soviet power. It was a treacherous wager on peasant revolt, on the discontent of the working class, on the defeats of Communist parties in the West, and so forth.

The Trotskyist thesis concerning Clemenceau had not left the stage.

The treacherous talk that the peasantry, forcibly driven into collective farms, would not defend Soviet power in the event of war was precisely a wager on Soviet power's defeat.

This position was formulated quite clearly by ELKOVICH (who came to maintain contact with the Leningrad organization after the ZINOVIEV–RYUTIN affair), undoubtedly constituting the orientation not only of ELKOVICH but also of those from whom he received directives and guidance, that is, the leading center of the Zinovievist organization.

Accounts of famine in Ukraine, accounts of entire villages being deported in the North Caucasus, accounts of peasant uprisings (ELKOVICH) — all this was seized upon and inflated in a way that only outright counterrevolutionaries could act, expecting by these very means to fulfill their task of returning to the leadership.

My account that wolves had appeared on Ukrainian beet plantations was given at a “tea party” at BAKAEV's in the summer of 1932, where, besides BAKAEV and KOSTINA, I, ELKOVICH, and GESSEN were present. This account was seized upon as an indication that the leadership could not cope with organizing collective-farm production.

RUMYANTSEV's accounts of alleged failures in the loan-subscription campaign (early 1933), like SAFAROV's information (in 1932, at his apartment) about a wave of strikes, were all received as facts confirming the correctness of our proposition that the leadership's collapse was inevitable. And that collapse was awaited impatiently, since there was no other way back into the leadership — that was clear to all of us.

The inflation of rumors of uprisings, strikes, and failures on the economic front was accompanied by whispers about disagreements within the Central Committee.

We moved throughout this time in this counterrevolutionary, treacherous atmosphere of expectation that the collectivization policy, economic construction, the Comintern leadership, and foreign policy would collapse.

2\. It is entirely natural that the desire to form a bloc with anyone whatsoever against the Central Committee and the Party followed from the counterrevolutionary position we occupied.

Hence the attempt to form a bloc with the Rightists (1929–1932). From what NATANSON said, I think that apparently there were meetings with Trotskyists (SMIRNOV I. N.) at the end of 1932.

BAKAEV and KOSTINA visited the apartment of the Trotskyist UFIMTSEV. At UFIMTSEV's (at S. SIMASHKO's \[1\] — UFIMTSEV's wife), NATANSON spent the night shortly before UFIMTSEV's arrest (at the beginning of 1933).

News of the arrests and exile of Trotskyists (SMIRNOV, UFIMTSEV, and others) was received in exactly the same way as the arrests of members of our organization, that is, with counterrevolutionary bitterness against the Party leadership and the GPU.

Written by me in my own hand — ANISHEV.

Interrogated by: Head of Section 1 of the SPO of the UGB — LULOV.
