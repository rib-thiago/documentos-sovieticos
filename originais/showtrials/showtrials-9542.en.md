Comrade Rutkovsky,

I am sending you my letter to comrade Stalin, asking you to hand it to comrade Agranov for forwarding to its destination. I am extremely anxious that this letter should reach comrade Stalin's hands as quickly as possible, that is, tomorrow itself.

December 18, 1934

L. Kamenev

Comrade Stalin,

I write to you at the most tragic moment of my life and implore you to read this letter, which will probably be my last.

I do not cherish the life that I myself have so thoroughly ruined over the last ten years, but I wish to declare to you and the entire Party leadership that I am innocent of the shameful and criminal activities for which the Leningrad group has been charged. These are people with whom, from the moment of my return to the Party, I broke not only political but all personal connections, because I understood and became convinced that they were chatterers and political masturbators. I was in their ranks (and this is the greatest mistake of my life) when it seemed that some idea and some goal united us. When life shattered this idea, these people turned into philistines on the Party's fringes and against the Party, living on political gossip and mutually titillating their nerves through supposedly political little conversations. This became clear to me even before my journey to Minusinsk, and I began withdrawing from them not only politically but even more personally. Having returned and received the Party's trust, I broke off all personal relations with them. From the moment of my return from Minusinsk I have not once seen Bakaev or his wife, Kuklin, Zalutsky, Naumov or Gertik, nor spoken a single word to them. During all this time I saw Evdokimov once, when he visited Zinoviev at the dacha in summer, and had no conversations with him. None of them ever visited me. I did this quite consciously, since I shared no common interests with them. What they did, what interested them, what they said did not interest me. I was entirely satisfied with the work the Party had given me (the publishing house, the Institute of Literature). I was happy that you and the leadership trusted me with this work, and devoted all my days and thoughts entirely to it. I frankly confess that I dreamed of becoming an academician and thus, after my political collapse, finding new work interesting to me that would fill the end of my life. In this work I acquired a new circle of acquaintances, Party and non-Party writers, and all old connections with the Leningrad people ceased. One would have to be mad, an adventurer or a hysteric to prefer masturbatory chatter with Gertik or Kuklin to the path that the Party, and you above all, comrade Stalin, were opening to me. And at the very moment when I felt that I really was returning home, to the Party, and that within it there was a corner for me of fruitful work that wholly absorbed me, these people are sinking me, entangling me in some affairs and conversations of theirs. I swear that I knew nothing about them, took no interest in any of them and had long considered them strangers to me, my work and my interests. I saw living work before me, saw the Party's trust and yours in me growing, and was sincerely happy and wanted nothing else.

The only person with whom I had not broken personal connections over the last three years was Zinoviev. I felt all along that, by continuing to meet him, I was making a mistake. Now I am convinced that it was a tragic mistake. Having broken all connections with the Leningrad people, I should also have broken off meetings with Zinoviev. But after returning from Minusinsk, what bound me to him was not politics (of which, in my opinion, he has none at all), but purely everyday circumstances. We had long shared a dacha, and there we inevitably met. In town I met him less and less. Throughout the autumn I visited him once. To end this situation, as early as the summer I began building a separate dacha for myself along another road. (This fact can be checked at the Writers' Union.) This would also have broken the thin thread that still held our relations together, since these relations had long been a burden to me and, given the differences in our characters, ways of life and attitudes toward people, afforded neither interest nor pleasure. But now this thin thread is strangling me! For were it not for these accursed meetings with Zinoviev, the Leningrad people, with whom I have had no relations for three years, could not even formally refer to me and mention my name in connection with their affairs, petty dealings and conversations.

Comrade Stalin, save me from the shame of being drawn into the same case as people with whom I have nothing in common, from whom I consciously distanced myself, who repeatedly brought me to the edge of the abyss and have now brought me to final, shameful ruin. I assure you: from the moment of my exile to Minusinsk, I have not wronged the Party and its leadership by a single deed, word or thought! I truly, fully and sincerely sought to atone for my past sins. From my mind and heart every remnant of opposition, factional irritation and vain claims to any role in the Party other than that of a faithful worker in the field assigned to me had vanished completely. Look through the Leningrad people's testimony: I am certain that they too could produce not a single fact, not a single word of mine after the Minusinsk exile, that would testify against my Party sentiments — because such facts and words did not and could not exist, and they could not have heard them because I wished neither to see them nor to speak with them. On the contrary, in articles in “Pravda” and “Izvestiya”, in oral speeches \[1\], I everywhere defended what is my profound conviction: the correctness of Party policy, the remarkable character of its leadership, the conviction that this leadership deserves deep love and gratitude because with a firm hand it is leading the country out of difficulties onto the path of prosperity.

And at the very moment when there is nothing in my soul but complete love for the Party and its leadership, when, having lived through wavering and doubts, I can boldly say that from the entire past I have gained the greatest trust in every step of the Central Committee and every decision of yours, comrade Stalin, when before me — for the first time after several years of estrangement from the Party — a path has opened to fruitful work in the Party's ranks, I am arrested in the case of people who have long become strangers to me and whom I find repugnant. I had just emerged from a filthy swamp onto a clean road, and these people, with their slander and irresponsible chatter, are dragging me back into their swamp. I write honestly, everything to the end. Help me, comrade Stalin, exclude me from this shameful, repugnant case that is alien to me. The thought that I may be brought to trial in such a case alongside these people torments me with the greatest anguish. Comrade Stalin, I have already experienced your profound justice and impartiality. In these alone I place my hope now. Sincerely devoted to the Party and to you.

L. Kamenev.

December 18, 1934.
