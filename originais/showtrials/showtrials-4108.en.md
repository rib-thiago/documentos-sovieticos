INTERROGATION RECORD

Of GORBACHEV, Georgy Yefimovich — dated January 5, 1935.

In response to the questions put to me by the investigation about the practical activities of the Zinovievist organization, I additionally testify:

The task of the Zinovievist organization was counterrevolutionary struggle against the party and its leadership, that is, thereby against Soviet power.

Defeated on the front of direct political struggle, with no possibility of open action there, the Zinovievist organization intensified its activity in fighting the party in various sectors of the theoretical front.

For those of us (Zinovievists) who worked specifically on the ideological front, a prospect opened up which we decided to exploit: to avenge our general defeat, that is, win authority and a leading position here for the same struggle against the party.

I know the methods and techniques of struggle on the front of philosophy, history, and especially literature.

In transferring counterrevolutionary struggle against the party into literature, we were guided by directives received by myself and others from ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV as early as 1928–29.

ZINOVIEV, previously little interested in literature, spoke with me about it in detail during these years, both at Tsentrosoyuz and at his apartment in Kaloshin Lane. Alongside general directives (return to the party at any price, maintain old contacts to preserve ourselves in the party as an independent force), he approved of my and LELEVICH’s struggle against the RAPP leadership in literature and advised continuing it. I took this as a directive instruction.

In addition, ZINOVIEV made attempts through me to rally writers around himself. The form of this rallying was ZINOVIEV’s proposal to publish at Tsentrosoyuz books by writers connected with me from the “Napostovist Left” (M. KARPOV, Al. TVERYAK, Iv. VASILYEV, Iv. NIKITIN), on subjects of rural cooperation, through the Tsentrosoyuz publishing house.

KAMENEV, in meetings with me in 1928–29, took an interest in the state of literature and approved my turn toward methodological interventions in this field, particularly interventions against RAPP’s theory.

I, LELEVICH, and VARDIN always regarded the struggle of the “Napostovist Left” group we organized against RAPP as a struggle in literature under the old Zinovievist banners against the same vices in literary policy from which, in our opinion, all party policy supposedly suffered (that is, blunting class struggle, administration instead of leadership, indulgence of petty-bourgeois elements at the expense of proletarian interests).

In reality, however, the entire policy of the “Napostovist Left” was based on underestimation of the peasantry, disbelief in the forces of proletarian revolution, and hostility to the party line.

In other words, the political meaning of my and LELEVICH’s struggle in literature, approved by ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, amounted to an anti-party counterposing of the Zinovievist platform and the Zinovievist group of theorists claiming to direct the party line.

Thus the Zinovievists’ counterrevolutionary activity was conducted in literature.

Members of the Zinovievist organization — the economist YAKOVLEV and the historians TATAROV and TSVIBAK — gave LELEVICH and me a number of suggestions on how best to organize this struggle. TATAROV himself spoke more than once (later, in 1930) on the literary front, demonstrating the effort to use every ideological sector to promote their own people. Both TATAROV and I always tried to coordinate our interventions.

“Litfront,” organized in 1930 with my closest participation, was a combination of the “Napostovist Left” (including non-Zinovievists who supported LELEVICH and me in literature, for example BEZYMENSKY and RODOV) with a group close to the Right-Leftist bloc of “theorists” (PEREVERZEV’s pupils, including the Syrtsovite ZONIN). “Litfront” was a reflection in literature of Right-Leftist attitudes; YAKOVLEV and TATAROV gave me advice on how LELEVICH and I could seize influence in “Litfront”; the latter, having returned from exile to ZOT, without officially joining “Litfront,” participated in directing its activity.

In 1930–31 I used the Institute of Modern Russian Literature of the Academy of Sciences to turn it into a base and refuge for Litfront members, with YAKOVLEV’s support in the bureau of the Academy collective.

In literature we continued our struggle against the party leadership even after RAPP’s dissolution. At first LELEVICH and I advanced the slogan of “party leadership in literature.” But when the party leadership, having liquidated RAPP, took all literary policy into its own hands, LELEVICH and I began with renewed vigor to discredit this leadership, describing its literary line as bureaucratic, official, and weakening the political sharpness of literary creation. I conveyed these propositions, formulated by LELEVICH and myself, in conversations with TSVIBAK, TATAROV, and YAKOVLEV, thereby also contributing here to discrediting and disrupting the party line.

For us Zinovievists, the slogan of party leadership in literature meant struggle for our own, Zinovievist leadership, that is, it had an anti-party, counterrevolutionary meaning.

When I heard (from the non-party professor OKSMAN) that KAMENEV’s election as chairman of the Writers’ Union was supposedly contemplated, I spoke of this, expressing joy, to YAKOVLEV and TATAROV, who agreed with me. What pleased us here, of course, was the strengthening of the Zinovievist group on the ideological front from the standpoint of the prospect of strengthening the positions of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization.

In a conversation with SAFAROV and YAKOVLEV at the latter’s apartment in the autumn of 1934, I described the writers’ congress as predominantly a “parade,” with speeches about universal literary achievements in the absence of serious debates on creative questions and criticism of the shortcomings of contemporary literature. Both my interlocutors expressed solidarity with this typically Zinovievist counterrevolutionary assessment of the congress as official and loudly proclaiming exaggerated achievements. This coincided with our assessment (myself, YAKOVLEV, TATAROV) of other manifestations of the country’s ideological life as analogies of the party leadership bearing an official character (for example, the new philosophical leadership appointed by the party was assessed in the same way).

Members of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization (myself included) also used work on the philosophical front to struggle against the party, to cultivate particularly hostile attitudes toward the party leadership and Comrade Stalin. The following fact is indicative: after the defeat of Menshevizing idealism, the Zinovievist KAREV began working at the Academy of Sciences. M. YAKOVLEV and I organized a seminar for studying HEGEL under KAREV’s direction. Participants in this seminar included, among others, KAMEGULOV (a Litfront member), who had been a Zinovievist before the bloc with TROTSKY, and the Litfront member RODOV. This seminar was accompanied by KAREV’s constant mockery, supported by YAKOVLEV and me, of the new philosophical leadership, installed by the party and carrying out its directive on the political activation of philosophy. The political meaning of this mockery is clear from the fact that among ourselves — myself, KAREV, and YAKOVLEV — we hounded this new leadership. We said that it was capable only of apologetics for the party leadership. We spoke in hostile terms of Comrade STALIN and his role in theoretical questions.

Thus the seminar under our direction became a place for shaping anti-Soviet views and was turned into an instrument of struggle against the party. It goes without saying that our attitudes and views seeped through to some extent and influenced other participants in the seminar.

I know less about the use of history for struggle against the party. I am poorly acquainted with the substance of TSVIBAK’s and TATAROV’s interventions, but it is highly indicative that they (like the Zinovievist opposition in politics before them) considered themselves “Leftists” in their fields, “signaled” bourgeois influences, considered themselves fighters against Right deviations; that is, here too, acting in solidarity, they proceeded from the same assessment of their role that was characteristic of Zinovievists generally in politics and of us Zinovievist literary figures in literature.

In ideology we Zinovievists, to camouflage our struggle against the party, considered our people in every sector of the theoretical front representatives of genuinely revolutionary and progressive thought, vanguard fighters for a supposedly correct theoretical line against the party line on the question, supposedly official and stereotyped from our viewpoint.

The facts I have cited above demonstrate the solidarity and cohesion of Zinovievists in various sectors of the theoretical front in their hostile attitude toward the party leadership and their struggle against it.

The Zinovievists helped one another occupy leading and authoritative positions in various ideological fields, acting as an integral part of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization, with mutual protection and responsibility for one another; in practice the Zinovievists adopted anti-party, counterrevolutionary positions on various specific ideological questions (KAREV in philosophy, LELEVICH and myself in literature).

In recent years the central point in the struggle against the party by Zinovievists working on the ideological front became hostile criticism of the line of the Culture and Propaganda Department of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), both in its general party orientation and in individual aspects of its work (literary philosophy). The starting point in our struggle against the party in this field became the slanderous assertions that the party stifled initiative and creative thought, that it had smashed valuable cultural forces at sharp and unexpected turns, and that all this had supposedly led to the degradation of theoretical thought in the country. These propositions were repeatedly expressed by myself, LELEVICH, YAKOVLEV, TATAROV, TSVIBAK, and VORONSKY (1932–34).

This also includes numerous counterrevolutionary conversations in various combinations of interlocutors and on various occasions (myself, YAKOVLEV, KAREV, TATAROV, TSVIBAK, LELEVICH, VORONSKY), especially during the discussion of Comrade STALIN’s letter and, in particular, in connection with my expulsion from the party for Trotskyist contraband. From the milieu of the persons listed came counterrevolutionary rumors and fabrications to the effect that the party leadership employed the method of “beating people over the head”; that this led to the disorganization of theoretical thought and theorists’ desire to remain silent; that its unexpectedness, sharpness, and political and organizational conclusions corrupted young people, fostering the emergence of opportunists who did not care whom they struck or whom they defended and praised, not only on particular but also on the most general political questions.

Such an assessment of the party’s ideological line was connected with our general assessment of the party line as incorrect. After the party’s economic successes it was difficult to counterpose anything coherent in agriculture and industry. We therefore concentrated our hostile criticism on other essential aspects of party policy. Thus in 1932 LELEVICH asserted that party policy rested on an incorrect theory, since the leadership’s errors in 1925–27 were supposedly being covered up. He formulated these errors as follows: the party leadership’s bloc with the Right, concessions to it, the smashing of Lenin’s Politburo and the party’s old Bolshevik cadres (that is, opposition cadres); therefore, he claimed, the party line was empirical, the party poorly foresaw events and the consequences of its measures.

YAKOVLEV said that the party leadership did not know its local cadres, who implemented its directives blindly and without thinking for themselves, disregarding local conditions; hence, he claimed, the excesses in collectivization. It was precisely this idea — that even correct directives were implemented locally and in individual links of the party and Soviet apparatus automatically, without discussion — that I expressed to SAFAROV in conversation with him at his apartment in 1934.

Underlying this idea was the old Zinovievist idea of the bureaucratization of the party apparatus and the deadening of party life. The attitude toward Comintern policy was also sharply negative. VUJOVIC said in the presence of LELEVICH and myself that the Comintern was a “provincial institution,” in the sense of the party’s scant attention to it and the weakness of its present leadership. SAFAROV had the same attitude toward the Comintern.

DOMBSKY spoke mockingly and hostilely of the leadership of the Communist Party of Poland in the presence of LELEVICH and myself.

Underlying these conversations was the old Zinovievist slanderous accusation that the party pursued a policy of “national narrowness.” The conclusion drawn from such an attitude toward the Comintern amounted to emphasizing the need to bring ZINOVIEV into the Comintern leadership (myself, LELEVICH, YAKOVLEV).

All these facts demonstrate that the Zinovievist organization, of which I remained a member until the most recent period, basically stood on the positions of its old platform, continuing from these positions the struggle against the party and Soviet power.

The following facts demonstrate the practice of establishing unprincipled blocs by members of the Zinovievist organization according to the principle “with anyone, so long as against the party leadership”: my and LELEVICH’s attempt to draw closer to the Trotskyist VORONSKY, whom we ourselves considered a Rightist and liquidationist on questions of culture and literature.

The rapprochement was envisaged on literary questions on the basis of VORONSKY’s hostility toward RAPP (1932). There was no rapprochement in literary practice, since VORONSKY did not want to write on questions of contemporary literature at all, but there were “friendly” conversations about stereotyped and official character in literary and ideological life generally.

Both LELEVICH and I sympathized with the Right opportunist in literature POLONSKY as a “cultured” and “independent” journalist hounded by RAPP, and expressed this to him. After a resolute polemic with PEREVERZEV’s pupils, I concluded a bloc with them in “Litfront” against the RAPP leadership.

Underlying all this was the typically Social Democratic idea of the party’s ideological life as an arena of struggle between different currents, a kind of “debating club,” rather than scientific, educational, and artistic work directed by the party and based on a single, coherent theory; and in practice all this meant the desire to obtain a free arena for propagating Zinovievist ideas, an arena for struggle against the party’s general line.

In connection with the defeat of the Zinovievists on a number of theoretical fronts, a decision arose to adopt the tactic of lying low here too in order to preserve their cadres. LELEVICH retreats into literary history, advising me to do the same. YAKOVLEV tells me that one must write about the classics of the past rather than the untalented writers of the present. YAKOVLEV himself withdraws from the party leadership of the Academy of Sciences, seeking “quiet,” narrowly practical work. At the same time everyone pretends to be people who are not allowed to work actively: KANATCHIKOV says that all he has left is to write memoirs and novels; YAKOVLEV says he is a Soviet priest’s assistant.

Nor is KAMENEV’s orientation toward problems of classical literature accidental here: his publication in “Zvenya” of only very old historical materials, his policy of working at Pushkin House (the Institute of Russian Literature of the Academy of Sciences) only on literature’s past.

Written by me in my own hand — GORBACHEV.
