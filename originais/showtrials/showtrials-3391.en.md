TO THE SECRETARY OF THE CENTRAL COMMITTEE OF THE ALL-UNION COMMUNIST PARTY (BOLSHEVIKS),

Comrade STALIN.

I forward a letter addressed to you from the arrested KHARITONOV.

A similar letter has also been written to Comrade Ordzhonikidze and sent.

PEOPLE'S COMMISSAR

OF INTERNAL AFFAIRS OF THE UNION OF SOVIET SOCIALIST REPUBLICS: (G. YAGODA)

December 25, 1934.

No. 31188

RGASPI, fond 17, inventory 171, file 200, folio 146.

Dear Comrade Stalin!

I hope you will read this letter, since it is written by the hand of a person boundlessly devoted to the Party and to you personally as the Party's leader, who leads it victoriously along the correct Leninist path.

On the seventeenth of this month I received a telegram in London from Comrade Rozengolts instructing me to leave for the Union. I left on the eighteenth, was arrested at the border on the twentieth, and today, the twenty-second, am being sent to Leningrad. I do not know what charges they will bring against me there, but I am absolutely convinced that within the next few days everyone will become convinced that my arrest is the result of the gravest misunderstanding.

I have committed no crimes or offenses against the Party except my former membership in the opposition. Inwardly I broke with the opposition already on the eve of the Fifteenth Congress, but lacked the courage to state this honestly then. Formally I broke with the opposition immediately after the Fifteenth Congress, and broke finally and irrevocably. I overcame the remnants of my former opposition sentiments very quickly, and from the moment I began working in the Central Control Commission and the People's Commissariat of the Workers' and Peasants' Inspectorate under Sergo's leadership until the present I have been a loyal Party member, firmly convinced that the opposition's path was a disastrous anti-Party path. To you, Comrade Stalin, more than anyone else, the Party owes the fact that this anti-Party path was exposed comparatively quickly and the opposition defeated. And since I became convinced of this I have been loyal to you. I am entirely calm about my fate, convinced that I shall easily prove what is written in this letter, but it is painful and offensive to realize that I could now be suspected of anything directed against the Party, all the more of any involvement or guilt, even the remotest, in the villainous murder of Comrade Kirov, beloved by the entire Party. Only the basest and most vicious class enemy could shoot or incite another to shoot Sergei Mironovich. If anyone from the former Zinovievist or Trotskyist opposition is involved in this most abominable crime, he must and will bear his punishment. I have no business [1], no connections related to former membership in the opposition, nor can I have any now. Zinoviev himself long ago became for me nothing other than a bankrupt leader of the former anti-Party opposition. I also lost interest in personal meetings with him, and we have not met in recent years. Sometimes one merely feels human pity for him, and nothing more. I went to Leningrad this summer to meet my family, who arrived by ship from London. In previous years I went to visit my son (a boy of fourteen), who lived at a boarding school in Detskoye Selo, and a year earlier still, on instructions from the People's Commissariat of Foreign Trade, to the port on business concerning timber shipments. Apart from these private matters and the direct assignment from the People's Commissariat of Foreign Trade I had no business, as I hope the investigation will easily and quickly establish.

Dear Comrade Stalin, whatever happens to me next—mistakes are always possible, after all—I want only for you to believe me that throughout approximately the past six years I have been and remain a loyal and devoted member of the Party and a person loyal and devoted to you personally.

With communist greetings,

M. Kharitonov

December 22, 1934.
