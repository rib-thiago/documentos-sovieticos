CONFRONTATION RECORD

Between EVDOKIMOV Grigory Eremeevich and ZINOVIEV Grigory Evseevich.

Dated January 12, 1935.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: You testified that until recently there existed an organization of Zinovievists. Do you confirm this?

Answer: I confirm that until recently there existed an organization of Zinovievists.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: You testified to the investigation that the anti-party organization of Zinovievists held counterrevolutionary positions. Do you confirm this?

Answer: I do. Insofar as we remained on the former platform of 1925–1927, which the party congress had recognized as counterrevolutionary; insofar as on fundamental questions—collectivization, industrialization, foreign policy, the inner-party regime—we had viewpoints opposed to the Central Committee's general line; insofar as on all these questions we criticized the party line, as stated in my testimony, to that extent, quite apart from the very fact of the existence of the counterrevolutionary organization, the organization's position was counterrevolutionary.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: You testified that until recently there existed a Moscow center of the organization, of which you too were a member. Do you confirm this?

Answer: Yes, I do.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Name the participants in the Moscow center.

Answer: The Moscow center included ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, SHAROV, GERTIK, GESSEN Sergei, GORSHENIN, KHARITONOV (until he withdrew), ZALUTSKY (also until he withdrew), FEDOROV.

KHARITONOV began withdrawing from participation in the center shortly before his departure abroad. This relates to 1931–1932. It was not an open break on KHARITONOV's part, with a statement to any of us that he was breaking with us. Later, from conversations about his attitude toward Zinoviev's expulsion from the party for connections with RYUTIN and company, I gained the impression that from that moment he was dissociating himself from us. Admittedly, this was said in connection with his speech at a cell meeting while working abroad, where he condemned ZINOVIEV's behavior in extremely harsh terms.

After that I did not meet him. But, having such reports about his attitudes, I did not try to raise general political questions with him, and he for his part did not raise these questions either.

As regards FEDOROV I must say that he too had somehow been little connected with us recently; moreover, he also had not met ZINOVIEV recently. During meetings with me he did not speak on political subjects. Although during our last meeting in Sochi the atmosphere was conducive to such conversations. I am speaking of 1934; there were no longer any such attempts on his part.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Name the participants known to you in the counterrevolutionary group of Zinovievists in Leningrad which existed until recently.

Answer: Among the participants in the counterrevolutionary group of Zinovievists in Leningrad I know RUMYANTSEV, LEVIN, KOTOLYNOV, REM, MIRONOV, SHIRYAEV Dm., MANDELSHTAM, MYASNIKOV, KOSTRITSKY, NALIVAIKO, TOLMAZOV.

I must say regarding KOSTRITSKY and NALIVAIKO that during my latest meetings with them in 1933–1934 we had no conversations on political subjects. From their behavior it seemed to me that even if they had not overcome their counterrevolutionary convictions, they at least wished to legalize themselves in the sense of not compromising themselves through political conversations with me, since they knew that I remained in the former positions hostile to the party.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: In your testimony of January 12, 1935, you informed the investigation that ZINOVIEV repeatedly told you that there was an established connection between the Moscow center and the Leningrad counterrevolutionary organization. Do you confirm this?

Answer: We had connections with the Leningrad group of Zinovievists all along. They took such a form that either Leningrad Zinovievists visiting Moscow called on one of the Moscow center members, or Moscow center members visiting Leningrad met someone from the Leningrad Zinovievist group.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Who specifically from the Moscow center visited Leningrad, and who from the Leningrad group visited Moscow?

Answer: I shall begin with the following somewhat distant examples. In 1928, during LASHEVICH's funeral, whose organization was designed to make possible a demonstration of sympathy by our like-minded associates in Leningrad, after the funeral a group of Leningrad Zinovievists gathered at my apartment in Leningrad, where ZINOVIEV, BAKAEV and I were present; I do not remember whether anyone else from Moscow was there. Present were RUMYANTSEV, NALIVAIKO, KOSTRITSKY. Who else was there I do not remember, but of course there were more than three; I have a poor memory for names. BAKAEV's memory is better. Perhaps Grigory Evseevich will remember who else was present.

These meetings of Moscow center members with Leningrad Zinovievists developed into conversations about the need to preserve our cadres in Leningrad and connections with Moscow Zinovievists.

In 1929, when I went to Leningrad for treatment, Grigory Evseevich proposed that I try to establish ties with at least several dozen worker party members among the Zinovievists who remained in production in Leningrad. I refused. Grigory Evseevich must confirm this. I stated that this venture could only lead to the fact that, before I could establish contact not only with several dozen workers but even with a few, it would become known to those whose business it was to know, and we would be swept not only out of Leningrad and Moscow but out of the party too.

I also pointed out to ZINOVIEV that this did not fit with our orientation toward integration into the party, which was one of our tasks.

We understood the task of integration into the party in a peculiar way, not in the sense of breaking with our past but in the sense of deceiving the party, declaring one thing and doing another.

In early 1929, while receiving treatment in Leningrad and consequently living there from January to May, S. GESSEN came to my apartment with KOTOLYNOV, RUMYANTSEV and someone else whom I do not remember precisely, but there was someone else—I do not remember. They raised the question of forming an underground center of Zinovievists in Leningrad; the impression was that they had discussed this subject before coming to me. I took an extremely negative attitude toward it then, and subsequently during my stay in Leningrad the Leningrad Zinovievists no longer raised this question with me.

I consider it necessary to say that, in refusing ZINOVIEV's assignment and answering GESSEN and the oth\<ers\> negatively on the question of organizing a center in Leningrad, I showed no more loyalty toward the party than other Moscow center members, but only greater caution; that is, I mean that in principle I would not have opposed these proposals if putting them into effect had not carried the danger of our being broken up.

Now, turning to individual journeys to Leningrad, I must begin with myself.

Until 1931 my son-in-law TARASOV Pyotr lived in Leningrad; at that time and later he remained on the former counterrevolutionary positions of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc.

I do not know his attitudes now, since I have not seen him for over a year, but I know that during the party purge he was expelled as not having overcome his former Zinovievist-Trotskyist attitudes. He sent me a letter about this; unfortunately I myself am not in the habit of keeping letters, and I have not preserved it. In this letter he regarded his expulsion as wrong, ending the letter with assurances that he would make every effort to return to the party's ranks. It is difficult for me to judge how sincere this was, because sending a letter of precisely that content may have been simple caution on his part.

In 1930 or 1931 I met RUMYANTSEV at his place (TARASOV lived in Leningrad). The conversation during the meeting was anti-party in character. What precisely we discussed I cannot now recall; I remember only that RUMYANTSEV said that “my attitudes and those of my lads remain firmly the same.”

I know that Pyotr TARASOV, while living in Leningrad, met quite frequently with some of the Leningrad Zinovievists I listed above, and of course during those meetings he spoke to them about my attitudes and possibly also the assessments I made. Thus my connections with the Leningrad Zinovievists were of course not exhausted by my personal meetings with individual Zinovievists in Leningrad.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Explain precisely what you mean by personal meetings.

Answer: By personal meetings I mean those in which I myself met Zinovievists. Among the Leningrad Zinovievists I met in Moscow KOSTRITSKY, NALIVAIKO, MANDELSHTAM, MIRONOV, REM, TSEITLIN Yakov and LEVIN Vl. It is now difficult to recall specifically whether during all these meetings with each of them there were conversations on general political subjects in an anti-party spirit, but regardless of whether these conversations took place or not, these meetings were meetings of people with a common anti-party past who had not renounced that past or the counterrevolutionary positions they held, and who assessed the whole domestic and foreign political situation from their former counterrevolutionary positions.

The only exceptions are my meetings in 1933–1934 with NALIVAIKO and KOSTRITSKY and my last meeting with REM in July or August 1934. I have already spoken about the first two above. About REM I must say only that at this last meeting we did not speak on general political subjects.

I also know that GESSEN, during his journeys even after 1929, met participants in the Leningrad underground organization in Leningrad. The same applies to GERTIK. In my opinion GERTIK made a special trip to Leningrad, for example, in connection with ZINOVIEV's expulsion from the party over the Ryutin affair, to inform the Leningrad people of the Moscow center's decision on how to behave at party meetings during discussion of this question. GERTIK also traveled to Leningrad at other times. I find it difficult now to indicate precisely the dates and purposes of such journeys.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Were these journeys by GERTIK organizational in character?

Answer: During these journeys GERTIK met Leningrad Zinovievists, and these meetings were undoubtedly anti-party in character.

BAKAEV also visited Leningrad and met participants in the underground Zinovievist organization. SHAROV and KUKLIN likewise visited Leningrad. They too met persons belonging to the Leningrad underground organization of Zinovievists. I find it difficult now to speak specifically about these journeys.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Do you know anything about LEVIN's visits to Moscow?

Answer: During one of his journeys to Moscow in 1932 LEVIN visited not only me but also Grigory Evseevich ZINOVIEV in Ilyinskoye. ZINOVIEV later told me that LEVIN had informed him of the deterioration of workers' supplies in Leningrad and the resulting growth of discontent, as well as growing discontent among students owing to their supposedly difficult material situation.

In conclusion I wish to say that all these connections were such that they gave us the opportunity at any moment we wished to inform our supporters who belonged to the Leningrad underground organization of whatever we considered necessary.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: In your testimony you reported that members of the Moscow center, especially ZINOVIEV, at gatherings and meetings with individual center members systematically discussed questions of party policy in a hostile spirit until recently, making counterrevolutionary, bitter and slanderous attacks against the party leadership and Soviet government. Do you confirm this?

Answer: Yes, I do. I have already said above that we remained on our former counterrevolutionary positions of 1925–1927. Our criticism was therefore counterrevolutionary. Moreover, because of our political convictions and the party congress decisions declaring those convictions incompatible with party membership, we were unable to express openly our attitude toward the domestic and international political situation, and this circumstance meant that the criticism was naturally very sharp in character.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Until recently?

Answer: I shall say later. And indeed counterrevolutionary, bitter and slanderous attacks were made against the party leadership and, specifically, against Comrade STALIN.

For example, on collectivization and industrialization we said that collectivization was being conducted in forms and at a pace causing enormous unnecessary overhead costs (overhead costs in both the economic and political senses); that industrialization was being conducted without sufficient account of the country's resources, without sufficient concentration of attention and means on genuinely decisive links and projects, which led, first, to excessive strain in the plans and, second, to a lack of coordination among individual elements of the national economy. Transport difficulties were cited as an example.

Hence ZINOVIEV's assertion, which he expressed repeatedly, that “STALIN will cost the country dearly.” That was his formula, but the assessment was not his alone: it was shared by other center members.

We assessed the inner-party regime as one of unprecedented apparatus violence.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Do you confirm that hostile, anti-party assessments of all measures of the party and Soviet government occurred until recently?

Answer: Recently, as I have already testified, our conversations on these subjects grew increasingly rare, and our criticism concentrated mainly on Comintern questions. We accused the Central Committee (and the Comintern leadership) of the fact that, incorrectly understanding the idea of the victory of socialism in one country, the Central Committee failed at moments of the greatest responsibility for the international working-class movement to take proper measures to activate that movement in accordance with the opportunities presenting themselves. Thus in essence, on this fundamental question of Bolshevism (the victory of socialism in one country), we advanced our former accusations against the Central Committee and Comintern leadership of national narrowness. I explain the recent restriction of anti-party criticism of the party's general line mainly to Comintern questions by the fact that the enormous economic achievements within the country, enormous cultural growth and rising welfare of the working masses offered no possibility for criticism as a whole and left very little scope for anti-party criticism on individual questions, especially after the Seventeenth Party Congress.

I must say, in particular regarding ZINOVIEV, that recently hardly anyone visited him besides myself and GORSHENIN. Recently I had no conversations with ZINOVIEV on economic subjects.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: What do you mean by recently?

Answer: Taking 1934; but this does not mean that my attitudes or those of ZINOVIEV or the other center members had changed. In this sense my last conversation with ZINOVIEV in November 1934 is indicative. During this conversation he said that work on establishing a united front was being conducted incorrectly in principle, in the sense that, as for example in France, the Comintern was proceeding toward a united front with the socialist party without presenting any conditions to the Second International, that is, effectively handing the initiative in creating a united front to the Second International.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm all this testimony by EVDOKIMOV?

Answer: As long as EVDOKIMOV speaks of 1928–1929, almost everything is correct. I too recall our anti-party work in these same features. LASHEVICH's funeral, the conversations around that funeral about attempts then to reach agreement with the “leaderless”—all this coincides.

Further on, a mass of things is entirely unclear to me. On the matter of the Moscow center: in 1928 it really existed, and we worked in it. The formal dissolution of this center occurred, in my opinion, in 1929 on the proposal, I believe, of KAMENEV and EVDOKIMOV himself. Since then there were no meetings of the center's membership, with the qualification that in 1932 there was a revival among people from the center, although even in 1932 I do not remember any meeting of a formally constituted center. Thus to speak of a center, say, after 1932, particularly in 1933–1934, seemed entirely wrong to me. No one can name a single meeting or a single question discussed in any organized fashion. There was none; there was none and could not have been, because after all we ceased believing in much of our platform. It must also be said that the five-year plan did not pass us by in vain and was curing us of many anti-party positions.

I had not seen SHAROV (that is, had not talked with him) for several years, had not seen GERTIK since 1932, and likewise had not seen BAKAEV since 1932, apart from a fleeting meeting in others' presence on a street in Kislovodsk in 1932. I had not seen KUKLIN since 1932 and met him only once at the end of 1934, when the conversation was no longer of the former character. Thus there is not and was not a center in the sense we had one in 1927–1928–1929.

Generally speaking, anti-party factions or remnants of various anti-party groups in Moscow and Leningrad looked upon these few people—myself, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV and oth\<ers\>—as a “center.” I cannot say that I regarded it so in recent years, but they regarded us as a center.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Answer the question directly. The investigation establishes that the center existed until recently too; therefore the issue is not how someone viewed you, but how you yourself regarded the group of persons named by EVDOKIMOV as the membership of the Moscow center.

Answer: Of course one can say: you did not assemble in full membership, and questions were not discussed by nine persons out of caution. Of course they could have gathered in different places in twos and threes, and so on. But even this did not happen. At least I do not know of it. That is, I knew these people met and retained anti-party attitudes, but I did not regard this as a center in the strict sense.

Question to ZINOVIEV: You do not wish to acknowledge obvious facts?

Answer: There was no organized existence.

Question to ZINOVIEV: All the materials speak of this organized existence. Do you confirm the presence of the center?

Answer: I said that these people undoubtedly regarded one another, and were regarded by the outside world, as remnants of the “Leningrad opposition,” but they did not assemble together, and in recent years discussed not a single question in an organized manner. I have already described 1932 to the investigation in detail. The year 1932 was the last year of difficulties, as has now become clear to us, and a year of the greatest difficulties. At that time very great vacillations were observable in many opposition groupings in Moscow and, in particular, among us. And again I shall not say that I remember any special meeting of the center as a center in 1932.

Question to ZINOVIEV: These are excuses. Answer the substance of the question put to you about the center.

Answer: I am setting out the facts as they were imprinted in my memory. In 1932 there was of course a great “revival” among us, but even then there was not a single full meeting. Several people gathered, wished to work out a common opinion; there was the thought that perhaps it was necessary to act, perhaps to say something. One way or another, the question really did arise that we must somehow say something. This was in 1932.

In 1933–1934 there was nothing of the kind, nor were there any meetings. I met EVDOKIMOV very rarely, and even then we talked much about trivialities, but of course we talked about politics too.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you acknowledge that after your exposure in the Ryutin affair and your return from exile, the center's work did not cease?

Answer: Recently I had conversations with EVDOKIMOV about foreign affairs, the international press, and so on. In 1933–1934 I considered that there was no center and, I repeat, met almost no one. With KAMENEV we met and talked more or less daily. We did not meet the others.

Again we are approaching the finale in every sense, and it must be said clearly that the entire group listed here long regarded itself as the moral representation of the Leningrad opposition. That is so. And I acknowledge it.

It seems to you that I am evading when I speak of a genuine center only up to 1928–1929, but it was really as I say.

Vacillations and hostility toward the party on one question or another naturally remained among us in general much later too. Conversations that we “had not been appreciated” and that “we were right in many things” also remained, but all this was not what had existed before.

We willingly listened to each of the anti-party groups, yet we took no concrete step even in 1932. Of course this was explained in some sense by precaution. But partly by internal doubts as well. Many “foundations” of our platform wavered. And we were not made of iron in our anti-party positions either. We began internally to renounce many anti-moral positions, and in view of the clear and great successes looked at the party leadership with different eyes. This is of course no “merit,” but it is a fact.

Question to ZINOVIEV: That you took no concrete step in 1932 is explained less by precaution than by the exposure of Ryutin's group and the exile of yourself and KAMENEV.

Answer: No; the complete truth would be to say that there was also the influence of the five-year plan and of the whole course of development.

Question to ZINOVIEV: And nevertheless you continued to remain on the former counterrevolutionary positions.

Answer: Yes. For example, in 1929–1930 a collection of documents on the party's struggle against the Leningrad opposition appeared, compiled by YAROSLAVSKY. We all seized upon these materials; from the documents gathered in it we assured ourselves that on many questions we “had proved right,” that this was our “historical merit.” Of course I now acknowledge that these were relapses into counterrevolutionary attitudes.

Had you asked me before my arrest about the Moscow center, I would have said sincerely that there was none.

Of course all of us retained dissatisfaction with the Central Committee, retained a hidden grudge; I do not deny this, but the fact remains that in recent years we did not assemble, each pursuing his own affairs. At EVDOKIMOV's, BAKAEV's and the oth\<ers'\> apartments, meetings were more of a family nature. As for the Leningrad organization, I did not know of it.

Of course EVDOKIMOV can speak about it, but I had no idea that there had been any center in Leningrad in recent years.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Did you know RUMYANTSEV, MANDELSHTAM, KOTOLYNOV?

Answer: I was certain that these “leaderless” young people had turned their backs on us long ago; I had no idea that they were carrying on any work; I was certain that in recent years they had been drawn into work and were working more or less honestly for the party.

In 1932 during the well-known revival of the center, even then, when I do not remember that there was any organization in Leningrad.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Did LEVIN visit you?

Answer: True, he visited, but there were no such conversations and I heard of no organization. Perhaps matters were arranged so that they created an organization without me. EVDOKIMOV will say whether, for example, he told me in recent years about an organization in Leningrad or not. I know that this did not occur, and as for this “leaderless” crowd, I knew nothing about them.

Question to ZINOVIEV: You undoubtedly knew that f\<ormer\> participants in the Zinovievist opposition had strengthened their positions in Leningrad. You also knew that they remained on their old counterrevolutionary positions. Do you confirm this?

Answer: About LEVIN I knew that he too thought that we supposedly “had been right” in many things, but there were no conversations about an organization. I know I am in a very unfavorable position before the investigation, but had you asked me a month ago, I would sincerely have said: I did not know and do not know.

Question to ZINOVIEV: That you are not giving truthful testimony is apparent if only from the fact that at every interrogation, under the pressure of facts, you add a little new information compared with your earlier testimony. YOU confirmed something today, admittedly in an insignificant dose. The investigation calls on you to give truthful testimony on the questions put to you.

Answer: What I recall I am ready to confirm and do confirm. I said what I really remember. I want to tell the truth here. Comrade KIROV's murder stunned me, as it did everyone.

I do not deny that my confirmation of facts during the investigation and subsequent account of everything known to me was an evolution from the positions I held at the beginning of the investigation.

I must acknowledge that when I was arrested I did not, of course, come here wishing to tell everything. But I long ago reached the opinion that I must now say everything about both myself and others.

I still say that I do not understand the story of the Leningrad organization. I should have known of this work, I could not have failed to know of it, but I did not know.

Among people connected with me I may have been a poor organizer, but still some sort of organizer, and all this could not have passed me by. But I say honestly that I do not understand what is being discussed and which organization. I still cannot understand this mystery.

That EVDOKIMOV went to Leningrad, that he spoke with his son-in-law, this was partly discussed; but that I knew LEVIN had become close to RUMYANTSEV, that they had organized some Leningrad center—I did not know.

Question to ZINOVIEV: All the investigation's materials say the opposite. The most striking expression of the organization's activity in Leningrad is the shot at Comrade KIROV and his death.

Answer: I am speaking now of Moscow. For us this was news and a surprise, and I cannot admit that you really suspect me of complicity in committing the terrorist act, or of encouraging or sympathizing with the fascist murder of S.M. KIROV.

Question to ZINOVIEV: The investigation has established that you and your like-minded associates educated your supporters in a spirit of hatred toward the party leadership and especially Comrade STALIN. Through sharp attacks you exacerbated attitudes and incited your supporters against the party leaders. You knew that among your supporters were resolute, active people. You inflamed terrorist attitudes among your organization's members, and you could not fail to understand that this led to terror.

Answer: About my attitude toward terror in principle I think I need not elaborate. I have always been as far from it as heaven from earth. I had neither seen nor heard of these people for many years, and it could not have occurred to me that they might become terrorists. As for the Leningrad organization, I did not know it and had heard of it from no one in recent years.

Question to ZINOVIEV: This is an unsupported denial. The investigation calls on you to give a truthful answer.

Answer: I really did not know this. I spoke with LEVIN, but this circumstance had no organizational meeting. I knew only that LEVIN had more or less anti-party attitudes, like myself.

Question to ZINOVIEV: In 1932 you told GERTIK to bring LEVIN to you. Do you acknowledge this?

Answer: GERTIK did indeed bring LEVIN to me. That I asked him to do so, I do not remember.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm that LEVIN headed the Leningrad organization?

Answer: I know nothing about this for the last two years. That GERTIK went to Leningrad in 1932 in connection with my expulsion, I do not remember. I said goodbye to GERTIK before leaving for exile, that is true. I met KOSTRITSKY last year in Kislovodsk; he barely greeted me. I was even surprised.

They speak of gatherings in 1932–1933; I know of no gatherings in 1933; I spoke about 1932 to the investigation in detail. There were no such gatherings, that is, with my participation, in 1933–1934. There were none and could have been none. I still say now that I spoke from the rostrum of the Seventeenth Congress entirely sincerely.

Regarding the united front in France. It is true that I recently had a conversation about this with EVDOKIMOV; I was very interested in the question. Discussion of it was not in a malicious tone but in a critical tone. That the Comintern was “neglected” was a subject of many conversations of mine with EVDOKIMOV himself and with KAMENEV, but this is neither the time nor place to speak of it.

The liquidation of the kulaks as a class dates to 1930. EVDOKIMOV says that at that time we spoke of how “the party leaders will cost the country dearly”; that is true, I too said this then.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Such conversations also occurred later in 1931–1932, 1933 and 1934; do you confirm this?

Answer: I do not conceal that both in 1933 and in 1934 I may have had conversations with someone close among those named, but they were far from being incitement in character. There was the dream that we would be believed and that Comrade STALIN would let us work “for real”; in particular, we all dwelt on this line because little by little we were after all becoming involved in work.

Since the question has been raised, I must say that we often thought \<that\> if we were given “proper work,” the disagreements we retained would very quickly dissipate. Whether that would have been so in practice is, of course, another question.

I sincerely ask EVDOKIMOV to explain how matters stood with the Leningrad organization. I did not know of the Leningrad organization, and the most I allowed here in the DPZ, the furthest I could reason, was that they probably had some personal meetings, as we had in Moscow, and that S.M. KIROV's murderer, NIKOLAEV, attached himself to these connections. That is all I can testify.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Do you wish to add anything to your testimony at the confrontation?

Answer: Regarding our attitudes in 1933–1934 I have already spoken in my testimony and consider it necessary to confirm here that in 1933–1934 our meetings took place increasingly rarely. I repeat what I have already said today: during these meetings our criticism concentrated mainly on Comintern questions.

As regards the characterization of our attitudes in 1933–1934 I agree with ZINOVIEV, but consider it necessary to add immediately, and I have already spoken of this in my testimony, that these were nevertheless not the attitudes of people who had understood the criminality of the counterrevolutionary positions they once held. The year 1934 was one of those moments during which we said that our line was being put into effect.

ZINOVIEV said more than once that our historical merit lay in having pushed the party and propelled it onto the correct path. At such moments we said that the party's general line was basically correct, but was being carried through in a deepened manner, leading to great overhead costs, and so on.

This was the attitude of people who remained on their former positions and who, judging from their entire past, if an opportunity presented itself or a reason appeared impelling them to become active, would become active again.

Question to EVDOKIMOV: Against the party?

Answer: Yes. Now regarding the Leningrad organization. ZINOVIEV says that if he had been asked some time ago about this Leningrad organization, he would have said that he knew nothing of it.

As the investigation knows, I initially answered this question in the same way, because both I and ZINOVIEV overlooked the circumstance that the issue was not form or name but the substance of what was happening in Leningrad and what was known to us.

And we knew that in Leningrad there was a definite circle of persons among f\<ormer\> Zinovievists who remained on the former counterrevolutionary positions of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist bloc of 1925–1927, and that these persons were connected with one another on the basis of those anti-party attitudes.

I have already spoken in my testimony, and repeat here the examples I gave there. When we decided in Moscow, in the Moscow center, how we should react at party meetings to ZINOVIEV's expulsion from the party over the Ryutin affair, we took this decision not only for Moscow but for Leningrad too. We expected that our supporters in Leningrad would put this decision into effect in a more or less organized manner.

It was not easy for me either to become conscious of this very existence of the organization. When I initially said that I did not know of the Leningrad underground organization of Zinovievists as such, and when in the record of the confrontation with SHAROV himself I asked for the investigation's direct question about what I knew of the organization's activity in Leningrad to be changed to the formulation “What do you know about individual participants in the organization in Leningrad?”—I was wrong.

I then made the same error ZINOVIEV has now made: I overlooked the substance of the matter, that the fundamental point was not what form the organization assumed, but the fact of the existence in Leningrad of a definite group of persons—our like-minded associates—their ties with one another and their connection with Moscow in the forms of which I spoke.

As for the terrorist act of which Comrade KIROV fell victim, I have already said in my testimony and repeat here: yes, we must bear political and moral responsibility for this act. For an underground organization of “Zinovievists” could hardly have taken shape in Leningrad had it not been for our policy of many years of deceiving the party, had it not been for the circumstance that our group existed in Moscow, which all opposition groupings—the outside world, so to speak, as ZINOVIEV expressed it—and the Leningrad Zinovievists regarded as the moral center of the f\<ormer\> opposition.

I repeat that it was not a moral center of the opposition; it was a center which at different moments, irrespective of the situation in the country and the party, remained throughout on counterrevolutionary positions and headed a group of people with anti-party attitudes, ready to come forward actively with their political views in opposition to the party's views.

Were it not for this circumstance, the underground counterrevolutionary organization of Zinovievists that took shape in Leningrad could most probably not have formed there.

I consider it necessary to add that, recognizing our political and moral responsibility in the sense stated above for the terrorist act against Comrade KIROV, I declare that this act contradicts all my political convictions and my entire political past.
