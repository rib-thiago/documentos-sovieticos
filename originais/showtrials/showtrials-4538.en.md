INTERROGATION RECORD

Of SAFAROV G. I., January 20, 1935.

(Supplementary).

In addition to my testimony, I testify to the investigation as follows: particularly in the autumn and at the end of 1932, and also in the first half of 1933, LOMINADZE, as MAGYAR related to me, and also NASONOV in conversations with me, sharply attacked the line of the delegation of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in the Comintern and the line of its Central Committee in German affairs, and portrayed the Central Committee of the German Communist Party as “a worthless, headless leadership.” They presented matters as follows: “The Stalinist leadership does not want to give battle to Hitler, consciously paralyzes the will of the working masses to struggle, consciously diverts them from the struggle against the fascists toward the struggle against social fascism. This is a sure course toward defeat. The German Communist Party is cut off from the masses and disintegrates the workers who want to fight through its preaching of passive waiting for events. In reality, the German proletariat ought to be mobilized to fight the fascists with weapons in their hands. Precisely because, instead of staking everything on armed struggle against the Hitlerites, the Party agitates for avoiding the general battle, it loses political initiative and influence over the masses more and more every day; initiative and mass influence pass to Hitler. This is a crime against the German revolution, whose interests are sacrificed to the interests of peaceful construction in the USSR. Stalin is guilty of this. He is the one who set Heinz NEUMANN and others to act in Germany in this spirit.”

It was exactly thus, literally, that N. NASONOV conveyed to me in conversations LOMINADZE's views and his own, NASONOV's, on the state of affairs in Germany. For me, this view of Lominadze's followers was in no way unexpected, since in their time in China as well, LOMINADZE and his like-minded associates had appeared as zealous advocates of insurrectionary adventurism, putschism, and the organization of armed uprisings at any cost, everywhere. In essence, they proposed the same thing for Germany in 1932–1933. I pointed this out to NASONOV in my conversations with him, considering that the leadership's mistaken policy, which I too assessed in an anti-Party spirit, lay in its refusal to apply united-front tactics. I remember that in 1933 MAGYAR, in conversation with me about the German Communist Party Central Committee's proposal for a united front to the all-German reformist trade-union federation and Social Democrats, said that “they consciously postponed applying united-front tactics until all deadlines had passed and the proposals for a united front had become a mockery.” I did not agree with this.

In the autumn and winter of 1932–1933, NASONOV told me that he had read Trotsky's articles and pamphlets assessing the situation in Germany and considered that much of what was said there was correct, both about the state of the Party, the danger of missing the revolutionary situation, and the leadership. At the same time, MAGYAR went along the Comintern corridors and in conversations savored even individual phrases of Trotsky's.

I have already testified about MAGYAR's account to me of his conversation with LOMINADZE after LOMINADZE met with me in mid-1933. It was precisely at this time that Lominadze's followers conducted a systematic campaign of slander and provocation against the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in connection with the German question, and at the same time LOMINADZE and his close like-minded associate NASONOV flaunted their friendly attitude toward Trotsky and the Trotskyists. In conversation with MAGYAR, LOMINADZE directly said that he considered his group's principal mistake to have been the struggle against TROTSKY and the Trotskyists, which had changed the balance of forces in the Party.

Lazar SHATSKIN, seeing me at approximately the same time in the Kremlin hospital, immediately asked: “Well, are things heading toward complete defeat in Germany?” MAGYAR, a very close friend and trusted person of LOMINADZE and at the same time an agent of ZINOVIEV, pressed in conversations with me the point that LOMINADZE and “his people” “are sincerely indignant that at such a time ZINOVIEV has not been brought into the Comintern leadership” and that they “repent of having fought Trotsky.”

In conversations with me in 1932, NASONOV expressed sharp hostility toward the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), constantly saying that “the bureaucracy will pervert and distort any policy beyond recognition, turn it into its opposite,” spoke bitterly of the Party leaders, and, in aid of MAGYAR, spread the most anti-Party “information” about difficulties in industry and famine in Ukraine, relating “large peasant actions and even uprisings” in Ukraine and Siberia. In words, he flaunted “irreconcilability toward the kulak and the Right,” but invariably explained difficulties with the countryside by saying that “officials, according to a fraudulent figure set from above, take an excessive amount from the peasant; the apparatus is cut off from the masses, flatters and ingratiates itself with superiors who are ready to cover up all the sycophants' sins.”

Throughout his work in the Comintern and afterward, NASONOV invariably displayed an extraordinarily great reverence for MAGYAR's “authority,” which was, among other things, a consequence of LOMINADZE's attachment to MAGYAR. NASONOV himself took a special interest in all counterrevolutionary gossip about “disagreements” in the Central Committee and spread this gossip himself, savoring it. NASONOV met with MAGYAR frequently during his time in the Comintern and, after leaving it, on his visits to Moscow.

NASONOV belongs among LOMINADZE's closest and most “faithful” men. In 1930, during the exposure of the anti-Party Right-Leftist bloc, he declared that “assertions about Beso's double-dealing are lies and demagogy,” that “all this has been invented and staged,” and that “LOMINADZE has nothing in common with the Right.” NASONOV's closest friends are LOMINADZE's closest friends and prominent representatives of Lominadze's anti-Party grouping: CHAPLIN, VARTANYAN, and ARKADYEV. In Lominadze's grouping, contacts operate under the cover of personal relations.

NASONOV spent considerable time in leading work for the Young Communist International abroad, then studied at the Institute of Red Professors. Like all Lominadze followers, he likes to “arrive at all conclusions with his own mind,” concocts “original” confusion, and portrays himself as a “critically thinking individual” standing “many heads above official sycophants.” He is a pupil of the Lominadze–Sten school and is likewise convinced that “cadres of young people must develop who will replace the official leaders.” He expressed this in conversations and, like other Lominadze followers, loudly expressed indignation at the replacement of the Young Communist International leadership in 1931, accompanied by the removal of VARTANYAN, himself, and others from leading international work.

Written in my own hand:

SAFAROV.

HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE USSR NKVD — PETROVSKY.
