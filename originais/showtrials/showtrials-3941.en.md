INTERROGATION RECORD

OF ANISHEV Anatoly Isaevich, 31 December 1934.

On the questions put to me by the investigation, I testify additionally:

1\. The Zinovievist organization's unprincipled blocs with anybody whatsoever, provided they opposed the party leadership and Stalin, arose both from its counterrevolutionary leadership's bestial hatred of Stalin and from the absence of any other political line (particularly in ZINOVIEV himself) apart from capitalist restoration, which always showed through behind all his vacillations.

The extent to which Left phrases served ZINOVIEV merely as tactical cover for capitalist restoration is apparent, for example, from the following:

During the drafting of the platform in 1927, when I came to Moscow, SAFAROV showed me the draft agrarian section written by SMILGA and approved by ZINOVIEV. Its basis was the demand to replace the tax with differential rent. Meeting me at his apartment, SMILGA formulated his positions quite clearly in the following propositions:

– that capitalism in Russian agriculture was still far from exhausting its progressive possibilities;

– that any attempt to restrain capitalist development in the countryside was reactionary and retarded the development of productive forces;

– that our line should be capitalism in the countryside, socialism in the city.

These were precisely the propositions he made the basis of drafting the platform.

SAFAROV then mobilized me and BYALY, and the three of us hurriedly began writing another version of the agrarian section, proceeding from the fundamental proposition that “capitalism in our country can triumph first and foremost as agrarian capitalism” (see the platform drafts among my papers). I do not know how it happened, but ZINOVIEV approved our draft too; the resulting platform, particularly the counter-theses for the Fifteenth Congress, was a compilation of both versions (that is, the counter-theses included the kulak theory, the demand to replace the tax with differential rent).

The search for blocs with the Rights in 1929–1932 was therefore no accident.

On collectivization, the Zinovievist organization's position essentially differed in no respect from the Rights', that is, it was openly kulak.

Already in 1932 and 1933 GERTIK and ELKOVICH visited us in Leningrad from the Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization. Both said the pace of collectivization was administratively imposed on the country; people were being driven forcibly into collective farms (even using military force, as ELKOVICH described concerning the North Caucasus); peasants joined because otherwise they would receive neither land nor goods; this led to peasant uprisings, famine and destruction of rural productive forces; pressure for collectivization would inevitably have to be followed by another turn back to individual farming; it would be better to make that turn now than wait until peasant uprisings forced it, but the party leadership, Comrade STALIN, was incapable of making it because through Left policy he created his authority as a victor.

These openly kulak, counterrevolutionary directives were fundamental to the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization's center.

ELKOVICH developed these directives shortly after the Ryutin–Zinoviev group's exposure. ZINOVIEV's connection with RYUTIN was not condemned.

Likewise, when KOSTINA's intervention in the ZINOVIEV–RYUTIN case was discussed at BAKAEV's apartment in 1933, in the presence, if memory serves, of KUKLIN, GESSEN, ELKOVICH and myself, nobody condemned ZINOVIEV's connection with RYUTIN; only tactical questions were discussed: how to behave at meetings to get out of the game without losses, conceal our sympathy for ZINOVIEV and avoid exposing our double-dealing.

ELKOVICH's visit to Leningrad in early 1933 had an entirely clear purpose: to explain to the Leningrad organization's members the necessity of blocs with the Rights.

On industrialization too nothing remained of Left phrases. There was a quite clear Right position, that of counterrevolutionary restorationists.

At the same “tea gathering” at BAKAEV's in 1933, the program of producing eight million tons of pig iron was assessed as another piece of demagogy by the party leadership, an obviously unachievable program. Organization members' attitude toward the pace of industrialization adopted by the party can be formulated approximately thus: “Sit and wait until the leadership, Comrade Stalin, breaks its head on unachievable tempos, then resume the struggle for leadership. This was not said openly, but followed quite clearly from the overall assessment of the situation, hints, and seizing on and exaggerating individual failures.

Such sentiments prevailed, differing in no respect from the sentiments and directives of counterrevolutionary wreckers.

PERIMOV, then working somewhere in construction, reflected these sentiments quite clearly in a conversation with me in a Moscow street in summer 1933. I do not remember the details, but recall that it was the talk of someone compelled to participate in construction, who did not believe in it and expected its failure.

ALEKSANDROV and ANTONOV gave the same assessment of construction when visiting NATANSON in late 1932.

2\. I consider it necessary to report the following facts known to me:

After NATANSON's arrest in February 1933, I went to RUMYANTSEV's so he could find out what the matter was and asked him to sound out SMORODIN. RUMYANTSEV said nothing would come of approaching SMORODIN, that this should be done with KOMAROV, but that it was inconvenient for him to approach KOMAROV just then and he would do so when the opportunity arose.

Among non-Trotskyist Leningrad officials, MAVRIN displayed an active interest in NATANSON's case; as she told me, he had visited her at the House of Preliminary Detention.

Written in my own hand. — ANISHEV.

INTERROGATED BY: HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO, UGB — LULOV.

Certified correct: V. Stoiko
