INTERROGATION RECORD

Of G. I. SAFAROV.

In addition to my testimony of December 23, 1934, I testify as follows in response to the investigator's questions:

In a conversation with me in July 1932, ZINOVIEV asked: “You constantly meet representatives of foreign parties in the Comintern. Are there any among them capable of thinking independently and raising their voices when necessary?” Of course, this meant seeking out “representatives” who might wage a struggle against the party. I replied that I knew of none. “Yes,” said ZINOVIEV, “MAGYAR holds the same opinion. The Comintern is no longer what it was; it has completely lost its individuality.” When the conversation turned to a possible military clash between the USSR and Japan, ZINOVIEV said: “Serious international complications will inevitably lead to major reshuffles and upheavals in the Central Committee. Then the question of bringing us in may arise differently” (obviously, into power). This opinion was expressed as an additional argument against my proposal to address the Central Committee with a signed document.

In the summer of 1932, before meeting ZINOVIEV in Ilyinskoye, I went to SHLYAPNIKOV's apartment for negotiations. SHLYAPNIKOV declared that the existing situation was, of course, disastrous for the party. “But what platform is there to write and send to the Central Committee?” he said. “As for us, we have our own platform.” “What platform?” I asked. “Our platform is the old one, with which we fought against LENIN,” he replied. “Both you and Trotsky attacked us for it, and now you are paying for it along with us. Still, it is something to think about. KAYUROV is doing something with the young people.” He avoided providing details about the nature of this “activity with young people.” Obviously, the “leaders” of the counterrevolutionary “Workers' Opposition” had a hand in the Ryutin platform.

In my conversation with ZINOVIEV in Ilyinskoye, either I or ZINOVIEV mentioned EMELYANOV's name. I told ZINOVIEV about my conversation with SHLYAPNIKOV. ZINOVIEV reacted as follows: “Well, the old platform is nonsense, of course. But he is a valuable man and a dyed-in-the-wool Bolshevik. How highly ILYICH valued him (!?), yet the Stalinists have hounded him mercilessly.”

Meeting SHLYAPNIKOV in the street, I realized that he was unsympathetic to the idea of addressing the Central Committee and therefore did not wish to go beyond “mutual contact.”

Later, I once met him late at night on the Arbat in the first half of 1934. He asked: “What is new with you in the Comintern?” “Well, we are implementing the united front tactic, and not without success. I am not such a pessimist now.” “Oh, give that up, SAFAROV,” declared SHLYAPNIKOV. “What workers, the devil take it, do you have in your parties? The workers are in the Second International, in the Social Democratic parties.” “So would you have us go to Vandervelde?” “Drop the official demagogy,” SHLYAPNIKOV said indignantly. I swore and left without saying goodbye.

It is utterly impossible to evade the question of MAGYAR's role in this whole tangle of counterrevolutionary conspiracy. This man managed adroitly to evade responsibility for belonging to the Trotskyist-Zinovievist counterrevolutionary bloc. Remaining in the party, during my exile in Achinsk in 1928 he was the Moscow plenipotentiary of the counterrevolutionary grouping of the so-called leaderless, acting on instructions and advice from Achinsk.

I can say with confidence that after my return from exile and the breakup of the leaderless group in July 1928, MAGYAR probably immediately joined the Zinovievist grouping, simultaneously establishing a firm and permanent connection with the LOMINADZE–STEN grouping. He became close to LOMINADZE at the time of the Eighth Plenum of the Executive Committee of the Communist International in 1928. Throughout his work in the Comintern, he collected and spread all manner of philistine, intrigue-ridden and counterrevolutionary rumors, gossip and jokes. He always knew about everything, using his extensive Comintern connections, his Hungarian acquaintances, and meetings, conversations and information picked up at “Bolshevik” and “Pravda.” Today he would bring the “freshest” gossip about “disagreements and disputes in the Central Committee”; a day or two later, about difficulties in industry (citing conversations with LAUER or KRAVAL [1]); a week later, he would popularize a new counterrevolutionary joke. He always presented Comintern affairs from a thoroughly intrigue-ridden perspective, with details and sensational stories about personal squabbles in the Central Committees of the respective parties. Such was his entire “working style.” Into all his work in the Comintern he introduced the spirit of a hardened bourgeois journalist hunting for sensations and intrigues.

Incidentally, that was precisely what he had been in Hungary before the revolution. At the same time, he always astonished one by his exceptional chameleon-like evasiveness. He could deliver entire speeches saying that the Central Committee was no good, that STALIN had no principles, no line; he could slander the party as he pleased, and literally within tens of seconds, when a party member arrived, switch to paeans to STALIN, his firmness and wisdom, and so forth.

For ZINOVIEV, whom he met astonishingly often and regularly until recently, he was, one might say, a “nutrient broth,” since all this Magyar gossip (the expression is not mine; it was a common description of his counterrevolutionary chatter) raised ZINOVIEV's cowardly, dissolute “spirits,” and he immediately began “combining” his counterrevolutionary conclusions, equipping them with new “arguments.” This gossip and slander was undoubtedly passed on along the chain of the Zinovievist organization.

Examples of gossip and slander: on one occasion, in 1932, I believe, MAGYAR said that L. M. KAGANOVICH had fallen out of favor: “He will soon be banished to the Comintern.” On another occasion, early in 1934, MAGYAR told me in MIF's presence that L. M. KAGANOVICH “has spent enormous sums on the metro; construction projects are stopping for lack of materials; STALIN gave him a severe dressing-down.”

Throughout 1931–34, MAGYAR vigorously spread rumors about the “struggle between MOLOTOV and KAGANOVICH.”

Foreign gossip was much the same: “Thälmann's boys, they say, sit in the Central Committee of the German Communist Party, understand nothing and are incapable of leading. Old PIECK actually has no say,” and so on and so forth.

MAGYAR was connected with STEN and LOMINADZE and often visited them.

From LOMINADZE, I recall, he once brought the gossip that “a new regime has been introduced in defense factories; the director can arrest anyone; the workers are being kept down. Where are we heading?”

In reporting his meetings with STEN and LOMINADZE, he was extremely restrained and tried to say as little as possible. I explain this by the fact that, by all indications, he was the connecting link and intermediary between the Zinovievist organization and the Lominadze–Sten grouping, and had probably been advised to protect this connection from his own garrulousness. For both groupings, he was truly indispensable, both because of his abundant contacts and information and because of the protection afforded by his official position. As I indicated in my previous testimony, I reached ZINOVIEV in Ilyinskoye through MAGYAR, who insisted on the meeting, said that “Grigory absolutely wants to see you,” and precisely arranged the time and place. He himself visited Ilyinskoye before and after this, and also ZINOVIEV's city apartment. It was always easy to guess whether MAGYAR had been to ZINOVIEV or LOMINADZE before a particular meeting with me. This weather vane always began the conversation with ideas and considerations he had heard in one place or the other. He passed them off as his own, then reinforced them: Grigory (or another interlocutor) thinks so too. Magyar was the “People's Commissariat for Foreign Affairs” of all the oppositions. An additional link between ZINOVIEV and MAGYAR was, of course, GORSHENIN, who worked in the RSFSR Gosplan before the purge and subsequently at the IMKh [2]. Some of Magyar's information undoubtedly reached ZINOVIEV through GORSHENIN. GORSHENIN was, one might say, a one-hundred-percent Zinovievist, unwilling to see or hear anything except his “leader's” gestures and statements. He and MAGYAR were close friends. When ZINOVIEV was sent to Kustanai, GORSHENIN obtained literature for him (books and foreign newspapers) and other things, and MAGYAR marveled at “his selflessness,” remarking: “Look, GERTIK was jailed for handing Grigory a stack of paper at the station, while GORSHENIN openly does everything for ZINOVIEV.”

During the revival of counterrevolutionary conspiratorial activity in the summer and autumn of 1932, in connection with the “platforms” and other matters, MAGYAR told me in one conversation: “If you are arrested for submitting a platform to the Central Committee, rest assured: I guarantee it will become known abroad and your document will be printed there.” MAGYAR subsequently traveled abroad and probably established contacts there, although he did not tell me about them. Knowing of MAGYAR's closeness to LOMINADZE and LOMINADZE's friendship with Heinz NEUMANN [3], I believe there may have been conversations with Heinz, who had been removed from work in the German Communist Party.

LOMINADZE tried, in 1933, I believe, to establish connections abroad, as is evident from his attempted conversation with MOREK [4]. After this unsuccessful attempt, Comrade PYATNITSKY told me: “Advise your LOMINADZE not to meddle in Comintern affairs, lest things turn out worse.” Obviously, he considered me as close to LOMINADZE as MAGYAR was.

The Zinovievists' connection with the Trotskyists was maintained through EVDOKIMOV, who constantly met people in the Council of People's Commissars dining hall. In general, that dining hall served as an extremely convenient place for meetings, negotiations and conversations, not only for EVDOKIMOV and SMIRNOV but also for other representatives of the antiparty groupings. It provided the “most legitimate,” legal cover for meetings and negotiations that otherwise involved a number of difficulties. Connections through the dining hall were especially active in 1932; it was used for the same purpose until recently [5].

From conversations with SMIRNOV, I concluded that the Trotskyists considered EVDOKIMOV the most authoritative representative of the Zinovievist organization, on whom, in their opinion, they could rely much more than on the “pair,” ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. In 1932 SMIRNOV had the broadest connections with all the Trotskyists in exile and elsewhere. He had an entire notebook of exiles' addresses, with instructions about whom to send rusks, books and the like. SMIRNOV played the role of the Trotskyist opposition's personnel department; his official position in the People's Commissariat for Heavy Industry also enabled him to have extensive personal contacts during his journeys and business trips. I remember that, in conversation with MRACHKOVSKY, he specifically planned whom he would see along the way when traveling to Sovetskaya Gavan.

To what I reported in my first testimony about my conversation with E. PREOBRAZHENSKY, I add the following: when I told MRACHKOVSKY about this conversation in SMIRNOV's presence and expressed my surprise at PREOBRAZHENSKY's position, MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV somehow strangely hushed the matter up, although their attitude toward the “Stalinists” usually erupted in fierce indignation against “toadies, official people,” and so forth. As far as I recall, when MRACHKOVSKY departed, SHURA SAFONOVA was in his carriage; it was precisely through her that he suggested I send a letter with someone traveling. It was her telephone number that I was given.

My personal connection with VARDIN broke off around the middle of 1933. He was constantly in an antiparty frame of mind and met VAGANYAN [6], LOMINADZE and the Zinovievists on several occasions. He always sought an opportunity to offer his antiparty assessment of the situation. Both he and LELEVICH, with whom I personally broke in 1928, always counterposed their views to the party's in the style in which these “dyed-in-the-wool Na Postu supporters” were accustomed to arguing with literary “fellow travelers.”

VAGANYAN remained the same “orthodox” counterrevolutionary Trotskyist he had always been. In 1932 it was said in the circles of the counterrevolutionary opposition groupings (Zinovievists-Trotskyists): “VAGANYAN is looking for some platform against the Central Committee to sign.”

Sergei GESSEN is a constant and unchanging Zinovievist who always maintained contact with the Zinovievist organization. His specialty is composing vulgar counterrevolutionary verse and spreading counterrevolutionary rumors, gossip and jokes under the guise of friendly sociability, right up to the most recent period. Counterrevolutionary bohemia is widely represented in the Zinovievist grouping, and GESSEN is typical as a representative of this degenerate, thoroughly prostituted bohemia.

In this connection, Sergei MANDELSHTAM must be mentioned. I lost sight of him long ago. In 1930 he was removed from Leningrad. He was always infected with active anti-Soviet sentiments. In 1929–30 he was undoubtedly a leader of former Komsomol members previously numbered among the leaderless. I know that after my statement to the Central Control Commission in 1928, he portrayed himself as the “guardian of the true traditions of the struggle.” He corrupted a group of former Leningrad Komsomol members both politically and in their personal lives, drawing them into drinking bouts with counterrevolutionary discussions. In particular, he strongly influenced V. RUMYANTSEV. “Vodka, women, politics”: such was Mandelshtam's mixture of counterrevolution and every sort of degeneration.

Vladimir MATVEEV, former secretary of “Leningradskaya Pravda,” as I can judge from meetings with him, became mired in counterrevolutionary philistinism, both because of his home environment and under the influence of resentment that he was “not recognized in literature.” His closest circle includes the well-known poet ZAB<O>LOTSKY, who wrote mockingly about “asses singing the praises of socialism.” Already in the autumn of 1934, meeting him at his apartment, I was struck by the state he had reached. The conversation turned to the possibility of war with Japan, and I spoke about the situation of Japanese workers and peasants. MATVEEVA, his wife, interrupted me: “Do you think our socialism is any better?” MATVEEV kept a sympathetic silence. I quarreled fiercely, saying that one had to sink lower than a prostitute to prefer ARAKI [7] to the country of socialism. A few days later, MATVEEV felt it necessary to emphasize his thinking even more and sent me a letter expressing the hope that everything was over between us. This philistine is significant as an expression of the spirit of internal emigration that has engulfed the participants in the Trotskyist-Zinovievist counterrevolutionary bloc.

In addition to my testimony of 23/XII/1934, I state that SMILGA was connected with the Zinovievist organization primarily through KAMENEV, with whom he was in constant contact.

The Zinovievists also include Yakov ELKOVICH, who visited EVDOKIMOV and others when coming to Moscow from Sverdlovsk.

Written in my own hand: G. SAFAROV.

[1] In the text, erroneously: “Kravalom.”

[2] Institute of World Economy and World Politics (IMKh i MP): a scientific institution of the USSR that existed in 1924–1947 and studied current economic conditions and the history and theory of economic cycles and crises.

[3] Heinz Neumann (1902–1937): activist in the German and world communist movement, journalist. Leader of the ultraleft wing of the Communist Party of Germany. Representative of the German Communist Party to the Comintern in 1924–1928. In 1931 Neumann developed disagreements with the policies of Stalin and Ernst Thälmann: he considered that they underestimated the danger of a fascist seizure of power. He was criticized in October 1932; in November 1932 he was removed from party posts and lost his Reichstag seat. At the end of 1934 he was arrested in Zurich by the Swiss immigration authorities, spent six months in detention and was then expelled from the country. After coming to the Soviet Union, he settled there. On April 27, 1937, he was arrested by the NKVD. On November 26, 1937, he was sentenced to death by the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court of the USSR and shot the same day.

[4] Possibly Novak Marek (real name Adolf Lampe) (1900–1943). Member of the Communist Party of Poland from 1921. From 1929 to 1930, a member of its Central Committee Politburo and of the Politburo's foreign secretariat in Berlin. Representative of the Communist Party of Poland on the Executive Committee of the Red International of Labor Unions from 1932 to 1933.

[5] This “brilliant” idea was subsequently adopted by the NKVD “scriptwriters” during preparations for the August 1936 show trial.

[6] In the text, erroneously: “Vaganyanom” [Ваганьяном].

[7] Sadao Araki (1877–1966): Japanese statesman and military figure.
