INTERROGATION RECORD

Of Ivan Petrovich BAKAEV,

dated January 6–7, 1935.

Question: What do you know about the counterrevolutionary group of Zinovievists in Leningrad?

Answer: As is known, the Zinovievist opposition had its deepest roots in Leningrad in 1925, 1926, and 1927. Despite the subsequent defeat of the opposition, throughout 1928–1934 we had fairly substantial cadres in Leningrad devoted to ZINOVIEV and to the ideas developed by the Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground.

The emergence of the counterrevolutionary group in Leningrad should be dated to the period of the Fifteenth Congress of the AUCP(b). Its prerequisite was that a considerable part of the Zinovievist opposition, on entering the Party, did not actually renounce their views and regarded themselves as cadres who would be needed to continue the struggle against the Party. In 1928, as ZINOVIEV's emissary, on his special assignment, I traveled from Moscow to Leningrad and worked to persuade oppositionists to return to the Party; I then had to wage no small struggle against the so-called leaderless, headed by SAFAROV, SARKIS, NAUMOV, and others, who opposed returning to the AUCP(b).

In my speeches I pointed out that a new wave of internal Party struggle could not be ruled out, that economic difficulties were approaching which might cause these complications within the Party.

On returning to the Party, the opposition cadres would have to play the role of a factor correcting the Party's policy and preventing the Party from turning right. —

I went on to say that outside the Party we would be doomed to a wretched existence as private citizens or would take the path of outright counterrevolution.

I must say that this trip of mine to Leningrad produced results; I became convinced of this both then and subsequently when I visited Leningrad. In particular, I was in Leningrad during LASHEVICH's funeral, I think in 1929; ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, and other oppositionists were there too; we met old opposition comrades who still sympathized politically with ZINOVIEV, constituted a separate group, and intended to develop political ties with us, the Muscovites.

The counterrevolutionary group of Leningrad Zinovievists included a fairly large number of old oppositionists tested in the previous struggle; I do not know many of the group's participants; some grouped around such active persons as Vladimir LEVIN and RUMYANTSEV, who in turn maintained ties with S. MANDELSHTAM, SOSITSKY, and MYASNIKOV. These comrades were the center of the group, had direct, immediate ties with us, the Muscovites: with ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, GERTIK, myself, and others.

I can name a number of Leningrad Zinovievists who maintained ties until recently with individual members of the Moscow central group: V. LEVIN, RUMYANTSEV, ANISHEV, the husband of Muska NATANSON, an old oppositionist; Konstantin SOLOVIEV, recently manager of the “ROSSAT” trust, which produced various kinds of fittings; Dmitry SHIRYAEV, a relative of EVDOKIMOV.

Furthermore, I know that until 1932 KOTOLYNOV and RUMYANTSEV maintained political ties with the Zinovievists NAUMOV and LEPESHINSKAYA.

Besides these persons I know a number of other Zinovievists, about whom I shall testify below, but my information about their ties to the Moscow center is limited to 1928, 1929, 1930, 1931, and 1932; this does not mean that these persons did not belong to Zinovievist groups in subsequent years; in any event, I simply do not know about that.

The following persons belong to this category: BASHKIROV; TARTAKOVSKAYA, an engineer; TIMOFEEV, her husband, the latter two being particularly irreconcilable in their attitudes; TSATUROV, who visited ZINOVIEV, GERTIK, and EVDOKIMOV; Leshka ZHULEV, whom I know from the Petrograd Side, a man very embittered against the Party leadership, whom I met in company with another person whose surname I cannot recall; Muska NATANSON knows ZHULEV well; MIRONOV, who held a responsible job on the Petrograd Side; MIRONOV may know ZHULEV; MIRONOV was personally connected with ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, and GERTIK. I last met him in the Alexander Garden in Moscow in 1931, gave him my address, and asked him to visit, but he did not come. Next I remember Fedor PICHURIN, a reserved man; V. P. MATVEEV, a newspaper worker, who systematically visited GERTIK and also visited ZINOVIEV and EVDOKIMOV. I further recall ZELIKSON, a former military worker, whom I met at GERTIK's; he subsequently left for Siberia; he had strong anti-Party attitudes.

Ties between the Moscow Zinovievist center and the Leningrad group were maintained either through visits to Leningrad by individual participants in the center or through visits to Moscow by individual members of the Leningrad group, who went to the apartments of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, GERTIK, and myself.

I know that Vladimir LEVIN, Vladimir RUMYANTSEV, MIRONOV, REM, TSVIBAK, and NALIVAIKO visited ZINOVIEV; A. MUSHTAKOV, D. SHIRYAEV, V. LEVIN, RUMYANTSEV, MATVEEV, and others visited EVDOKIMOV's apartment.

The apartment of center member GERTIK was the main headquarters through which all Leningraders usually passed before reaching the other center members.

V. LEVIN and many other comrades visited him.

As for my apartment and KUKLIN's, we mainly received Muscovites, members of the center, although I must say that individual meetings with Leningraders also took place at my home. The Leningrader ANISHEV visited me; he is an old oppositionist, a participant in the Zinovievist underground. In our circles it was said that he had been expelled from the AUCP(b) as a Kolchak officer. He visited in 1933 and 1934. Once, in his presence at my apartment, I think GORSHENIN raised the question of the prospects for developing ferrous metallurgy; I do not remember who else was there; we all considered the production program of 8 million tons of pig iron set by the Party and government to be another piece of demagogy by the Party leadership, since this target was clearly impossible to fulfill.

On another visit ANISHEV informed us of Muska NATANSON's situation in exile, that she had been arrested again because a counterrevolutionary diary had been found in her apartment, written by the bourgeois nationalist ABDULRAKHMAN [1], who had infiltrated the Party and been arrested for sabotaging grain procurements.

I immediately conveyed this information about the exile, with whom we had formerly maintained active political ties, to another participant in the Moscow center, Grigory FEDOROV.

Our interest in the fate of our cadres was no accident, since we systematically collected various information about Zinovievist comrades, keeping track of where each was located.

On arriving in Moscow, ANISHEV had the habit of making the rounds of the apartments of EVDOKIMOV, GERTIK, FEDOROV, SAFAROV, NAUMOV, and other comrades in order, as he said, to “stock up on information”; he then visited me, shared political news, tried to establish what I knew, and, laden with the collected information, left for Leningrad.

Twice I accompanied Vl. LEVIN to ZINOVIEV's apartment.

LEVIN held a political conversation with him, describing the state of affairs in Leningrad, giving ZINOVIEV a detailed account of the city's workers' food situation and speaking about their political attitudes. I did not usually take part in these conversations, going into another room or, while present, looking through journals and newspapers, and consequently did not hear all the conversations.

I was also present at other meetings between ZINOVIEV and individual Leningraders.

From KAMENEV I know that the Komsomol members RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV, and TARASOV approached him for advice concerning their submission of a statement to Comrade YAROSLAVSKY at the former Central Control Commission of the AUCP(b) for reinstatement in the Party: L. B. KAMENEV told me this, I think in 1929. KAMENEV edited the statement, and the three comrades mentioned were soon admitted to the AUCP(b). This fact of reinstatement in the Party expressed the group solidarity that already existed within the Leningrad and Moscow Zinovievist underground.

Members of the center traveled to Leningrad repeatedly. GERTIK, who went there more than once, told me about his latest trip in 1934; he met Vladimir LEVIN, V. RUMYANTSEV, and others; he reported that these comrades were wholly ours, sought information from him on the state of affairs in Moscow, demanded information, and were crushed by ZINOVIEV's position, by his absence from the leadership.

I know that when GERTIK was in Leningrad in 1932, he met LEVIN and others and informed them that the number of those discontented with the Party leadership within the Party ranks was continually increasing.

KUKLIN also visited Leningrad. I know that he was in Leningrad in 1933. He met LEVIN and RUMYANTSEV, who informed him of attitudes in the Party organization and spoke of the loyalty to ZINOVIEV of the section of oppositionists associated with them.

Describing the work of individual participants in the group, RUMYANTSEV and LEVIN said that “the people are studying; they will become engineers, good cadres.” In 1932 KUKLIN was also in Leningrad; as far as I remember, of the group's participants he saw only SOLOVIEV.

I know that PERIMOV, a person very close to ZINOVIEV, also traveled to Leningrad. In 1932, when he was in Leningrad, he met V. LEVIN, MANDELSHTAM, and other comrades. I assume that he had special assignments concerning Leningrad entrusted confidentially to him alone by ZINOVIEV. I also assume that during 1933 and 1934 PERIMOV visited me on G. E. ZINOVIEV's instructions to find out my attitudes and with whom I maintained ties, since in those years I did not visit ZINOVIEV.

As for EVDOKIMOV, I know that he often visited Leningrad; he came at different times throughout the year, came annually before May Day, obtained tickets for parades from the regional executive committee, and attended them repeatedly; he usually stayed with his relatives. I do not remember the results of his trips; I know only that in my presence ZINOVIEV personally expressed dissatisfaction to EVDOKIMOV, calling him a “lumbering oaf,” a bear, over the results of these trips; they were unsatisfactory.

Question: Did the Moscow center of the Zinovievist underground have any political ties outside Leningrad, in other cities?

Answer: Yes, there were underground participants in other cities too.

Thus, in Rostov-on-Don, the old oppositionist Pyotr Eduardovich ROTSKAN was a participant in the Zinovievist underground; recently he lived in Pyatigorsk; I was close to him and had known him since 1917. He belonged to a group of Zinovievists alongside Timofei DMITRIEV and Nikolai GORDON. Each of these persons, when coming to Moscow on various business, visited one of the Moscow center's members. ROTSKAN usually visited me when in Moscow; he last visited me in 1934; he visited ZINOVIEV and GERTIK; T. DMITRIEV visited me, ZINOVIEV, and GERTIK. GORDON mainly visited GERTIK and ZINOVIEV.

A participant in our underground, Yakov ELKOVICH, is in Sverdlovsk. I have known him since 1922–23. I saw ELKOVICH several months ago in Moscow; he had arrived on a business trip and was staying with GORSHENIN. GORSHENIN telephoned me and said that ELKOVICH was in Moscow and “longs to see you.” I went; we talked on general subjects. I know that GORSHENIN had given him political information in the usual anti-Party, counterrevolutionary terms before my arrival. Whether ELKOVICH visited ZINOVIEV on that trip I do not know. I know that he usually visited ZINOVIEV.

The old oppositionist, now a participant in our counterrevolutionary underground, Boris SAKHOV lives in Arkhangelsk and works there in the procuracy. During SAKHOV's work in Leningrad in the opposition years, factional meetings were held at his apartment. SAKHOV is close to EVDOKIMOV. He visited ZINOVIEV, GERTIK, and KUKLIN; he visited me about twice during 1933–1934. Another participant in the underground was ROZNER, who worked in the planning department of the territorial executive committee. I remember him as an underground participant in 1929–1930.

The old oppositionist and participant in the Zinovievist underground Olga RAVICH is in Voronezh. I often met her at ZINOVIEV's apartment; she knew ZINOVIEV's true political attitudes more intimately than many of us around him and has influence over him.

Question: In your previous interrogation record of January 4, 1935, you testified about the composition of the Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground. Were there underground participants in Moscow besides the persons constituting the center?

Answer: Yes, in Moscow a number of old oppositionists belonging to the underground were connected with individual center members. These persons include GERTSBERG and his wife POZDEEVA, who were connected with GERTIK; GERTSBERG visited ZINOVIEV; PERIMOV, personally connected with ZINOVIEV until the very latest time, as he personally told me; FAIVILOVICH, connected with GERTIK at least until 1930; MAGYAR, a Comintern worker: I know from ZINOVIEV that he systematically met MAGYAR, whom he considered politically close to himself. To confirm his conclusions that affairs in the Comintern were bad, ZINOVIEV often cited MAGYAR as a source of information; moreover, MAGYAR met center member GORSHENIN until recently. Boris Lvovich BRAVO was very close to EVDOKIMOV until recently.

Question: What instructions were disseminated by participants in the Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground among your like-minded associates and accomplices?

Answer: First, we fed our like-minded associates slanderous, anti-Party information about the state of affairs in the Party, the Central Committee, the country, and the Comintern. This information was usually very abundant.

Second: by systematically exerting influence on our cadres, we educated them in a spirit of malice and hostility toward the existing leadership of the AUCP(b) and the Soviet government, particularly and especially toward Comrade STALIN; we kept harping on the idea that the aggravated difficulties supposedly existing in the country, the Party, and the Comintern largely resulted from ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's removal from leading work, and that one means of overcoming them was the return of our leaders, and hence ourselves, to the Party leadership.

Third, we pushed our like-minded associates toward selecting, preserving, and further strengthening ties among us; therefore we were always interested in the comrades' situations and attitudes, keeping track of where they were located.

We showed great interest in what was happening inside Leningrad's Party organizations and in the workers' districts.

We obtained this information from comrades visiting from the localities; GERTIK, KUKLIN, and others brought it from Leningrad. From other cities, respectively, those comrades, our like-minded associates, who lived there.

ZINOVIEV personally usually had detailed and extensive information about what was happening in the localities; this is explained by the fact that he personally received many people and accumulated all the information.

Question: What do you know about KHARITONOV's and GERTIK's role in the Zinovievist underground?

Answer: I know that KHARITONOV and GERTIK belonged to the Moscow center of the Zinovievist underground: KHARITONOV left the center shortly before going abroad, while GERTIK remained until recently, maintaining close relations with ZINOVIEV.

The record has been written correctly from my words and read by me.

BAKAEV.

INTERROGATED BY: ASSISTANT HEAD, EKO, GUGB, NKVD OF THE USSR, DMITRIEV

Certified correct:

RGASPI, fond 671, inventory 1, file 127, folios 3–12.

[1] This refers to Yusup Abdrakhmanovich Abdrakhmanov (1901–1938), a statesman and political figure of Kirghizia, chairman of the Council of People's Commissars of the Kirghiz ASSR, member of the Central Asian Bureau of the AUCP(b) Central Committee and of the USSR CEC. His diaries (published in 1991) contain entries on events of 1928–1932 (and also on his love affair with M. Ya. Natanson, whom he met during M. Natanson's exile in Tashkent). Yu. Abdrakhmanov was expelled from the Party on October 14, 1933, and arrested on April 4, 1937. He was sentenced to death by a traveling session of the Military Collegium of the USSR Supreme Court on November 6, 1937, and shot on November 7, 1937.
