INTERROGATION RECORD

Of G. I. SAFAROV, December 25, 1934.

In addition to the testimony I have given, I consider it necessary to add the following:

During his conversation with me in Ilyinskoye in the summer of 1932, ZINOVIEV strongly emphasized the deterioration of the workers' situation, particularly in Leningrad. “They have turned Leningrad into an insignificant provincial town,” he said. “Who is leading?” In doing so, he spoke venomously about KIROV and CHUDOV.

Even ZALUTSKY burst out concerning KIROV (the reference is to the libel reprinted by ZALUTSKY). “The workers do not react because a mass ‘peasantization’ of the working class is taking place. The old Petersburg workers, the experienced cadres, are said to be sidelined. Instead of political life in the factories, there is a din arranged by secretaries appointed from above and by Komsomol members. And these young newcomers to the factories from seven-year schools have barely begun at the machine. They are interested in the cinema, pleasures, parties, careers and advancement. They are bribed with life's comforts, promotion and higher education.” “But how can the country be industrialized without drawing new workers into industry alongside the old cadres?” I asked. “That is not the point,” ZINOVIEV continued. “The essence of the question is that the experienced worker has been pushed back both in production and in public life. On the other hand, nothing remains of Lenin's cooperative plan. Does this administrative collectivization resemble what LENIN had in mind? LENIN wrote about ten to twenty years of proper relations with the peasantry. What STALIN did in two years, LENIN expected to accomplish in ten or even more years.”

The thesis of the peasantization of the proletariat was also developed by SMILGA in a conversation I had with him in mid-1932, before meeting ZINOVIEV. SMILGA derived this thesis from the “inflated tempos,” which, he said, inevitably led to disruption of construction plans, since these tempos did not correspond to available resources, and threatened to nullify all economic management because they created inflation.

At one meeting in mid-1932 I had a conversation with MRACHKOVSKY that even led to a certain cooling of relations. In this conversation, speaking of the possibility of war, I pointed out that Trotsky's thesis concerning CLEMENCEAU had been buried by life: if there were war, regardless of anything else we would have to fight, giving unconditional support to the leadership, because it would be a war of socialism against capitalism. I advanced this as one argument in favor of immediately coming forward with a platform addressed to the Central Committee. MRACHKOVSKY replied literally as follows: “Do you think the people will fight? We will all go to hell with Stalin's collective farms. At my construction site in Kazakhstan, special settlers worked, and among them were quite a few participants in the civil war in the Urals. Some had even been commanders. That is who they drive off as settlers. They will not let anyone get away with it. Anger against Soviet power is boiling.”

In 1933 MAGYAR — by all indications, after a conversation with the Zinovievists or LOMINADZE — expressed the view that collective farms concealed a rebirth of classes under a socialist signboard. New class formation was occurring through the more “enterprising” collective-farm activists. The peasant, he said, would force his way to the market, since inflation would engulf economic life. Inflation was the favorite hobbyhorse of all counterrevolutionary conspiratorial gossip, “information” and arguments.

In mid-1932 MAGYAR told me that LOMINADZE had pointed out to him that, simultaneously with the liquidation of the old classes, a new bureaucratic stratum was growing and acquiring ever greater privileges, becoming the source and basis of a new class stratification. According to MAGYAR, LOMINADZE advocated a waiting position, arguing that new youth cadres must grow and settle, must “mature” in order to feel the need for definite ideological and political formation. LOMINADZE zealously protected his former Komsomol “cadre” and maintained constant contact with it, gathering not only his closest like-minded associates, such as SHATSKIN and CHAPLIN (constant information about the Tsentrosoyuz passed from CHAPLIN through LOMINADZE to MAGYAR), but also people personally devoted to him. A multilevel system of “transmission belts” developed in this grouping. I remember that after information in the Comintern about the exposure of the Right-Left bloc in 1931, MAZUT, now second secretary of the Magnitogorsk City Committee, told me: “As for Beso's double-dealing, that still needs checking. One cannot rely on what people say.”

The counterrevolutionary and antiparty groupings of Zinovievists, Trotskyists and so-called “Leftists” definitely propagated the idea of the degeneration of the working class in the socialist country, striving thereby to explain the complete absence of support for their counterrevolutionary, antiparty views among proletarian party members and in the proletarian milieu generally. The counterrevolutionary degenerates sought to justify and conceal their degeneration through monstrous slander of the working class, the party and its leadership. They hid their defense of the remnants of the class enemy being finished off (this applies, of course, to me as well) behind slanderous phrases about a rupture between the working class and the peasantry. The alienness and unconditional hostility of these groupings toward the working class and its party found expression throughout this system of counterrevolutionary views: the proletariat had become peasantized, while the bureaucracy, riding on its back, pursued a course of rupture with the peasantry. From this organically arose the atmosphere of internal emigration and its political philosophy: a “chosen, enterprising minority” “suffers and fights for masses that have lost class consciousness and organization.” This political philosophy of internal emigration was bound to nourish, and did nourish, sentiments favoring counterrevolutionary banditry and terrorism. A purulent growth fastened onto the great Communist Party like a hidden parasite, reviving in the struggle against the party and Soviet power the philosophy and psychology of White Socialist-Revolutionarism. The participants in the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc and their sympathizers bear the full direct political responsibility for S. M. KIROV's murder. Not only the systematic and persistent incitement against party leaders through rumors, gossip and slander of every kind, but the entire system of political views, matured into a fascist–Socialist-Revolutionary form, led toward finding release in the most abominable murder. —

Written in my own hand. —

G. SAFAROV
