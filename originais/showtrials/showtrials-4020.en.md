INTERROGATION RECORD

OF ZINOVIEV Grigory Yevseyevich.

January 3, 1935.

Question: During your interrogation of December 22, 1934, you testified that after the Fifteenth Congress of the VKP(b), an underground organization of the so-called Zinovievists existed in Leningrad.

Who headed this organization, who directed it, and until when did it exist?

Answer: In 1928, the underground organization of the so-called Zinovievists in Leningrad was still sufficiently well formed and strong; in 1929, elements of an underground organization existed. The leaders of this underground through 1929 inclusive were LEVIN V., MOROZOV, SHURYGIN, RUMYANTSEV V., NALIVAIKO (Rumyantsev represented the group of “Leftists”; Nalivaiko partly shared the same sentiments), and others whose surnames I do not remember.

During the period I have indicated, that is, through 1929 inclusive, the underground leaders maintained contact with me and received guiding instructions from me.

Besides the organization I have named (and in 1929, elements of an organization), there was also an underground group in Leningrad headed by YAKOVLEV Moisei. Until 1928, YAKOVLEV Moisei played a very large role in Leningrad opposition circles, effectively performing the role of secretary of the so-called Zinovievist organization. After that time, YAKOVLEV's group existed as a formally constituted organizational unit until approximately the beginning of 1929.

YAKOVLEV M.'s main connections were concentrated in the Vasileostrovsky district. After 1929, the underground of the so-called Zinovievists in Leningrad ceased to exist.

Question: On what grounds do you maintain that the underground organization of the so-called Zinovievists, or the underground, as you call it, ceased to exist in Leningrad after 1929?

Answer: I considered that after 1929 the underground of the so-called Zinovievists in Leningrad no longer existed because by that time my connections with the organization or elements of the organization had ceased, people from there stopped visiting me, I received no information about underground work, and, moreover, the entire political situation at that time dictated the necessity of ending all organizational struggle against the party.

Question: And what measures did you take, as organizer and leader of the underground organization, to liquidate it in order to end the struggle against the party, as you testify?

Answer: My grave guilt before the party and Soviet power consists in the fact that, as leader of the underground organization in 1927 and 1928 and of the underground of the so-called Zinovievists remaining in 1929 in the form of elements of an organization, I did not inform party or state bodies of the underground's existence and thereby did not put an end to it. However, I then considered that the disintegration of organizational connections among its members meant the liquidation of the organization itself and of the underground of the so-called Zinovievists in general.

Question: During the previous interrogation, when the investigation asked whether you confirmed GORSHENIN's testimony that at the end of 1928 you told him that an underground organization of the so-called Zinovievists headed by LEVIN and RUMYANTSEV existed in Leningrad, you replied that you could not confirm it. Yet during your interrogation today, January 2, 1935, you testified not only to the existence of an underground organization in Leningrad in 1928, but also that an underground of the so-called Zinovievists existed in 1929.

How can you explain to the investigation the contradictory nature of your testimony?

Answer: I am answering before the investigation for an extended period of time, and therefore may allow individual inaccuracies in my testimony or forget individual facts. I attribute this particular fact to such cases, although I do not remember the conversation with GORSHENIN to which the investigation refers.

Question: KHARITONOV M.'s testimony has established that in 1929 and 1930 you told him that an underground organization of the so-called Zinovievists continued to exist in Leningrad, naming LEVIN V. and RUMYANTSEV V. among its leaders. Do you confirm this?

Answer: I admit that in 1929 I told KHARITONOV that elements of an underground organization existed in Leningrad, as I have already testified today, and that among the leaders I may have named LEVIN and RUMYANTSEV to him. However, I deny such a conversation with KHARITONOV in 1930, since by then, as I testified above, the underground no longer existed.

Question: In 1928, you met the former leader of the Right, BUKHARIN, in an attempt at collusion between the Right and yourself. Give the investigation precise explanations of when, where, in whose presence and on whose behalf BUKHARIN conducted negotiations with you.

Answer: In 1928, I was summoned from Kaluga to the CC for talks about my future work; Comrade MOLOTOV, then working at the CC of the VKP(b), summoned me. My arrival in Moscow coincided with the work of the Sixth Congress of the ECCI \[1\]. On the day I arrived or the following day, BUKHARIN called at my apartment in the evening and took me to his apartment in the Kremlin. Our conversation lasted a very long time; in any event, I stayed at his home until morning. There was nobody else at BUKHARIN's besides myself. BUKHARIN telephoned RYKOV to ask him to come over, but for some reason he did not come. The main political thrust of BUKHARIN's conversation with me, as in his approach to KAMENEV, consisted in proposing a bloc between us, the so-called Zinovievists, and the Right. Although BUKHARIN did not formally say on whose behalf he was negotiating with me, it was understood that he was speaking on behalf of the Right. BUKHARIN told me approximately the following: “You [Zinovievists] are now in the best position. Everyone will court you. We [the Right] have already approached you. You will be summoned to the CC, drawn into work and offered participation in the struggle against the Right. Do not agree to it. In any event, wait some time.” Here BUKHARIN reminded me how he had once warned me against taking extreme measures in the struggle against TROTSKY, now directing this argument against Comrade STALIN. Both he and I abused Comrade STALIN in every way, accusing him of being to blame for everything. BUKHARIN went on to say that the accusation that he, TOMSKY and other\<s\> were Rightists was invented, since, in particular, he, BUKHARIN, was not at all a Rightist or a supporter of the kulak, but considered that the peasantry had to be approached more cautiously; he complained that a line was being pursued to isolate him in the ECCI and slander him before delegations of fraternal Communist parties, but that he had great support among German conciliators, Italians and other\<s\>, and had his own men in the ECCI apparatus, specifically naming Humbert-Droz; he complained that he was being removed from work at “Pravda.”

During the conversation, BUKHARIN told me that fifteen or sixteen CC members were ready to support them, the Rightists, but I think he did not name them.

Question: What did you answer to BUKHARIN's proposal?

Answer: I reproached him for supporting, in the struggle against us, what I then regarded as the wholly incorrect theory of the possibility of the victory of socialism in one country; I attacked his group of young disciples for their Rightist views, calling them “Struve's brood.” BUKHARIN told me that he did not defend the theory of the victory of socialism in one country in the form advanced by Comrade STALIN, that he defended only the proposition that our revolution was socialist in character and that he had supposedly smuggled these views into the Comintern program. His statement about the theory of building socialism in one country was intended to smooth over disagreements and make a concession to us. Regarding his little “school” of young people, he told me that I was unfair to them, that of course individual mistakes had occurred, but that they were talented young people and should not be attacked.

The essential meaning of my answer to BUKHARIN was that there could be no political unity between myself and my like-minded comrades and the Rightists, although there was no disagreement between him and me in our hostile assessment of Comrade Stalin's role.

Question: Did you report this conversation with BUKHARIN to KAMENEV? When, where and in what form?

Answer: After leaving Moscow, as soon as I arrived in Kaluga, I reported my meeting with BUKHARIN to KAMENEV in the greatest detail.

Question: How did KAMENEV react to this conversation with BUKHARIN?

Answer: KAMENEV agreed with me that we should not enter a bloc with the Rightists, although he considered that contact with them should nevertheless be maintained because they could supply us with valuable political information.

Question: When and to which of your like-minded comrades did you report your negotiations with BUKHARIN?

Answer: I spoke to many of my like-minded comrades who visited Kaluga during that period, probably including EVDOKIMOV, BAKAYEV, SHAROV, FYODOROV, ZALUTSKY and others whose surnames I do not remember.

Question: Did you inform party bodies of the fact of your negotiations with the Rightists?

Answer: No, I concealed it from the party and have now informed the investigation of it.

Question: Inform the investigation about KAMENEV's meeting with former leaders of the Right in 1929: where and in whose presence did it take place, and what did their negotiations consist of?

Answer: From KAMENEV's words, I know that either in the autumn or winter of 1929, at TOMSKY's or SHMIDT's dacha, KAMENEV met former leaders of the Right. Those present were TOMSKY, BUKHARIN or RYKOV (possibly both BUKHARIN and RYKOV), and SHMIDT V. These negotiations took place on the eve of either a party conference or a plenum of the CC of the VKP(b). During this conversation, the Rightists voiced their grievances against the party leadership, saying that they were being oppressed and so forth. They told KAMENEV that some members of the CC of the VKP(b) supported them. The main point of the conversation was that they were effectively asking KAMENEV's advice on what impression would be made by a step such as their submitting a collective resignation—that is, BUKHARIN, RYKOV and TOMSKY.

They approached KAMENEV as an experienced politician representing the group of so-called Zinovievists.

Question: How did KAMENEV react to the statement of the former leaders of the Right?

Answer: I do not remember, but evidently he opposed collective resignation and proposed that they capitulate, since both he and I considered this the only way for the Rightists to preserve themselves in the party.

Question: Describe in detail your meeting with TOMSKY in 1929 in Sochi and the content of it.

Answer: In 1929, when I was living in Sochi at the dacha of the Union Central Executive Committee, TOMSKY and FYODOROV G. were also there. At that time, I had a long conversation with TOMSKY, in which he gave the most pessimistic assessment of the Rightists' situation. He told me that the Rightists' fate was already decided, that they would be removed from leadership work. TOMSKY also said that the trade unions did not in fact exist. He reproached me for attacking the Rightists in individual articles of mine that were occasionally published, saying that all my efforts were futile, that we, the so-called Zinovievists, were finished men in any case, and that one should keep silent, lie low and await a suitable time. This last remark meant that the future would show whether the line of the CC of the VKP(b) would prove justified.

Question: Was FYODOROV present during these conversations, and did he take part in them?

Answer: I do not remember whether FYODOROV was present during this conversation, but he knew of all my conversations with TOMSKY.

Question: Did you agree with TOMSKY to establish contact with him, and through whom?

Answer: I do not remember exactly, but I think we agreed to maintain contact in the future; since FYODOROV was very close to TOMSKY, I admit that he was to maintain this contact with him. However, as far as I remember, no connections followed.

Question: Describe your meeting with TOMSKY in 1932.

Answer: On the eve of my departure for exile in Kustanai in 1932, after my conviction, I called on TOMSKY at his OGIZ office on business concerning my literary work. We had a conversation there. TOMSKY assessed the countryside's situation in the darkest tones, saying that the countryside was being ruined by the ongoing collectivization, that in the villages he had visited half the cottages were depopulated, that life itself had shown that the Rightists' policy was justified and that the sum of all the CC's measures would inevitably fail. In response, I told him that in my opinion retreat from collectivization policy was inadmissible and impossible; however, I expressed great fears for the country's condition in connection with the situation in the countryside. In particular, I expressed to TOMSKY my doubts about the correctness of the law of August 7 on protecting socialist property, and the advisability of introducing political departments at the MTS, a measure that in my opinion smacked of War Communism and was of little practical value. We reached no definite conclusions, but both considered that the future would show whether the line of the CC of the VKP(b) had been correct.

Question: Did you tell KAMENEV of your meeting with TOMSKY in 1932, and how did he react?

Answer: KAMENEV left for exile in Minusinsk considerably earlier than my departure for Kustanai, so it is possible that I did not report the conversation with TOMSKY to him, although I do not rule out that I may have spoken to him about it.

Question: To which of your like-minded comrades, when and where did you speak of your meeting with TOMSKY?

Answer: I admit that I may have spoken to EVDOKIMOV about my conversation with TOMSKY.

Question: SAFAROV's testimony has established that in 1932 you attempted to reach agreement with other anti-party groups to organize a bloc for struggle against the party leadership.

Tell us exactly with whom and what specific negotiations you conducted in this direction.

Answer: The difficulties of 1932, the vacillations of individual elements in the party from the remnants of former opposition groups, the recurrence of anti-party views among these elements, rumors of anti-party sentiments among persons who had previously belonged to no opposition, particularly in the Institute of Red Professors (I think STEN named DZENIS), among supposedly old Bolsheviks—I remember SOLTS being mentioned—and talk of disagreements in the Ukrainian top leadership: all this intensified my vacillations and created a basis for reviving the remnants of various anti-party groups. At this time, various anti-party groups attempted political collusion in order to organize a bloc for struggle against the party leadership. A number of steps were taken in this direction: 1) STEN contacted me and conveyed a platform from the Ryutinists for information and probing; 2) KAMENEV met SHATSK\<IN\> and LOMINADZE on the latter's initiative at the end of summer 1932 in Gagra, where he had a conversation with them—that is, SHATSK\<IN\> and LOMINADZE—in which they essentially sought a bloc with us, the so-called Zinovievists, on the basis of struggle against the party and Comrade Stalin. Their search for a bloc was veiled by proposals for mutual contact and exchange of political information. KAMENEV, in his words, refused; 3) the Trotskyists, in the person of I.N. SMIRNOV, contacted EVDOKIMOV. SMIRNOV met EVDOKIMOV at the Sovnarkom dining room and once, having expressed dissatisfaction with the situation in the country and inside the party, proposed that, to clarify their respective viewpoints on the questions raised, EVDOKIMOV meet him, SMIRNOV, MRACHKOVSKY and, I think, VAGANYAN \[2\]. EVDOKIMOV, having told me all this, asked what he should do. I opposed contact with SMIRNOV and other\<s\>, since, although I had anti-party sentiments, I was against struggle; 4) my meeting with SAFAROV was also a direct attempt to draw closer to him. During this meeting in the summer of 1932 at my dacha in Ilyinskoye, SAFAROV and I exchanged our anti-party sentiments concerning the situation in the country.

SAFAROV also spoke to me negatively about the Comintern's work. I also admit that he spoke to me of the need to send a collective letter to the CC of the VKP(b) about the situation in the country and party. The following circumstances gave various anti-party groups reason to seek rapprochement with me and attempt to forge around me a bloc of anti-party groups: a) my vacillations and anti-party sentiments and those of my closest like-minded comrades; b) the certainty of representatives of anti-party groups that we would not betray them to the party; c) anti-party groups saw in KAMENEV and me people with a political name who could head the struggle against the party leadership.

Question: To which of your like-minded comrades did you describe your own or their negotiations with representatives of various anti-party groups about forming an anti-party bloc, and how did they react?

Answer: KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV and, partly (about STEN's approach), BAKAYEV and KUKLIN knew of the negotiations. In particular, EVDOKIMOV also participated in my rapprochement with SAFAROV. Our general mood was to hear the sentiments of various anti-party groups, keep abreast of them, wait and see, without establishing organizational connections with them.

Question: What form did your connection with MAGYAR, a Comintern employee, take?

Answer: MAGYAR visited me fairly often. During the period of official existence of the opposition group of so-called Zinovievists, he sympathized with us; afterward, he was a concealed oppositionist. Since MAGYAR has extensive connections in the Comintern, “Pravda,” where he previously worked, and among various responsible party members, he was always informed about different questions and sentiments. I was interested in these connections and his information.

Question: What information did MAGYAR give you, in particular about the Comintern's work and the situation of Communist parties in various countries, and under what circumstances?

Answer: MAGYAR gave me information mainly about the work of the Comintern and Communist parties in other countries, which was mostly secret. I recall that he told me about alleged differences between PYATNITSKY and MANUILSKY over united-front tactics; that DIMITROV was being considered for ECCI secretary and that MANUILSKY was therefore seeking rapprochement with him against PYATNITSKY; that recently, at a meeting of the Politburo of the CC of the VKP(b), the ECCI leadership had been reprimanded for a submitted draft appeal, I think in connection with the last anniversary of the October celebrations, which allegedly contained a number of errors on elementary questions; he spoke of disagreements in the top leadership of the German Communist Party, among which there was great dissatisfaction with PIECK's leadership, and that consequently comrades arriving for instructions did not receive them. He conveyed all this information to me during 1934. After my removal from work at the journal “Bolshevik,” MAGYAR told me that Comrade ORDZHONIKIDZE, meeting Comrade STALIN while on leave, allegedly asked him whether my removal from work at “Bolshevik” meant my political destruction, to which Comrade STALIN answered in the negative, saying that I would be given other work. MAGYAR said that he had learned of this conversation from one of Comrade ORDZHONIKIDZE's assistants—KRUGLIKOV.

MAGYAR was very close to LOMINADZE.

Question: What assignments did you give MAGYAR to establish connections with representatives of various anti-party groups, when and what exactly?

Answer: I regarded MAGYAR as a source of information and never gave him any assignments concerning contact with representatives of anti-party groups.

Question: SAFAROV's testimony has established that MAGYAR played a large role in forging a bloc of various anti-party groups in 1932, carrying out your direct assignments in that direction. In particular, according to SAFAROV's testimony, MAGYAR arranged his meeting with you in 1932. What can you testify on this question?

Answer: I gave MAGYAR no assignments to contact representatives of anti-party groups. Regarding the meeting with SAFAROV, I admit that MAGYAR may have proposed it to me. In that same period, I wished to meet LOMINADZE, but tried to do so not through MAGYAR but through STEN. By chance, the meeting with LOMINADZE did not take place; I do not now remember the specific reasons that prevented it.

Question: In what form did you raise the question of your attitude toward UGLANOV and other Rightists in your conversation with SAFAROV in 1932?

Answer: I admit that UGLANOV may also have been touched upon in the conversation with SAFAROV; if the conversation with SAFAROV relates to the period when STEN conveyed the Ryutin platform to me, then the question of UGLANOV may have been raised in connection with STEN telling me that the platform probably came from Uglanov's district officials. The conversation with SAFAROV relates to this period. I had the most negative attitude toward UGLANOV and could only have assessed him accordingly.

Question: What did SAFAROV tell you during his meeting with you in 1932 about his negotiations with SHLYAPNIKOV?

Answer: I admit that SAFAROV may have spoken to me about SHLYAPNIKOV, but I do not remember what exactly.

Question: SAFAROV's testimony has established that during his meeting with you in 1932 he told you of his negotiations with SHLYAPNIKOV and of the latter's statement that they, that is, the so-called Shlyapnikovists, stood on the positions of the old anti-party platform and assessed the situation as disastrous for the party. Expressing sympathy for SHLYAPNIKOV, you said he was a valuable man and a Bolshevik to the core, and accused the party leadership of hounding him.

Do you confirm this?

Answer: I repeat that I admit a conversation with SAFAROV about SHLYAPNIKOV. I can say that he, SHLYAPNIKOV, is a valuable man, that Ilyich valued him at a certain period; but I could not have said that the party leadership was hounding him, since I considered the party's repressive measures toward him correct and prompted by his conduct.

Question: LEVIN V.'s testimony has established that during a meeting with him in 1932 you told him of attempts by representatives of various anti-party groups to reach agreement with you. What can you say about this?

Answer: As far as I remember, the meeting with LEVIN occurred before I received the Ryutin platform from STEN and also before my meeting with SAFAROV. However, I admit that the meeting may have taken place after the facts I have indicated. In the latter case: a) when telling him (Levin) about the Ryutin platform, I must have expressed my negative attitude toward it; b) when telling him about STEN and SAFAROV, I may have connected this with attempts by representatives of anti-party groups at political collusion.

G. ZINOVIEV

INTERROGATED BY: DEPUTY HEAD OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD OF THE USSR—LYUSHKOV.

Certified correct: Biryukov
