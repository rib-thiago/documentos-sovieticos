INTERROGATION RECORD

Of GESSEN, Sergei Mikhailovich, December 22, 1934.

QUESTION: EVDOKIMOV's testimony of December 19 of this year has established that in early 1929 you, together with KOTOLYNOV and others, raised with him the question of creating a center of the Zinovievist organization in Leningrad. What testimony can you give on this question?

ANSWER: At the end of January 1929, when leaving Moscow for a few days in Leningrad, I received an assignment from ZINOVIEV to prepare the creation of a local Zinovievist center in Leningrad, with work divided by districts and representatives of different strata of the former Zinovievist organization included in the center, that is, both supporters of the so-called group of twenty-three and “Leftists.” This conversation in Moscow took place between ZINOVIEV and me alone. In Leningrad, at EVDOKIMOV's apartment, a conference was held in late January 1929 attended, as far as I remember, by Vladimir RUMYANTSEV, Vladimir LEVIN and others whose surnames I cannot now recall. In discussing the question of creating a center, disagreements arose before EVDOKIMOV's arrival over its composition (I think over the number of seats for the “Leftists”).

When EVDOKIMOV arrived he opposed creating any center at all. Because of these disagreements the question was not decided on the spot but referred to Moscow for decision. In Moscow I reported everything set out above at a meeting of the leading Zinovievist group at ZINOVIEV's apartment. Of those assembled I now remember ZINOVIEV and the chief opponents of creating a Leningrad center: KHARITONOV, SHAROV and BAKAEV. The motive for opposing the creation of a center in Leningrad was fear of some exposure, even partial, resulting in everyone's expulsion from the Party and the disruption of the planned tactical line. It was decided not to create a center and to limit ourselves to maintaining personal connections as they developed on their own, that is, on the basis of old acquaintances from anti-Party work.

Subsequently the question of creating a formally constituted organizational center in Leningrad was never raised again in the Moscow political center of the Zinovievists. Besides the narrowly conspiratorial consideration indicated, other considerations were added.

Since the moment of possible political action was being postponed indefinitely, it was considered obvious that major personal reshufflings were inevitable and that the composition of those struggling would be far from identical to that of 1927–1928. I remember, for example, ZINOVIEV saying in 1932: “The composition of the people will, of course, change considerably. We must see clearly that to attract people STALIN has not only prisons and material power but something stronger: the keys to socialist construction.” It was assumed that if conditions favorable for action arose, the elements inclined to struggle would quickly find one another.

QUESTION: Clarify your testimony concerning your conversation with ZINOVIEV in 1932 about possible action by the Zinovievists. In connection with what did this conversation arise, and what action did ZINOVIEV have in mind?

ANSWER: In the second half of 1932, as I have already testified, in connection with a general revival of the activities of the entire anti-Party underground, it was considered that conditions might mature for political action by the Zinovievists. It was in this connection that my conversation with ZINOVIEV quoted above arose.

QUESTION: In what forms was the Zinovievists' action envisaged in 1932?

ANSWER: Action was envisaged in some form of discussion within the Party.

The question of forms, timing and so forth was not discussed in greater detail, and the very possibility of action was not yet considered definitely established; the situation was regarded as uncertain.

QUESTION: Who belonged to the political center of the Zinovievists in Moscow?

ANSWER: The political center was in practice composed of former members of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and of the Bureau of the Leningrad Provincial Committee from earlier memberships, before the Fourteenth Party Congress. To discuss the most important political questions, the following persons gathered at ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's in various combinations: ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, ZALUTSKY (until the end of 1929 or 1930), KHARITONOV, KUKLIN, FEDOROV, less frequently SHAROV, and REINGOLD (until 1929).

During visits to Moscow I too attended these conferences.

In addition, former IKP members—GORSHENIN, KAPITONOV (I think until 1931), KAREV [1]—as well as GERTIK frequently participated in discussing questions.

In 1929–1931 NAUMOV often visited ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV.

QUESTION: The investigation has information that an organizational connection existed until recently between the Moscow political center and the Leningrad center of the Zinovievists. What do you know on this question?

ANSWER: I know of no formally constituted organizational center in Leningrad.

QUESTION: Vladimir LEVIN's testimony has established that during the period of acute struggle by the Rightists against the Party you came to Leningrad with directives from ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV to establish a bloc with the Rightists. Do you confirm this testimony?

ANSWER: LEVIN's testimony is mistaken. The question was considerably more complex. In late 1928 and early 1929 fairly substantial disagreements emerged within the Zinovievist group, and two political tendencies took shape, one represented by ZINOVIEV and the other by KAMENEV.

KAMENEV's position tended toward a bloc with the Rightists and unconditionally rejected steps that might disrupt this bloc.

ZINOVIEV's position, while permitting informational conversations with the Rightists, was oriented toward the possibility that acute disagreements within the Politburo and Central Committee might create conditions for an acceptable agreement with the “STALIN group,” as we then called the Party leadership.

I remember three circumstances in connection with these disagreements:

1. At the end of December 1928 or beginning of January 1929, as I was told by KHARITONOV, I think, though I do not remember exactly, KAMENEV met the Rightists, apparently TOMSKY and RYKOV (approximately six months after the well-known meeting with BUKHARIN recorded by KAMENEV).

According to the person who told me, ZINOVIEV opposed this meeting.

I do not remember other details, since I was then ill in bed.

2. In January or February 1929, after my arrival from Leningrad, a relatively broad meeting of fifteen to twenty people was held at ZINOVIEV's apartment, devoted to the same disagreements. As far as I remember, this meeting came as a surprise to KAMENEV. The immediate subject discussed was whether ZINOVIEV should seek to speak at the Moscow Party Conference due to take place in the near future. This question was closely connected with the question of relations with the Rightists, since speaking against them at the conference would make the path to agreement more difficult. KAMENEV opposed speaking, proceeding from the view that removing the Rightists from the Central Committee Politburo would mean removing the last members of the Politburo from Lenin's time except STALIN; that “we have nothing to rejoice about here”; that ZINOVIEV would be unable to take any independent position at the conference and would be forced to repeat what MOLOTOV and the others said. ZINOVIEV insisted on speaking.

At the meeting, as far as I remember, REINGOLD and FEDOROV leaned toward KAMENEV's position. I also remember that KHARITONOV and I spoke for ZINOVIEV's position.

3. Soon after this meeting ZINOVIEV wrote a document read within a narrow circle of Zinovievists and informally known as “Yuzus” (an instruction—rules of conduct). This document set out the positions to be taken toward the “Stalin group” and the Rightists, and rejected the possibility of agreement with the latter.

Under these conditions I could not have raised in Leningrad the question of a bloc with the Rightists, both because the question itself caused disagreements within the Moscow center of the Zinovievists and because I personally opposed such a bloc.

QUESTION: In the record of your testimony taken on December 15 you testified about your connections with SHATSKIN and LOMINADZE in 1930–1931.

What do you know about the Zinovievist center's relations with the “Leftists” and about proposals by the Zinovievists to unite with the “Leftists” for joint struggle against the Party?

ANSWER: First of all I must correct an inaccuracy that crept into my testimony. I met LOMINADZE and SHATSKIN several times not in 1930 but in 1929. In 1930, when LOMINADZE was in Transcaucasia, I did not see him.

The Zinovievist group regarded the “Leftists” as a later and politically less clearly defined expression of the same sentiments that characterized the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc. It was considered beyond doubt that in its further development this group would come fully to the views of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. In 1932 contact with the “Leftists” was maintained, as I have already said, chiefly through STEN, who often visited ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. However, there was no hurry to formalize the rapprochement. In 1932 ZINOVIEV said: “There is no need to hurry with this. At the necessary moment all the ‘Leftist’ groups and little groups within the Party will find one another.”

QUESTION: The investigation has established that ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV and the other members of the Zinovievist political center were hostile toward Party policy and the Party leadership. What do you know on this question?

ANSWER: The leadership's tactics in Comintern matters were subjected to the sharpest attacks by ZINOVIEV and the other members of the center. In 1932 ZINOVIEV said that the Party leadership underestimated the Hitlerite danger, sharply criticized the tactics of the German Communist Party, particularly the red plebiscite (August 1932), considering that these tactics facilitated the fascists' victory. ZINOVIEV maintained that the Party leadership did not know how to apply the united-front tactic and was pursuing a disastrous policy of splitting the trade-union movement in a number of countries. In connection with this last question, the splitting of trade unions, ZINOVIEV once said in 1931: “This is a kind of sabotage in the Comintern.” It went without saying that responsibility for the erroneous leadership of the Comintern was laid on the leadership of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), and first and foremost on Comrade STALIN.

ZINOVIEV also sharply attacked the Party leadership for not convening a Comintern congress for a number of years. On domestic policy questions ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV and others considered that industrialization was being conducted without calculation and with mismanagement, without a rigorously thought-out plan, resulting in masses of unproductive expenditures and immobilized capital; that industrialization had not led to a significant improvement in the working class's position; and that the backward material and living conditions of the workers were one factor delaying the maturation of revolutionary movements in the West. The Ivanovo events in the spring of 1932 attracted considerable attention from the Zinovievist group as an indication of the difficult material position of substantial strata of the working masses and as evidence of mass dissatisfaction among workers with their material position.

I shall give supplementary testimony on the remaining questions.

I have read the record. Recorded correctly from my words.

S. GESSEN
