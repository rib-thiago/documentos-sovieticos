## INDICTMENT

in the case of NIKOLAEV L. V., KOTOLYNOV N. I., MYASNIKOV N. P., SHATSKY N. N., MANDELSHTAM S. O., SOKOLOV G. V., ZVEZDOV V. I., YUSKIN I. G., RUMYANTSEV V. V., ANTONOV V. S., KHANIK L. O., TOLMAZOV A. I., LEVIN V. S., and SOSITSKY L. I., CHARGED WITH CRIMES UNDER ARTICLES 58-8 AND 58-11 OF THE RSFSR CRIMINAL CODE.

On December 1, 1934, at 4:30 p.m., in the city of Leningrad, at Smolny, Comrade Sergey Mironovich KIROV, a member of the Presidium of the Central Executive Committee of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, a member of the Politburo of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), and secretary of the Party's Central and Leningrad Committees, was killed by a revolver shot.

The murderer detained at the scene of the crime proved to be NIKOLAEV Leonid Vasilyevich, aged thirty, an employee expelled from the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in March 1934 for violating Party discipline, but subsequently reinstated in his rights as a Party member in view of his statement of repentance.

The investigation conducted in this case established that NIKOLAEV L. V. belonged to an underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group formed from members of the former Zinovievist anti-Soviet group in Leningrad, and that the murder of Comrade Kirov S. M. was committed by Nikolaev L. V. on the instructions of the underground terrorist “Leningrad center.”

Formation of the counterrevolutionary terrorist

underground group in the city of Leningrad

The investigation established that, despite the capitulation of the former Zinovievist anti-Soviet group, the underground work of the most active members of that bloc did not cease and continued until the most recent times.

This activity intensified particularly in 1933–1934, when an underground counterrevolutionary terrorist anti-Soviet group, headed by the so-called “Leningrad center,” formed in Leningrad from former members of the Zinovievist group.

On the emergence and organizational formation of this group, the accused Zvezdov testified:

“…The organizational formation of our group proceeded gradually, with Rumyantsev Vladimir and Kotolynov Ivan acting as the principal organizers…” (vol. 1, case leaf 196).

The accused Sokolov G. V., in his testimony of December 7, 9, and 12 of this year, also confirmed these circumstances, emphasizing that:

“…Beginning in October 1934, the persons belonging to this group began strengthening their connections and establishing closer contact with one another…

The group was essentially united on the basis of the old Zinovievist platform…” (vol. 1, case leaf 249).

An even fuller picture of the organizational formation of this underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group was given in the testimony of the accused Khanik L. O., who confirmed that he —

“…did indeed belong to this group, formed, as he put it, on the basis of the former Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc…

Our organization took its final shape in 1933…” (vol. 2, case leaf 33).

As established by the investigation, this group was headed by the underground terrorist “Leningrad center,” consisting of Kotolynov I. I., Shatsky N. N., Rumyantsev V. V., Mandelshtam S. O., Myasnikov N. P., Levin V. S., Sositsky L. I., and Nikolaev L. V. — all former members of the Zinovievist opposition.

This is confirmed both by the testimony of the members of this center themselves and by that of other members of the Leningrad underground anti-Soviet group.

Thus, the accused Rumyantsev V. V. testified:

“…Levin Vladimir, I — Rumyantsev — Mandelshtam Sergey, Kotolynov Ivan, Myasnikov Nikolai, and Sositsky Lev belonged to the center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization in Leningrad. For reasons of secrecy, the center's members very rarely all met together; contact was ordinarily maintained personally between individual members of the center…” (vol. 2, case leaf 3).

On the same question, the accused Mandelshtam S. O. testified:

“…The formation of the Leningrad center of our organization dates from the same time; it consisted of myself — Mandelshtam — Rumyantsev Vladimir, Levin Vladimir, and Sositsky, and continues to exist in the same form to this day…” (vol. 2, case leaf 250).

The accused Myasnikov, questioned about the composition of the “Leningrad center,” testified that this center included Levin V. S., Rumyantsev V. V., Kotolynov I. I., Sositsky, and himself (vol. 2, case leaf 108).

In the same testimony, Myasnikov also described the distribution of functions among individual members of this center.

The accused Khanik also speaks of the leading role of Rumyantsev, Levin, and Kotolynov in the underground anti-Soviet Zinovievist group (vol. 2, case leaves 33–34).

The murderer of Comrade S. M. Kirov — L. V. Nikolaev — also belonged to this same “Leningrad center” of the underground anti-Soviet terrorist group.

On the accused Nikolaev's participation in this group, the accused Zvezdov testified:

“…He (that is, Nikolaev Leonid) had been connected with us for a long time, beginning in 1924. For a time he worked under the direct supervision of Kotolynov, when the latter was secretary of the Vyborg District Komsomol Committee. Nikolaev was educated by us, nourished on the ideological sap of our group, and undoubtedly absorbed all the experience of our factional struggle against the Party and the Party leadership.

It should further be said of Nikolaev that he belongs to the category of reserved people who are nevertheless ready for decisive action…” (vol. 1, case leaf 199).

The accused Sokolov, Antonov, Kotolynov, Khanik, and Shatsky also testified to Nikolaev's participation in the underground anti-Soviet group of former Zinovievists.

Nikolaev L. V. himself, in his testimony of December 13 of this year, confirmed that he belonged to a group of former oppositionists consisting of Kotolynov, Shatsky, Yuskin, and others, which carried on counterrevolutionary work, adding:

“…The group's members stood on the platform of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc. They considered it necessary to replace the existing Party leadership by every possible means…” (vol. 1, case leaf 266).

As established by the investigation, all the work of the underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group was conducted under conditions of strict secrecy. Literally all the members of this group charged in the present case speak of the strictly clandestine nature of relations within it. In particular, the accused Khanik testified:

“…For reasons of secrecy, only members of the center possessed information about the organization's structure. However, from what Rumyantsev told me, I know that our organization had a number of cells situated in places where young people congregated…” (vol. 2, case leaf 35).

The accused Myasnikov, one of the leaders of the Leningrad group, also speaks in his testimony of the secrecy of the underground counterrevolutionary group. On his instructions, a complete archive of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist opposition was seized at an apartment belonging to one of the group's members.

Aims and methods of the underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group

The preliminary investigation established that the principal objective of the underground anti-Soviet terrorist group was to seek to disorganize the leadership of the Soviet Government through terrorist attacks directed against the chief leaders of Soviet power, and thereby bring about a change in current policy in the spirit of the so-called Zinovievist-Trotskyist platform.

Having lost all hope of support from the masses and constituting a closed, politically doomed anti-Soviet group, its members, seeing no prospect of attaining their aims, embarked on the path of direct terrorism.

The accused Khanik, one of the group's active members, characterizing its “ideological and political” positions, acknowledged that they derived —

“from the platform of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc aimed at undermining the authority of the existing Party leadership and replacing it with the leaders of our organization — Zinoviev, Kamenev, and others — who favor changing the course currently pursued by the Party…” (vol. 2, case leaf 35).

The accused Khanik further testified:

“…The leaders of our organization constantly pointed out that all evil came from the present Party leadership — Stalin, Molotov, Kaganovich, and Kirov. In this connection, our organization's leaders instilled in us, especially the so-called youth, the view that difficulties in the country would continue (achievements were not taken into account) as long as the present leadership headed the Party.

From these counterrevolutionary positions and convictions, the terrorist attack committed against Kirov by our organization's member Nikolaev Leonid became possible…” (vol. 2, case leaf 35).

The accused Rumyantsev describes the sentiments prevailing in this group in similar terms; according to the description given by another accused, Tolmazov, he himself was “the most unequivocal advocate of terrorist methods of action.”

The accused Rumyantsev acknowledged that —

“in such an inflamed atmosphere of intense hatred toward the leadership of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), sentiments of a terrorist nature could not but grow…” (vol. 2, case leaf 9).

“…The direct outcome of such sentiments,” testified the accused Tolmazov, “was precisely the shot fired by Nikolaev Leonid at Kirov…”

The accused Nikolaev L. V. also gave exhaustive testimony about the terrorist methods of struggle adopted by this underground anti-Soviet group:

“…We could not,” he testified, “expect a change in the Party leadership through methods of internal Party democracy. We realized that this path was entirely excluded. Hence only one path remained — the path of terrorist attacks.

When I fired at Kirov, I reasoned as follows: our shot should be the signal for a challenge, an action within the country against the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and Soviet power…” (vol. 2, case leaves 190, 192).

Not counting, however, on the feasibility of such actions “within the country,” the group in question placed its direct hopes on assistance “from outside” — armed intervention and help from certain foreign states.

Hope of intervention as the only means of overthrowing Soviet power clearly characterizes the views of the accused Nikolaev himself, who did not conceal this from those closest to him.

Thus, Nikolaev Leonid's brother — Nikolaev Pyotr — questioned as a witness in this case, testified:

“…Leonid also told me that Soviet power could be overthrown only through an attack upon it by foreign capitalist states, and that, while abroad, he would assist by every means any capitalist state attacking the Soviet Union in order to overthrow Soviet power…” (vol. 3, case leaf 199).

Similar testimony was given by another relative of Nikolaev, also questioned as a witness in this case — the citizen Kulisher R. M.

“Leonid Nikolaev told me that intervention against the Soviet Union was to be expected in the near future; the content of his statement and the manner in which he expressed it were such that he, L. Nikolaev, would be pleased if intervention actually occurred” (vol. 3, case leaf 262).

The investigation established that the accused Nikolaev, by prior agreement with a member of the “Leningrad center,” the accused Kotolynov, repeatedly visited the … \[1\] consul in Leningrad … \[2\], with whom he negotiated possible forms of assistance to this group and to whom Nikolaev supplied information on certain questions of interest to the consul:

“…I indicated,” Nikolaev says in his testimony of December 20, 1934, “that we were always ready to help the consul by correctly reporting what was happening inside the Soviet Union. Here I had in mind the conversation with Shatsky and Kotolynov about the need to interest the consulate in anti-Soviet material on the Soviet Union's internal situation.

Furthermore, I asked the consul to give us material assistance, stating that we would return the money received from him as soon as our financial affairs changed. —

At the next meeting — the third or fourth in the consulate building — the consul told me that he agreed to grant my requests and handed me five thousand rubles.

He said that he could establish contact with Trotsky if I gave him some letter from the group to Trotsky.

I informed Kotolynov of my conversation with the consul, giving him four thousand five hundred rubles of the money received and keeping five hundred for myself…” (vol. 2, case leaf 280).

Thus, the aims and methods of struggle of this counterrevolutionary terrorist group in Leningrad coincide fully with those of the openly declared enemies of the people — the émigré White Guard landlord-capitalist organizations, the “Russian All-Military Union” and the “Brotherhood of Russian Truth” (Denikinites) — which openly advocate terrorism, systematically send their agents into USSR territory to organize and carry out terrorist attacks against representatives of Soviet power, and carried out the murders of Comrades Vorovsky V. V. and Voikov P. L.

Preparations for the murder of Comrade Kirov

During the investigation it was established that two terrorist groups operated in Leningrad at that time. One was headed by a member of the “Leningrad center,” Kotolynov, and included Nikolaev, Zvezdov, Antonov, Sokolov, and Yuskin.

The other group, according to the same Nikolaev, operated under the leadership of Shatsky N. N.

“…Kotolynov's group,” Nikolaev further testified, “was preparing a terrorist attack on Kirov, and its actual execution was entrusted to me personally.

I know from SHATSKY that the same assignment was given to his group, which carried out this work independently of our preparations for the terrorist attack. I know from Kotolynov that his group was preparing a terrorist attack on Stalin, using the connections it had in Moscow. As I stated in the interrogation record of December 6, 1934, Shatsky, and apparently his group as well, was also preparing a terrorist attack on Stalin. Thus each group had specific instructions for terrorism, carrying them out independently of the other, although their immediate objectives often coincided. This can be seen from the preparations for Kirov's murder, which I prepared mainly in the Smolny area, while Shatsky prepared it chiefly around Kirov's apartment…” (vol. 1, case leaves 273–274).

Speaking of Shatsky's role in this case, Nikolaev testified:

“…I first met Shatsky in 1933. Our next meeting was in the summer of 1934, at 28 Krasnykh Zor Street, where Shatsky was watching the apartment and establishing all of Kirov's movements. He did this to prepare a terrorist attack…

In the October days of 1934 I again met Shatsky near Kirov's apartment, keeping him under observation. Since Shatsky was watching Kirov's apartment, I turned my attention to Smolny and planned to kill Kirov there…” (vol. 1, case leaves 50–51).

As for the circumstances immediately preceding the accused Nikolaev's final decision to commit the terrorist attack on Comrade S. M. Kirov, they appear as follows:

Knowing Nikolaev well since 1922 through work in the Vyborg district, and having grown particularly close to him in recent years, the accused Kotolynov, at one of his meetings with Nikolaev in the autumn of 1934, directly raised with him the question of murdering Comrade Kirov.

This is how the accused Nikolaev himself describes the conversation:

“…It was in mid-September, after the meeting with Shatsky in August 1934. Kotolynov began speaking to me about the work of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization. He referred to my August conversation with Shatsky and proposed that we begin work… From this conversation Kotolynov could conclude that I was prepared to undertake the most extreme actions, including a terrorist attack. At the next meeting, which took place in the same September in the Institute building, Kotolynov began talking about terrorism. Kotolynov asked whether I was ready to carry out a terrorist attack on Kirov. I replied that I was ready to undertake the actual execution of the attack…” (vol. 2, case leaf 81).

Clarifying the content of this conversation, Nikolaev L. V. testified that it was then that he and Kotolynov worked out possible alternatives for the attempt, and it was decided that

— “we would have to act according to the circumstances, which would have to indicate which of the alternatives discussed for the attack should be adopted…” (vol. 2, case leaf 82).

At the same time, according to Nikolaev L. V.'s testimony, Kotolynov said that —

“the elimination of Kirov will weaken the existing leadership of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), and that the former opposition has its own particular scores to settle with Kirov in connection with the struggle he organized against the Leningrad oppositionists…” (vol. 2, case leaf 82).

Subsequently Nikolaev L. himself acknowledged that the accused Kotolynov had also taken a direct part in drawing up the plan for the attempt on Comrade Kirov's life.

This plan, dated November 1 of this year, found on Nikolaev when he was detained and entered in the case as one of the pieces of material evidence, was worked out down to the smallest details, even anticipating difficulties and obstacles that might arise in its execution.

Speaking of his accomplices' role in preparing Comrade Kirov's murder, the accused Nikolaev testified:

“…Kotolynov, having worked out the method of carrying out the attack directly with me, approved that method and specifically ascertained how accurately I could shoot: he was my immediate leader in carrying out the attack. Sokolov ascertained how suitable particular points along Kirov's usual route were, thereby facilitating my work. Sokolov established Kirov's route before the October celebrations of 1934.

Yuskin knew of the preparations for the attack on Kirov and worked out with me the option of an attack at Smolny.

Zvezdov and Antonov knew of the preparations for the attack. They were directly connected with Kotolynov, from whom I know that they were used to gather information about Smolny” (vol. 1, case leaf 274).

The investigation established that Nikolaev L., following the underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group's decision to kill Comrade S. M. Kirov, spent a long time carefully preparing this vile crime together with his accomplices.

Thus, for several months (August–November 1934), the accused Nikolaev L. “test-fired,” as he himself put it, the Nagant revolver in his possession intended for the murder of Comrade Kirov, systematically practicing his marksmanship. At the same time, the accused Nikolaev L. took steps to go underground, preparing forms bearing the stamps of various state institutions. It should also be noted that, in order to conceal traces of the crime and his accomplices, as well as to disguise the true motives for Comrade Kirov's murder, the accused Nikolaev L. prepared a number of documents (a diary, statements addressed to various institutions, and the like), in which he tried to portray his crime as an act of personal despair and dissatisfaction arising from his allegedly difficult material circumstances, and as a protest against “the unjust treatment of a living human being by certain state figures” (vol. 1, case leaf 6).

The accused Nikolaev L. himself acknowledged the falsity and fabricated nature of this version, explaining that he had devised it by prior agreement with the members of the terrorist group, which had decided to portray Comrade Kirov's murder as an individual act and thereby conceal the true motives of the crime.

In his testimony of December 13 of this year, Nikolaev L. says directly:

“…I was to portray Kirov's murder as an individual act, in order to conceal the Zinovievist group's participation in it” (vol. 1, case leaf 266).

This circumstance is also confirmed by the fact that, as established exhaustively during the investigation, the accused Nikolaev L. firmly and systematically rejected every proposal to find him employment, citing at times his poor health and need to study, and at others various circumstances allegedly preventing him from taking up employment.

From the testimony of a number of persons questioned as witnesses in this case, including the accused Nikolaev L.'s mother — M. T. Nikolaeva — and his wife — Draule Milda — the investigation established that the accused NIKOLAEV suffered no material hardship during this period, nor did his family. The absence of any financial difficulties for the accused Nikolaev L. during this period is also indicated by his occupying a decently furnished three-room apartment and, moreover, renting a dacha from a private property owner at the Sestroretsk resort in the summer of 1934. The accused Nikolaev L.'s material circumstances and means of subsistence can also be judged from his own testimony, quoted above, about receiving substantial sums of money from the … \[3\] consul … \[4\].

In one of his statements, the accused Nikolaev declares directly:

“I informed him (Kolotynov) that I had decided not to take employment during the preparations for the attack, so as to have sufficient free time to carry out Kirov's murder. Kotolynov approved my decision” (vol. 2, case leaf 85).

The accused L. Nikolaev's wife — Milda Draule — confirms the same, testifying:

“From the end of March 1934 until his arrest (that is, Nikolaev L.'s), he did not work anywhere. This was not because Nikolaev could not obtain work, but because of his persistent unwillingness to take any employment. Having devoted himself entirely to preparing the terrorist attack, I believe he did not wish to tie himself down to work anywhere…” (vol. 3, case leaf 201).

## Formulation of the charges

Analyzing the foregoing, the investigation considers the following established:

1\) that in 1933–1934, in the city of Leningrad, an underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group was organized and operated among former members of the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group, with the aim of disorganizing the leadership of the Soviet Government through terrorist attacks directed against the leaders of Soviet power and thereby changing current policy in the spirit of the so-called Zinovievist-Trotskyist platform;

2\) that this underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group included the accused in the present case: Nikolaev L. V., Kotolynov I. I., Myasnikov N. P., Shatsky N. N., Mandelshtam S. O., Sokolov G. V., Zvezdov V. I., Yuskin I. G., Rumyantsev V. V., Antonov N. S., Khanik A. O., Tolmazov A. K., Levin V. S., and Sositsky L. I.;

3\) that this underground counterrevolutionary group was led by the illegal terrorist “Leningrad center,” consisting of the accused Kotolynov I. I., Shatsky N. N., Rumyantsev V. V., Mandelshtam S. O., Myasnikov N. P., Levin V. S., Sositsky L. I., and Nikolaev L.;

4\) that the counterrevolutionary terrorist “Leningrad center,” proceeding from its criminal aims described above and also motivated by revenge against Comrade S. M. Kirov, a member of the Presidium of the Central Executive Committee of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, a member of the Politburo of the Party Central Committee, and secretary of the Party's Central and Leningrad Committees, who had ideologically and politically defeated the Leningrad group of former Zinovievists, devised the plan and organized the murder of Comrade S. M. Kirov, which was carried out on December 1 of this year by Nikolaev L. V.

Those charged in the present case testified as follows concerning the substance of the charges brought against them:

1\. Nikolaev L. — admitted his guilt in full and gave the testimony set out above;

2\. Kotolynov — admitted guilt both in belonging to the underground counterrevolutionary group formed in Leningrad among former members of the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group and in being one of its leaders. While denying his direct participation in Comrade S. M. Kirov's murder, the accused Kotolynov nevertheless acknowledged that, as an active member and leader of this group, he bore responsibility for this crime;

3\. Shatsky — did not admit guilt, but is incriminated by the testimony of Nikolaev, Kotolynov, Rumyantsev, Mandelshtam, and others;

4\. Sokolov — admitted guilt in belonging to the underground terrorist group, denying his direct participation in Nikolaev's preparations for the murder of Comrade Kirov;

5\. Zvezdov — admitted his guilt in full;

6\. Yuskin — while denying his membership in the group mentioned above, nevertheless acknowledged that he knew of the preparations for Comrade Kirov's murder;

7\. Antonov — admitted his guilt in full;

8\. Rumyantsev — admitted guilt only in belonging to the underground group of former Zinovievists;

9\. Mandelshtam — admitted guilt both in belonging to the group mentioned and in being a member of the “Leningrad center,” while denying knowledge of the terrorist methods of this group's activity;

10\. Tolmazov — admitted guilt in belonging to the group mentioned above;

11\. Levin — admitted guilt in being one of the leaders of the Leningrad underground group of former Zinovievists;

12\. Khanik and

13\. Myasnikov — admitted guilt only in belonging to the underground group of former Zinovievists;

14\. Sositsky — admitted guilt in belonging to the “Leningrad center” and in knowing of the terrorist sentiments of the members of the underground anti-Soviet group of former Zinovievists.

On the basis of the foregoing, the following are charged:

1\) Nikolaev Leonid Vasilyevich, born in 1904, an employee, a former Party member who had been expelled for violating Party discipline but reinstated in view of his statement of repentance, with having, as a member of the underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group formed among members of the former Zinovievist anti-Soviet group in Leningrad, and a member of the underground counterrevolutionary terrorist “Leningrad center,” on the latter's instructions, committed on December 1, 1934, in the Smolny building in Leningrad, the murder of Comrade S. M. Kirov, a member of the Presidium of the USSR Central Executive Committee, a member of the Politburo of the Party Central Committee, and secretary of the Party's Central and Leningrad Committees, that is, with crimes under Articles 58-8 and 58-11 of the RSFSR Criminal Code;

2\) Kotolynov Ivan Ivanovich, born in 1905, a student, a former member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), expelled for belonging to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group but reinstated following his official statement of full solidarity with the policies of the Party and Soviet power;

3\. Shatsky Nikolai Nikolaevich, born in 1899, an engineer, expelled from the Party in 1927 for belonging to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group, non-Party;

4\. Rumyantsev Vladimir Vasilyevich, born in 1902, an employee, a former Party member expelled for belonging to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group but reinstated following his official statement of full solidarity with the policies of the Party and Soviet power;

5\. Mandelshtam Sergey Osipovich, born in 1896, an employee, a former Party member expelled for belonging to the Zinovievist opposition but reinstated on the same grounds;

6\. Myasnikov Nikolai Petrovich, born in 1900, an employee, twice expelled from the Party for belonging to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group but reinstated on the same grounds;

7\. Levin Vladimir Solomonovich, born in 1897, an employee, a former Party member expelled for belonging to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group but reinstated on the same grounds;

8\. Sositsky Lev Ilyich, born in 1899, an employee, a former Party member expelled for belonging to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet group but reinstated on the same grounds;

9\. Sokolov Georgy Vasilyevich, born in 1904, a student, a former Party member;

10\. Yuskin Ignaty Grigoryevich, born in 1898, an employee, a former Party member;

11\. Zvezdov Vasily Ivanovich, born in 1902, a student, a former Party member;

12\. Antonov Nikolai Semyonovich, born in 1903, a student, a former Party member;

13\. Khanik Lev Osipovich, born in 1902, an employee, a former Party member;

14\. Tolmazov Andrey Ilyich, born in 1899, a student, a former Party member —

all with having, as participants in the underground counterrevolutionary terrorist group in Leningrad, organized and prepared the murder of Comrade S. M. Kirov committed by the accused Nikolaev on December 1, 1934, that is, with crimes under Articles 53-8 and 58-11 of the RSFSR Criminal Code.

In consequence of the foregoing, and in accordance with the resolutions of the Central Executive Committee of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics of July 10 and December 1, 1934, all the persons named above are subject to trial before the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics.

This indictment was drawn up in Leningrad on December 25, 1934.
