INTERROGATION RECORD

OF KAMENEV, Lev Borisovich

dated January 19, 1935.

I want to relate everything I know, or even suspect, about people whom I have had occasion to meet or about whom I have heard, insofar as they expressed anti-Party opinions, sentiments, and views to any degree whatsoever.

First of all, I want to emphasize that the sentiments displayed by LOMINADZE and SHATSKIN during my conversation with them in 1932 seem to me very dangerous. Both were sharply hostile to the Party line and to the leadership in general, and in particular to Comrade STALIN.

Both considered it impossible to remain inactive and spoke to me very insistently about the need for contact between the various anti-Party groups, proposing as the form of this contact that each group create a strictly clandestine group of three, which would maintain relations with one another.

They said in this connection that they had such a group of three, consisting of SHATSKIN, LOMINADZE, and, I believe—I do not remember the surname exactly—REZNIKOV. I remember that this third person was in Voronezh. In reply to my question about their attitude toward STEN, they spoke dismissively of him, saying that he was an intemperate man, that he associated with goodness knows whom, and that he could not be trusted. They were undoubtedly disappointed when I called their undertaking childish, pointed out that the experience of the Party's entire life showed that no secret organizations could survive within the Party, and said that I considered it childish to embark on such an enterprise.

At the end of the conversation, it seemed to me that they had lost all hope in me but had not lost the slightest confidence in the correctness of the path they were following. At any rate, they never again approached me with conversations of this kind.

My impression is that they cannot be trusted under any circumstances. After that conversation I heard nothing of their sentiments or activities except for one incident which, in my opinion, confirms the impression I have expressed. The incident was as follows: quite recently, after returning from Kislovodsk, ZINOVIEV told me the following: supposedly, at a small dinner party at Comrade STALIN's in Sochi, LOMINADZE was also present and proposed a toast to Comrade STALIN; when those present remarked to him that this was not enough on his part, he rose a second time and could find only one thing to say: that he would no longer deceive those present.

In relating this story, ZINOVIEV named the person who had told it to him directly, on the authority of LOMINADZE himself. Unfortunately, I do not remember the narrator's name; I think that, if there is any connection between LOMINADZE and MAGYAR, this narrator was MAGYAR.

I consider it necessary to pass on this story because it seems to me very important to identify the person who could report this fact so directly, and also because the very fact that LOMINADZE told the story, and the tone in which he did so, cannot attest to any sincerity on the part of the narrator, that is, LOMINADZE.

Returning to SHATSKIN and LOMINADZE's plan, I must connect the following fact with it; I must admit that at the time I saw no connection between these facts and had simply forgotten what I shall now relate. I remembered it only now, carefully straining my memory to establish my relations with VAGANIAN \[1\].

Only now did I remember that in 1932, probably in the spring, or perhaps even in the winter of 1931–1932, VAGANIAN very persistently importuned me, on at least two occasions, pointing out the inadmissibility of passive conduct by the former opposition groups and, in particular, by me personally, in the face of the grave mood through which the country was passing.

I recall that he conducted the first conversation very cautiously and said only that it would be useful for former oppositionists to meet at least occasionally, without any mutual commitments, to exchange opinions.

Although I rejected his proposal and all his arguments, he came to see me a second time and again very insistently proposed that we abandon our passivity. I remember that he hinted that my rejection of his proposal would be regarded in certain circles simply as cowardice; apparently to strengthen my belief that the undertaking was organized on strictly conspiratorial principles, he said that their group of three consisted of such people as MRACHKOVSKY, I. SMIRNOV, and himself.

I remember being outraged by this hint of cowardice and sharply rejecting all his proposals, declaring that I would not join any conspiratorial organization. I remember that he was visibly upset by the firmness of my refusal, immediately rose to leave, and said to me in parting: “So this is unshakable, Lev Borisovich?”

Having now recalled these conversations with VAGANIAN, I cannot help connecting them with what I heard several months later from SHATSKIN and LOMINADZE. It now seems to me that both proposals rested on a single organizational plan, that the two groups knew of one another, and that their groups of three maintained some kind of relations; I now think, although I have no factual evidence, that a plan for a strictly conspiratorial organization of this kind could have been developed, among the people I have mentioned, only by I. SMIRNOV, who, even during the open factional struggle, was regarded among the Trotskyists as a kind of chief of staff and their best organizer, specifically an organizer of small, clandestine cells of this kind.

If I relate such details, to which I myself paid no attention at the time and whose full significance I have appreciated only now, it is because these details and my conjectures may perhaps help the government unravel all the threads and knots of the underground.

If I point out my rejection of the proposals both of VAGANIAN and of SHATSKIN–LOMINADZE, it is under no circumstances to relieve myself of even the slightest responsibility. I remember well: conspiracy with VAGANIAN, SHATSKIN, MRACHKOVSKY, and so forth did not attract me in the slightest even then; I saw no prospects and no program for it, and that is why, without consulting anyone, I gave so sharp a negative answer to both groups.

These two groups, both in their composition and in their mood in 1932 (I also saw VAGANIAN in 1934, when he came to the publishing house to ask for work, but I had no political conversations with him), seem to me the most harmful and dangerous of all the groups and individuals known to me.

The next place in terms of danger, it seems to me, is occupied by SHLYAPNIKOV. In recent years I have not met him except on chance encounters, but I have neither seen nor heard of any signs of any softening whatever of his or MEDVEDEV's sharply hostile sentiments. They are unquestionable enemies, who do not even make much effort to conceal their hostility.

I consider MEDVEDEV more dangerous than SHLYAPNIKOV, for he has always acted as a man who goes to the end and is not afraid to draw conclusions from his premises.

I turn to a characterization of another group, the former Rightists. Before leaving for Minusinsk I met Tomsky several times on business matters, but the conversation also turned to politics.

I must state that I noticed no reconsideration on his part of his hostile attitude toward the Party leadership. He proposed nothing to me and apparently had no prospects at all, but all his statements and descriptions of the situation in the Party and of its leaders were quite sufficient evidence that he retained his entirely negative attitude toward them.

His sentiments can be characterized in these words: “We have been defeated, we shall never rise again now, but all this was done wrongly and I have nothing to renounce.”

I remember this remark of his: “We allowed STALIN to outwit us (in answer to my question about how the Rightists now assessed their participation in the struggle against us in 1925–1928). Now he has won for a long time and will torment us for a long time.”

After returning from Minusinsk I did not see Tomsky. My last meeting with Rykov took place while he was still in his office at the Sovnarkom, and therefore before his removal. He was restrained, but also had the air of a man who had absolutely nothing to reconsider in his political opinions.

Of his remarks I remember only one: in answer to my question about the mood of his like-minded associates, he replied approximately as follows: “Tomsky and I think alike, but I do not understand Bukharin; he is engaged in some sort of balancing act and seems to hope to outwit Stalin.”

At that moment his attitude toward Bukharin's line was clearly negative.

As for Bukharin himself, I have no firm evidence on which to characterize his sentiments. I met him several times (two or three times) in 1933–1934 for purely literary reasons. Once he came to see me at the publishing house with a proposal that I write an article for an Academy of Sciences collection devoted to Lenin, an article entitled “Lenin and the Dictatorship of the Proletariat.”

When I asked why he did not write it himself, he replied that he was afraid of faultfinding.

On the second occasion I myself brought him an article at the editorial offices of “Izvestia,” where it was published. He then proposed that I take on the editorship of the literary criticism section of “Izvestia.” I refused, pleading lack of time, but in reality because I considered it politically unacceptable for me to be on an editorial staff together with Bukharin, and tactless on Bukharin's part to make me this offer without the Central Committee.

On neither occasion were there political conversations, but from isolated expressions, unfinished remarks, and so forth I formed the impression that he had essentially reconsidered nothing, continued to regard his removal from participation in the Party leadership as an injustice and an accident, and maintained, as it were, two ideological households: one for the outside world, which at the same time enabled him to run the newspaper, and the other secret, for himself, to which he perhaps admitted no one, but in which all the feelings and thoughts he had entertained during the open struggle against the Party remained intact.

I repeat that I have no firm and indisputable proof for the characterizations of the Rightists given above. They are the result of an accumulation of impressions. I consider it necessary to give this characterization, however, because I myself have now become convinced what dangerous material anti-Party sentiments represent, even when left unspoken, even when concealed.

Undoubtedly, BUKHARIN wants to return to a leading role in the Party, to earn trust, but not because he has reconsidered his views; rather, he is continuing the policy of which he told me as early as 1929: “We have learned from your experience and will not allow ourselves to be put out of action as you were.”

The difference between Tomsky's and Bukharin's sentiments seems to me to be explained not by the degree to which either has reconsidered his attitude toward the Party, but only by the fact that Tomsky gives the impression of a man who has given up and sees no prospects for himself, whereas Bukharin is younger, more agile, better able to disguise himself, and has by no means given up on his future in the Party.

SMILGA's sentiments are not entirely clear to me, although we met several times over the last two years (when coming from Tashkent, he invariably visited the publishing house), but there were no direct political conversations between us, and I had no opportunity to hear from him any reasonably coherent exposition of his sentiments or opinions. I heard no malicious outbursts from him against the Party line or its leaders. Nor did he pass on to me any malicious information from his field of work.

It seems to me, however, that he belongs to those people who consider themselves, as it were, on leave from politics, but are ready to report to the assembly point at the first signal; it will then turn out that they have in no way changed their negative attitude toward the whole of the most recent period of Party activity.

I did not hear from SMILGA that he was connected with any group or that anyone stood behind him. It is true that in 1932, when I was forming the editorial staff of the Academia publishing house and invited him to undertake the editorship of the foreign memoirs section, he told me that he knew a group of young historians. And he did indeed distribute a number of assignments to ROKHKIN \[2\], VOLKOV, STAROSELSKY, and others.

A number of these collaborators, as I later learned, were implicated in the Ryutinites' case. I remember that, having returned from Minusinsk and met SMILGA, I laughed at him, saying: “Why were you boasting about your young historians? They turned out to be Ryutinites.” To this SMILGA replied: “Who the devil knew they were Ryutinites?”

In 1932 TRIFONOV worked with me at the Glavkontsesskom. From his accounts I gathered that he met SMILGA quite often and that SMILGA passed on to him the information he obtained through his work at Gosplan.

TRIFONOV's attitude, and consequently SMILGA's as well, toward the policy being pursued at the time, and their assessment of the situation in the country, were negative. In TRIFONOV's case, in my view, this had a somewhat philistine character: that is, he drew no political conclusions or generalizations from his views, but simply pointed out that things were bad in such and such a place, that such and such a thing was being done poorly, focusing mainly on the failure to fulfill the plan in coal and metals.

I also met SMILGA in the autumn of 1932 in Gagra. Here too I recall no political conversations. It seems to me that I told him about the existence of Ryutin's platform. As I remember, he received this account as something new to him.

Neither TRIFONOV nor SMILGA can be considered to stand on Party positions.

In general, summing up my impressions over recent years of a number of former leaders of opposition groups or their active participants, I believe that many of them must be characterized as people “on leave,” that is, as people who have reconsidered nothing, have by no means renounced their hostile, and often extremely hostile, attitude toward the Party leadership, but consider that there is nothing to be done now and that all that remains is to wait for some moment when they can take active action.

Besides the people mentioned above, I consider, for example, that UGLANOV belongs to this category, whatever he may say and however he may behave.

My last conversation with UGLANOV (probably in the winter of 1931), by chance in the street, where he caught up with me, remained in my memory because he used the following formulation: if there is a war, we shall fight knee-deep in blood.

I did not understand and asked him again. He then said that, according to his impressions, the mood of the peasantry was such that before going to the front it would be necessary to suppress enormous peasant revolts. To my somewhat malicious question, “Well, has everyone deserted you?”, he replied: “No, you are mistaken; when I am in Moscow, I visit KOZLOV (a former member of the MK bureau); he is a good fellow, the lads gather around him.”

There are probably a number of other people in the same mood of whom I do not know. I shall point out only that one of the focal points for the accumulation of an attitude, if not directly hostile, then highly critical, toward the Party's current policy is V. I. NEVSKY. He is so unable to restrain this critical mood that he once even put me in an awkward position before non-Party people by bursting out, at an editorial meeting of the publishing house at the end of 1933, with a speech defending the need for freedom of opinion and freedom of criticism. Moreover, he spoke so absurdly and with so clear a hint that someone was preventing him from working on historical questions with complete freedom that he provoked objections not only from the Party members present but also from a number of non-Party people. I was obliged to rebuff him decisively in my concluding remarks.

On several occasions he spoke very highly of the young historian PARADIZOV as his pupil. I saw the latter several times on publishing business and discussed literary, but not political, subjects with him. NEVSKY, at any rate, belongs among those who have not inwardly reconciled themselves with the Party.

Among the writers whom I have had occasion to meet through my work, I consider I. A. TEODOROVICH to be of the same kind.

I shall now go through the people whom the investigation reminded me of: ZOF—I have an ambivalent impression of him. I remember that in 1932 ZINOVIEV told me with some surprise that ZOF had unexpectedly come to see him, as if in repentance for having left our group; he spoke of the dreadful situation in the country, in particular of the famine in Ukraine, and said that our role had not yet been played, that the government was in such difficulties that it would have to call on us. But when, in 1933, I happened to see ZOF at the rest home in Foros, he said nothing of the kind to me; on the contrary, he spoke enthusiastically of his work and of the great successes in Ukraine. Neither I nor he brought up his visit to ZINOVIEV.

I met PREOBRAZHENSKY only at the Seventeenth Party Congress, after my speech, and the short conversation with him gave me the impression that he had indeed reconsidered his views and indeed made peace with the Party.

SOKOLNIKOV broke with us definitively and long ago. We live next to each other, but over the past three or four years he has not come to see me once and has generally made it quite clear that he does not want even personal relations, just as PYATAKOV too broke off relations personally and definitively.

Concerning POLUYAN (which of the two, I do not remember), I heard from ZINOVIEV that, on returning from a journey through the Kuban, he wept and said: look what they have done to the Kuban, where, instead of beautiful wheat, everything is overgrown with weeds taller than a rider's head.

I consider SAFAROV a very dangerous man. Dangerous because of the instability of his character, his rapid changes of mood, and his constant readiness to draw the most extreme conclusions from any situation.

Before turning to a characterization of ZINOVIEV, I shall point out a few other things which may perhaps be of no importance but which the Party ought to know.

During the congress ZINOVIEV conveyed the following impressions to me: he was sitting next to KUBYAK and KOMAROV; throughout the congress their mood was somehow skeptical and ironic. ZINOVIEV told me: “It is clear that these people are essentially interested in nothing; they became animated only at the election of the Central Committee, and when they heard their surnames among the candidates for the Central Committee, one of them wrote the other a note: ‘That is something, at least they will not throw us out of our apartment.’”

ZINOVIEV told me he read this note while one of them was writing it.

In 1932 ROTSTEIN spent several days living next to us at the OGIZ rest home. He came to see me once or twice and spoke sharply negatively about the situation created by the Party's policy, saying that he understood nothing of this policy, that it was leading to the impoverishment of the country, and so forth.

I must add that when I met ROTSTEIN again in 1934 at Ostafyevo, there was no trace of those sentiments in him, or at least he said nothing to me about them. On the contrary, with complete enthusiasm and satisfaction, in answer to my question about how he was getting on, he told me that he had written a letter to Comrade STALIN and had received full satisfaction, that thanks to Comrade STALIN he now had the opportunity to devote himself entirely to his work, that he was excellently provided for materially for this work, and so forth.

To avoid returning to the subject, I must also say something about STEN. I became acquainted with him and met him on closer terms only in 1932.

He was very critical of the Party's policy and of Comrade STALIN personally, but gave the impression of a solitary individual expressing his personal opinions, and of a man essentially little interested in politics.

My conversations with him very quickly turned to literary and philosophical subjects.

I shall now relate what actually happened regarding the so-called Ryutin platform. At the very end of October 1932, having completed all the arrangements for my leave and already having my accommodation voucher in hand, I stopped at the dacha for the last time to collect the books I needed and so forth. It was in the evening, about nine o'clock; during supper ZINOVIEV's housekeeper came and said that ZINOVIEV asked me to come upstairs to him. I found STEN at ZINOVIEV's. As soon as I came in, ZINOVIEV handed me a document and said: “Read this and tell me where you think this document could have come from.” When I asked what it was about, he replied: “STEN and I are arguing about who could have written it; read it and tell us your opinion.” I read the document then and there and said, as I distinctly remember: “These are some Rightists gone berserk.” To this ZINOVIEV said: “That is what I think too, but STEN insists that it comes from the Leftists.” I then said: “But STEN knows whom he received it from, and therefore he can judge from that where it comes from,” and asked STEN directly: “Whom did you receive it from?” STEN replied: “That I cannot say, and it is not indicative anyway; but why do you think it comes from the Rightists?”

I began pointing to particular passages of the document which, in my opinion, confirmed my idea. I stumbled over one passage.

I do not remember whether it was ZINOVIEV or STEN who said to me: “But we know the Rightists; who personally, in your opinion, could have written this?” I replied that the first thought that suggested itself was that it was the work of BUKHARIN's pen, but that I did not think he had reached the boiling point evident in this document, and that I therefore did not venture to attribute it to BUKHARIN; STEN, however, continued to insist that it nevertheless came from Left circles and shortly afterward left in haste because his train was about to depart. I do not remember whether it was in STEN's presence or after his departure that I said: “They will shoot people for things like this, and they will be right.”

I must add that, when telling SMILGA and SHATSKIN–LOMINADZE about this document a few days later, I was still under the same impression and used the same characterization: “They will shoot people for things like this, and they will be right.”

A few days later I received a telegraphic order to appear before the Central Control Commission and, on arriving in Moscow, went to see ZINOVIEV, still without connecting my summons with this document.

At ZINOVIEV's apartment I found, besides himself, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAYEV, and, I believe, KHARITONOV. There was also a fourth person, but I do not remember who exactly.

Only then did ZINOVIEV tell me that the document STEN had shown us belonged to the Ryutinites (which, I remember, surprised me greatly, for I had never heard or suspected that RYUTIN could play the role of leader or ideologist of any group), that STEN had been arrested, and that the Central Control Commission was implicating us in this case.

What followed is known. I shall now relate what I know about connections in the international movement.

Personally, I had no connections. As for ZINOVIEV, I recall three incidents of which he told me and which may be of some significance.

It must be said that ZINOVIEV returned from Kustanai very frightened by the successes of fascism. Fascism, the reason for its victories in Germany, and its subsequent fate initially occupied ZINOVIEV entirely, and he spoke of almost nothing else. Gradually it became clear to me that his fear of fascism was pushing ZINOVIEV toward a revision of the Comintern's recent tactics.

I cannot say that ZINOVIEV's individual critical remarks on this subject coalesced into any finished system that he had thought through to the end. In general, however, his critical remarks can be grouped around three points:

1). He emphasized that the characterization of the Second International as social-fascist had proved incorrect;

2). That the united-front tactic should have begun to be implemented earlier and, above all, without insisting so firmly on separating the masses from their leaders;

3). (This was after reports of the united front in France had appeared in the newspapers.) That, if a united-front policy on the French model had been pursued in Germany, it might perhaps have been possible to prevent Hitler's accession to power.

Once, I remember, ZINOVIEV “rose” to this generalization: it was clear, he said, that a tremendous wave of fascism was sweeping across Europe and that only the working class as a whole could resist it; now, he said, the fascists were beating the working class in sections, first the Communists and then the Social Democrats.

This point of view was naturally accompanied by a critical attitude toward the Comintern's activity; in connection with the latter I recall the three conversations mentioned above. The first concerns a report delivered by ZINOVIEV at the Institute of World Economy. He came to see me the day after the report and enthusiastically told me that it had been very well received, that afterward a number of leading Comintern figures had gathered around him (he named, as I recall, PYATNITSKY, HECKERT, and BÉLA KUN), congratulated him on the report, and that one of those named had said aloud: “Now there is a chairman of the Comintern.”

On another occasion ZINOVIEV told me of his conversation with one of the Germans, I think with HECKERT. As I recall, he related that the impression he had received was very sad, that there was squabbling in the German party's Central Committee, that there was no real leadership, that the leader was PIECK, but that he too had degenerated and collected carpets, and that it would be good if ZINOVIEV returned to the Comintern.

After a meeting with PYATNITSKY, apparently by chance in the street, ZINOVIEV related that he had the impression that PYATNITSKY was aware of the Comintern's poor work and likewise would have no objection to his return to the Comintern.

All these conversations of ZINOVIEV's, I repeat, did not have the character of any thought-out, finished political line, but were rather complaints and expressions of doubts and vacillations, whose general meaning nevertheless clearly tended toward a revision of the Comintern's tactics.

I must admit that I attached no particular importance to all this and did not even take the trouble to argue with ZINOVIEV, knowing how often his mood changed and clearly realizing that beneath it all lay some vague hope that he would yet be called back to the Comintern to “correct its mistakes.” On one occasion I even laughed at him, saying: “I see that you are still drawn to the Comintern.”

In the end, these revisionist sentiments concerning the Comintern's tactics led to ZINOVIEV clearly falling under the influence of TROTSKY's writings. This happened very recently, after his return from Kislovodsk, that is, already in the month of November. He obtained access to TROTSKY's writings at the Marx and Engels Institute and, after reading a great deal of them, once came to see me and, in recounting what he had read, stressed two points: first, that TROTSKY had joined the French Socialist Party (which was news to him and even more so to me), and that for some reason our newspapers were keeping silent about it; second, that after reading TROTSKY's articles he had gained the impression that TROTSKY was, of course, wrong in his positive program, but right in almost all his criticism, especially in the international field.

This was undoubtedly a recurrence of Trotskyist sentiments, which I explained to myself at the time by the fact that ZINOVIEV had found in TROTSKY's writings confirmation and, as it were, a culmination of his critical sentiments concerning the Comintern's tactics, and by the fact that his removal from “Bolshevik” had once again thrown him into a bitter mood.

In this connection I also recall that ZINOVIEV several times drew my attention to PYATNITSKY's feuilletons in “Pravda” on the Comintern's situation (which I never did read), in which he read a capitulation of the Third International to the Second. When I asked in surprise what this meant and how PYATNITSKY could advocate the Comintern's capitulation in “Pravda,” ZINOVIEV explained that between the lines of PYATNITSKY's article one could read: let the Second International present us with whatever demands it likes—we shall accept them all.

I must add that the days when ZINOVIEV and I showed each other the manuscripts of our articles beforehand and consulted about their content have long since passed. At any rate, during the last two years neither I showed ZINOVIEV my articles nor did ZINOVIEV show me his. I must admit that I have read hardly any of his articles, even in print, and therefore cannot say whether the sentiments I have described above found any expression, even veiled, in his articles in “Bolshevik.”

That is everything I can recall at the present moment about the people and sentiments known to me in recent times.

I must add that, unfortunately late, I have come to the conviction that not only openly hostile sentiments and groups are dangerous to the Communist Party's cause, but also those people who keep to themselves their negative attitude toward the Party's policy or its leadership; I therefore ardently wish to relate and expose all sentiments of this kind, even those that at first sight seem most innocent.

L. KAMENEV.

\[1\] In the text, here and below, erroneously: “Vaganyan” [Ваганьян].

\[2\] Rokhkin, Grigory Yevseyevich, born in 1890 in the town of Volkovysk. He was rector of the All-Ukrainian Institute of Marxism. At the same time, in 1923, he lectured on general history at the workers' faculty of the Kharkov Technological Institute and at the Communist University. Full member of the chair of the history of European culture at the Ukrainian Institute of Marxism (1927). He later worked in Moscow at the Institute of Red Professors. A member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), he was expelled in 1932 for opposition activity. That same year he was arrested in the case of the “Union of Marxist-Leninists” (Ryutin's group) and sentenced to five years in a corrective labor camp. He was released on February 8, 1937. Arrested again on March 28, 1937, he was sentenced to death by shooting and shot on September 10, 1937.
