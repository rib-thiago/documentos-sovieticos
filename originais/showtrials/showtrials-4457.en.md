INTERROGATION RECORD

OF ROTSKAN, Pyotr Eduardovich

dated January 13, 1935.

ROTSKAN Pyotr Eduardovich, born in 1894, from the town of Lyutsin, Latvia, Latvian, father a worker, citizen of the USSR, residing at Pyatigorsk, Gogolevskaya Street, No. 16, apartment 14, head of the forestry administration of the North Caucasus Territorial Land Administration in Pyatigorsk. Married. Wife Anna Ivanovna, aged 40; childless; I support my wife's mother, who lives in Moscow. Owned no real estate before or after the revolution. Education: four classes of gymnasium in former Petersburg. Member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) since 1915; expelled in 1927 for factional work as a Zinovievist and reinstated six months later. Took part in a number of strikes before 1917; from 1917 worked in the military organization of the Russian Social Democratic Labor Party (Bolsheviks), member of the Leningrad Soviet, in the Red Guard throughout 1917, participated in the October uprising, then held various state and public posts. No convictions before or after October. Military registration category: 10. Did not serve with the Whites.

QUESTION: How did your ties, as a participant in the Zinovievist organization, develop with members of the organization's Moscow center, ZINOVIEV, BAKAEV, and others?

ANSWER: After I submitted my statement joining the “23,” the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) sent me to work in the Far East, at Blagoveshchensk-on-Amur; I went as a non-Party person and was appointed chairman of the District Union of Industrial Cooperatives; I remained in the Far Eastern Territory until July 1929.

From Blagoveshchensk I wrote ZINOVIEV several letters and received replies from him; I wrote about how I had settled in, wrote about Trotskyists whom I happened to meet; I do not remember all the subjects I touched upon in the correspondence, but remember asking ZINOVIEV to help me get out of the Far Eastern Territory.

In Blagoveshchensk I was reinstated in the Party and recalled by the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) to Moscow, from where I was sent to the North Caucasus.

While in Moscow I saw ZINOVIEV, KUKLIN, then ZINOVIEV's secretary BOGDAN and old EMELYANOV; I cannot remember whether I saw BAKAEV.

From conversations with all these people I established that they constituted a closed collective living a political life, were hostile to the Party, and in fact held the positions of the former Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc; in conversations with me they complained in anti-Party terms about the Party leadership, accusing it of persecuting opposition cadres and allegedly suppressing ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, explaining this as a “mocking attitude” toward them; at the same time they made attacks in indecent terms on STALIN and other leading members of the Central Committee.

I had a conversation with ZINOVIEV about the forthcoming purge of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in 1929. I told him that I had retained opposition views and therefore asked for advice on how to behave during the screening.

To this ZINOVIEV replied: “The Party purge must not be turned into an arena of struggle for our views.”

These words of ZINOVIEV impressed themselves so deeply on my memory that I quote them almost literally. It is not difficult to understand what such an answer meant in plain language: “Do not lay down your weapons; practice double-dealing; if you tell the truth about your views you will be expelled, and we still need you.” During my screening in 1929 I acted in accordance with this advice from ZINOVIEV.

At that time I also read the stenographic report of the plenum of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks); ZINOVIEV gave it to me, having obtained it illegally from STEN; I do not know how STEN obtained the report. In this way I learned in detail about the disagreements that had arisen between the overwhelming majority of Central Committee members and the group of BUKHARIN, TOMSKY, and others.

I shared this information about the plenum with the participants in the organization whom I met then; I think I shared my impressions with the participant in the organization in Rostov-on-Don, GORDON, to whom, incidentally, I conveyed ZINOVIEV's directive on behavior during the purge.

This information about disagreements within the Central Committee served for me as confirmation of the view constantly developed by ZINOVIEV that the Party would “call” ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV to leadership work.

I also had a conversation with old EMELYANOV N. A. (of Sestroretsk), who told me about N. K. KRUPSKAYA's anti-Party sentiments and that she had spoken sharply about STALIN. This was in 1929.

Then I went to the North Caucasus, to Rostov-on-Don. In October-November 1930 I traveled to Leningrad on an assignment from the North Caucasus Territorial Executive Committee, stopping in Moscow; I cannot recall whether I had time to see any of our comrades; I allow that I saw KUKLIN or BAKAEV, if they were in Moscow at that time.

In the summer of 1931, while on a business trip to Moscow for a conference on grain procurements, I saw ZINOVIEV, KUKLIN, BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, and, if memory serves, BOGDAN.

At BOGDAN's I saw the address of ZINOVIEV's dacha; he lived at Ilyinskaya station on the Moscow Railway.

I asked ZINOVIEV two questions: 1) was what had been reported in the press and at Party meetings true concerning attempts to create a new opposition bloc between BUKHARIN's group and KAMENEV–ZINOVIEV's group? 2) how did ZINOVIEV assess the Party's present policy and its leadership?

ZINOVIEV confirmed that negotiations with the Rights had taken place, but denied the Party's information that there had been an attempt to create a bloc.

To the second question he replied that “production costs” were high, that economic construction was proceeding with enormous overhead costs, and that the Party leadership was to blame for this, having demonstrated its inadequacy. ZINOVIEV plainly gave me to understand that if a different leadership were at the helm of the Party and government — he meant himself and KAMENEV — then all construction would proceed differently.

I told ZINOVIEV that, besides myself, the old oppositionist Timofei DMITRIEV was also an associate of his in Rostov-on-Don.

At the end of the conversation I expressed to ZINOVIEV, on my own behalf and on behalf of T. DMITRIEV, the wish that he should not lose heart. ZINOVIEV replied with the phrase: “Never mind, Marxism [1] comes to the rescue” (literal expression).

Incidentally, I must say that while in Rostov-on-Don I had coordinated my visit to ZINOVIEV with T. DMITRIEV, agreeing that I could speak on behalf of both of us.

KUKLIN's political sentiments were the same as ZINOVIEV's.

From KUKLIN I heard condemnation of the Party leadership in the same terms; the same assessment of economic construction (enormous overhead costs, excessive sacrifices, and so on), complaints against the Party leadership about distrust and persecution.

KUKLIN told me that shortly after the attempt by BUKHARIN's group and ZINOVIEV's group to form a bloc, ZINOVIEV had intended to convene a meeting of 40 participants in the organization who were in the provinces, including summoning me to this meeting, and to warn them against attempts at a bloc with the Rights locally; I cannot now remember why this meeting was not convened — I have forgotten KUKLIN's explanation.

In his conversation with me BAKAEV confirmed that he shared ZINOVIEV's assessment of the Party's policy and leadership, using the same expressions about “overhead costs,” “production costs,” and so forth. I informed BAKAEV that I was not alone in Rostov-on-Don, that I had an associate in T. DMITRIEV.

The conversation with EVDOKIMOV contained nothing of interest; it seemed to me that he was avoiding political conversation with me, whether out of distrust — we knew each other much less than, say, I knew BAKAEV and ZINOVIEV — or for other reasons.

EVDOKIMOV gave me a little red book to read — secret materials of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) containing testimony in the case of the Labor Peasant Party, “TKP” (CHAYANOV, KONDRATIEV, and others), which I returned to him there and then after reading — I read only part of the materials; the meeting took place at the “Paris” hotel, where EVDOKIMOV was then living.

I cannot recall the conversation with BOGDAN; it was of the same character as the conversation with ZINOVIEV and BAKAEV; BOGDAN usually repeated all of ZINOVIEV's arguments against the Party.

In my next testimony I shall report further facts to the investigation.

Everything set out above was correctly recorded from my words. The record was read to me —

ROTSKAN.

INTERROGATED BY:

ASSISTANT HEAD OF THE EKO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD — DMITRIEV.

certified correct: Kazakova
