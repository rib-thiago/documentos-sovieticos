INTERROGATION RECORD

of ZALUTSKY, Pyotr Antonovich

January 17, 1935.

For opposition work I was expelled from the AUCP(b) by decision of the Fifteenth Party Congress.

I was reinstated in the party in July 1928 after submitting a statement renouncing opposition views and work.

Meanwhile, my opposition past weighed so heavily upon me that, despite submitting the statement, I continued to remain in the Zinovievist underground, remained a Zinovievist.

In 1928, after my reinstatement in the party, I went to Kaluga to visit ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, to learn about their political sentiments, convey my own sentiments and understanding of the situation that had arisen for the Zinovievist group, and ascertain what to do next.

ZINOVIEV pointed out that the opposition had proved right, that at the Fifteenth Congress the party had in fact accepted the opposition's main demands, that the struggle would have to be continued, adapting it to the new conditions, and that ties with like-minded people should not be severed; on the contrary, they should be preserved and relations strengthened.

KAMENEV developed the same views.

At the end of 1928 I visited ZINOVIEV's apartment; present were ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, if I am not mistaken SHAROV, and myself. I cannot now recall in connection with what question ZINOVIEV, addressing all of us, said: “It would do no harm to organize a center, something like a troika, to maintain contact with our friends,” that is, he proposed forming a Zinovievist center; we immediately supported this proposal.

In 1929 we gathered at L. B. KAMENEV's apartment; present were myself, BAKAEV, BABAKHAN, SHVALBE [i], and KAMENEV; TROTSKY was being deported abroad. KAMENEV objected to this measure, and everyone supported him. BAKAEV declared that he considered it necessary to protest against the deportation; the others supported him.

As far as I remember, that same evening KAMENEV went to TOMSKY's dacha to negotiate a bloc; TOMSKY's driver came to the apartment to collect KAMENEV. KAMENEV asked our advice as to whether he should go to TOMSKY and said that the Right had very sharp disagreements with the majority of the AUCP(b) Central Committee and was seeking a rapprochement with the ZINOVIEV–KAMENEV group.

KAMENEV went to TOMSKY; incidentally, the driver had been looking for ZINOVIEV to take him to TOMSKY as well, but had not found him.

Those assembled stayed to await KAMENEV's return; he came back about two to two and a half hours later. KAMENEV reported that at TOMSKY's he had found, besides TOMSKY himself, BUKHARIN, RYKOV, and, I believe, SCHMIDT; that the entire group was dissatisfied with the Central Committee majority headed by STALIN and feared the onset of a catastrophe in the country as a result of the incorrect policies of the AUCP(b) Central Committee; that the Right considered joint work with ZINOVIEV's group more acceptable, believing common ground could be found with the latter; and that he, KAMENEV, had made no proposals but had merely listened and ascertained the Right's intentions.

The following day, myself, BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, KAMENEV, ZINOVIEV, and several other people whose surnames I cannot recall gathered at ZINOVIEV's apartment to discuss the answer to the Right.

It was decided to regard an agreement with the Right as possible on the basis of the so-called Zinovievist platform.

In that same year, 1929, I visited DESOV, chairman of the Regional Control Commission of the AUCP(b) in Leningrad, at the “Paris” hotel. Several people were with him: BORODIN, YUNOSOV, and a number of others whose surnames I cannot now remember. DESOV was embittered against KIROV because of his impending removal from office — that was what I understood from the conversation with DESOV. He took out an issue of the newspaper “Terek” for 1911 and read out several articles published in it of an openly reactionary character, attributing their authorship to KIROV. I spoke harshly of KIROV and noted the newspaper's name and publication year in my notebook in order to pass this on to my friends.

Some time later I was summoned to the AUCP(b) Central Control Commission and accused of intrigue; following a statement by BORODIN, I received a severe reprimand with a warning. I remember that YUNOSOV was expelled from the AUCP(b); at the time I was a member of the territorial party committee of Saratov territory, then the Lower Volga territory, and a candidate member of its bureau, and in view of this Central Control Commission decision I was removed from the candidate membership of the bureau.

I forgot to say that I was summoned to the AUCP(b) Central Control Commission together with DESOV by Sergo ORDZHONIKIDZE, who was then its chairman; referring to myself, ZINOVIEV, and KAMENEV, he said that we remained enemies of the party to this day.

That same day I informed ZINOVIEV of this conversation, and he told me he would go to Sergo to intercede for me. I understood that ZINOVIEV was also guided by another consideration: the apprehensions aroused in him by Sergo's words concerning himself and KAMENEV.

On returning from Sergo, ZINOVIEV told me: “I went to Sergo to talk about you as the leader of our group; Sergo scolded me on your account.” Of course, ZINOVIEV did not tell Sergo that he represented a group; what he meant by this phrase was that he had been scolded on someone else's behalf.

My harsh statement about KIROV was not accidental; it resulted from my participation in the Zinovievist underground and the general attitude toward KIROV as the organizer of the defeat of the opposition in Leningrad.

In the second half of 1929, in a conversation with us — myself, BAKAEV or EVDOKIMOV — ZINOVIEV, assessing the Union's immediate internal prospects, stated that “it is impossible that very large uprisings should not occur in the countryside in the near future and spread to the towns,” that all this was a consequence of the party's incorrect policies, and that under the pressure of these events the party's course would change and one should expect to be brought into the leadership.

In this conversation ZINOVIEV clearly revealed that he was counting on difficulties arising in the Union in the form of uprisings against Soviet power, seeing this as a means of returning to the leadership.

In subsequent testimony I shall give further facts concerning my participation in the Zinovievist underground.

The record has been written correctly from my words and read by me.

P. ZALUTSKY.

INTERROGATED BY:

ASSISTANT HEAD OF THE EKO, GUGB, NKVD OF THE USSR — DMITRIEV.

Certified true: A. Svetlova
