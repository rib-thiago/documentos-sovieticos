STENOGRAPHIC TRANSCRIPT

INTERROGATION RECORD

OF NIKOLAEV, Leonid Vasilyevich

dated December 17, 1934.

QUESTION: Inform the investigation of your political views.

ANSWER: In 1925–1928 I did not openly belong to the opposition's ranks, but I must say that on a number of questions of Party policy I held the opposition's views: on the removal of leading opposition figures from the Party leadership and on overcoming economic difficulties.

In 1934, considering the state of affairs in the country, I believed that the main cause of all our difficulties was that the most prominent opposition figures — ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and others — had been removed from the Party leadership, and that the existing leadership headed by STALIN was pursuing an incorrect policy. Within the Party there is an extremely oppressive regime stifling all expressions of free thought. This led the Party to take the wrong path on the most important questions of domestic policy.

This assessment aroused great bitterness in each of us, the organization's members, first and foremost against STALIN. We could not expect changes in the Party leadership through methods of internal Party democracy; we realized that this path was entirely excluded. Hence only one path remained — the path of terrorist attacks.

In my “Answer Before the Party and the Fatherland,” I briefly formulated my views on the position of the working class under conditions in the Union as follows: “For us, the working people, there is no free access to life, work, or education.”

By this I wished to indicate the workers' difficult material situation in the country and the fact that access to culture and knowledge is obstructed for the worker.

Furthermore, I believed that the Soviet Government's policy of fines was taking us back to the practice of fines in prerevolutionary times. On this subject my document states briefly: “Everywhere, at the slightest pretext, as in former times, the threat of a fine hangs over us.”

On the state of the national economy, I asserted that there was a lack of planning in the country, leading to the decline of the productive forces. On transport, I pointed out that because of this lack of planning, transport was being used incorrectly, resulting in enormous waste and unproductive movements of trains.

I disagreed with the Party's and government's punitive policy, objected to the use of execution by shooting, and saw this practice as a means by which the Party leaders disposed of their political opponents. I believed that because of the Party's and government's incorrect policy, money circulated at a slow pace, resulting in a number of unhealthy phenomena within the national economy.

I pointed out that enormous failures in the economy were concealed by noise and empty talk about successes that did not actually exist. There is a suppression of self-criticism in the country, combined with an enormous growth of bureaucratism and the domination of the apparatus by alien elements.

On the question of war, I asserted that there was no danger of war for the Soviet Union, whereas the Party constantly pointed to an enormous threat of war. I regarded this agitation as a cover for diverting the broad working masses' attention from the difficult internal situation resulting from the incorrect policy of the Party Central Committee and the government. I believed that the investment of enormous resources in developing the military industry was taking place at the expense of pressure on the working masses and the deterioration of their material situation.

Assessing the general situation, I believed that there was a great gulf between the ruling elite and the masses.

Likening myself to RADISHCHEV, a figure in the liberation movement in Catherine II's time, I wrote that “his strength lay in his inability to remain indifferently silent on seeing abuses.” I compared the Soviet Government to the autocracy, saying: “Catherine cherished her power and rewarded favorites at her whim,” concluding that the same situation existed in the Union.

I prepared myself for decisive action and drew the direct conclusion that all means were good in the struggle against the Party leadership that had created such a regime.

Having embarked on recognizing terrorism as an acceptable means of political struggle, a view shared by my comrades with whom I jointly prepared and carried out the terrorist attack on KIROV, I believed that this path would be closed to all those who wished truly to struggle for a better future. I wrote: “Yes, many more brave people will be needed, ready to give themselves in the name of the historical mission. I am ready to do so for the sake of all humanity.”

When I fired at KIROV, I reasoned as follows: our shot should be the signal for an explosion, for actions within the country against the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and Soviet power.

I was not alone in my views; my comrades in the organization shared them.

Taken down correctly in shorthand from my words; I have read the interrogation record.

L. NIKOLAEV

INTERROGATED BY:

HEAD OF THE EKO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD OF THE USSR — MIRONOV
