INTERROGATION RECORD

Of GORBACHEV, Georgy Yefimovich,

dated January 3, 1935.

In response to the questions put to me by the investigation about the activities of the Zinovievist counterrevolutionary organization, I testify:

The Zinovievist counterrevolutionary organization, in which I am a participant and which took shape during the period of the 15th Party Congress, continued to exist until 1934.

Despite all the diversity in the character of the links between the organization’s members in the past and recently, the organization rested on the separation of the milieu of participants in the opposition of 1925–27, who held a non-party view of their past role; on the effort to “preserve” one another from “the party’s excessively stinging blows” against opposition participants; on special mutual trust and sympathy, and so forth.

This was action aimed at weakening the party’s blows against its enemies and had the character of an organization that weakened party unity, that is, an anti-party, anti-Soviet, counterrevolutionary organization.

Of the members of this organization, I personally had contacts during 1928–30 with M. YAKOVLEV, Vl. LEVIN, RUMYANTSEV, MOROZOV, NATANSON, M. LEVIN, and NOTMAN; in Moscow, with ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, GERTIK, and EVDOKIMOV.

During 1930–34 I maintained contact with the following members of this organization: 1) in Leningrad, YAKOVLEV, M., and TSVIBAK; I believe NOTMAN was a member (I shall testify separately about the other members of the Leningrad organization). 2) In Moscow, LELEVICH, SAFAROV, TATAROV, and ZAKS-GLADNEV. I believe NAUMOV was a member of the organization.

The forms of contact during this period consisted of mutual support among “people who had traveled the same path,” special trust in one another, which allowed them to discuss their disagreements with the party line on particular questions among themselves more frankly than with other party members. A mutual appraisal of one another as “people who, though mistaken, could be especially useful to the revolution,” supposedly often unjustly persecuted by the party and unable, because of the distrust toward them, to speak in the party with a full voice.

During this period contacts were maintained according to the principle, so to speak, of a “chain,” along the following lines: myself — M. YAKOVLEV; myself — TSVIBAK — TATAROV (the latter in Moscow); myself — LELEVICH; myself — LELEVICH — NAUMOV; myself — LELEVICH — VUJOVIC — VORONSKY (I met the latter in 1931–32); myself — KANATCHIKOV — M. KARPOV (all this in Moscow); myself — M. YAKOVLEV — T. VOLPE.

These contacts varied in character, from conversations about particular “shortcomings” of party policy to personal “mutual assistance in trouble” and “special sympathy,” not only along the lines of “acquaintance before the opposition” or common interests, but also of “mutual amnesty and general information.

Thus a closed milieu was created, old oppositionists’ ties were strengthened, and a negative attitude toward the party leadership was cultivated.

Specifically, for the present as examples: conversations between LELEVICH and me about the insufficient political sharpness of contemporary literature, supposedly caused by the party leadership of literature’s inability to “free” literary thought fettered by RAPP in the latest period; SAFAROV’s conversation with me in which, condemning the line on the united front of the French and Dutch Communist Parties, he spoke negatively about Western Communist Parties in general; a conversation between myself, YAKOVLEV, and SAFAROV about the writers’ congress, in which I spoke of the congress as general mutual praise, while SAFAROV spoke skeptically about the general character of contemporary literature.

Conversations in 1930 between myself, TSVIBAK, and NOTMAN about alleged disagreements within the Central Committee.

A conversation with T. ELKOVICH about her brother ELKOVICH’s long “exile” in the Urals.

My “special” sympathy for TATAROV on his expulsion from the party in 1933.

YAKOVLEV’s “sympathy” during the Control Commission’s consideration of TSVIBAK’s, NOTMAN’s, and my cases, or upon my expulsion from the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in 1932.

My conversations with T. VOLPE, on the one hand, and LELEVICH, on the other, about the “reprisals” against me as a former oppositionist, concerning my expulsion for Trotskyist contraband in 1932 (with which, incidentally, VOLPE disagreed).

KANATCHIKOV’s general feeling of resentment about his position in literature (in conversation with me).

Sometimes this was outwardly “innocent” in character: my delivery of books to EVDOKIMOV at YAKOVLEV’s request; questions from LELEVICH and me to VORONSKY about his exile, return to the party, and so forth; parties with the participation of former oppositionists — NAUMOV, LELEVICH, or DOMBSKY, LELEVICH, or VORONSKY, VUJOVIC, LELEVICH, or TATAROV, KARPOV, KANATCHIKOV — on the occasion of my arrival in Moscow; my and YAKOVLEV’s farewell to VOLPE on her departure for the Donbass — all this with literary and “friendly” conversations, but all this emphasized a special friendship of “former Leningraders,” not only on the basis of old acquaintances and common interests in literature or history but also of shared experiences and opposition.

All this recently bound together Zinovievists of different shades and created a milieu alien to the party, which served as a condenser of sentiments hostile to the party’s organizational and political line.

All this, as well as the insincerity of every kind of “disarmament” in 1927–1928 and the insufficient exposure and stigmatization of one’s own past, nourished counterrevolutionary ideas and sentiments in the ranks of former oppositionists and led certain elements of the opposition to direct counterrevolutionary actions counting on broad sympathy throughout the former opposition milieu, and to the vile murder of S. M. KIROV by NIKOLAEV, who came from the Zinovievist opposition.

For all the consequences of their counterrevolutionary activity, all Zinovievists who preserved their ties bear full political, moral, and legal responsibility, myself included, and by no means least, as someone who did much in practice for the “moral” and political rapprochement of former oppositionists of 1925–27, who preserved their anti-party, counterrevolutionary organizational and ideological identity until the most recent period.

Written in my own hand — GORBACHEV.

INTERROGATED BY:

HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE UGB — LULOV.
