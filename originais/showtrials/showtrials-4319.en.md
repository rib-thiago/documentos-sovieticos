INTERROGATION RECORD

Of Mikhail Petrovich STREMYAKOV

dated January 7, 1935.

STREMYAKOV, Mikhail Petrovich, born in 1903, native of Leningrad, father a military medical orderly (deceased). Resides in Moscow, 3rd Neopalimovsky Lane, building No. 13, apartment 3. Editor of the newspaper “For Communications Cadres,” Podbelsky Engineering and Technical Academy of Communications. Wife Elizaveta Vasilievna STREMYAKOVA, nonparty, does not work anywhere; daughter Tatyana, aged 4½; mother Alexandra Konstantinovna STREMYAKOVA in Moscow, 29 Vorovsky Street, apartment 12; brother Evgeny Petrovich STREMYAKOV, nonparty, a contributor to the journal “Samolyot,” lives at the same address; brother Boris Petrovich STREMYAKOV, member of the AUCP(b), deputy director of the 12th special sector, lives somewhere on Petrovka. Property: owns nothing. Secondary education. Member of the AUCP(b) since 1930, candidate since 1925; in 1926 expelled from AUCP(b) candidate membership by the Far Eastern Territory Control Commission for an anti-Party act (concealing a factional document from the Party); in 1929 reinstated as a candidate by the Central Control Commission and six months later admitted to AUCP(b) membership. No convictions. I am not on the military reserve register, under Article 66.

To the investigation's questions about my participation in the Zinovievist organization, I testify:

I was drawn into the Zinovievist opposition in 1925 during my work in Leningrad by O. TARKHANOV. At different times until 1928 I came into contact with active oppositionists N. TSARKOV, A. BATASHOV, L. FAIVILOVICH, Ya. TSEITLIN, and I. TARASOV; I met SAFAROV and EVDOKIMOV once each.

In 1926 I was working in Khabarovsk; there I met the oppositionists N. TSARKOV, P. REPNIKOV, TUNTUL, N. OSIPOV, and N. RADIN. I remember that TSARKOV gave me a factional letter by EVDOKIMOV, and I was warned that if I betrayed him, TSARKOV, things would go badly for me. The territorial control commission learned of the letter (I showed it to one AUCP(b) member); I did not betray TSARKOV and deceived the Party by declaring that I had no letter and never had one, and for this I was expelled from AUCP(b) candidate membership (I passed the letter to TUNTUL's wife).

Arriving in Moscow from the Far Eastern Territory, I again fell into the oppositionists' milieu. I most often visited TARKHANOV and visited BATASHOV a couple of times. I was not a convinced oppositionist, but, as I have already testified, I was close to a number of Zinovievists. In associating with them, I constantly heard the most hostile conversations directed against the Party leadership. I heard Comrade STALIN called “Bonaparte,” heard it said that “he is leading the Party toward a split,” and so forth. This constant abuse, vile slander, and provocation against Comrade STALIN gave rise in my youthful head to the proposal to “start acting with dynamite,” which I put forward in a conversation with comrades in 1927 at A. BATASHOV's apartment. At that time it seemed to me that this proposal to act by radical, forceful means fully accorded with what I heard from older opposition comrades about the Party leadership. Neither TARASOV nor BATASHOV supported me, and I never spoke of it to anyone again.

QUESTION: Tell us about your ties with the Zinovievists after the Fifteenth Party Congress.

ANSWER: After the Fifteenth Congress I met O. TARKHANOV more often. I remember going to see him after his telephone call before his departure for Kazan; SAFAROV was there. No conversations took place in my presence. Once TARKHANOV (before his departure for Kazan) asked me to arrange a room for a meeting. Under the pretext of a party, I sent my mother out of the room, and a meeting took place there attended by TARKHANOV, SAFAROV, VARDIN, and another three or four persons whose surnames I do not know. Seeing that my presence embarrassed them, I went to the kitchen and returned only toward the end of the meeting; I remember this fragment of SAFAROV's speech: “Thus we are all withdrawing with you to the third line of trenches.”

I know that after the Fifteenth Congress the Zinovievists' headquarters was at KOPYLOV's apartment (next to the State Bank), where TARKHANOV also lived.

KOPYLOV's and TARKHANOV's wives typed some documents. I remember that on TARKHANOV's instructions I took a suitcase containing some documents to a building next to the People's Commissariat of Defense (I do not remember the building or apartment number). O. TARKHANOV told me then that “the Party no longer exists,” that STALIN, MOLOTOV, and VOROSHILOV were governing the country, making counterrevolutionary attacks against them.

From 1929 to 1934 I met O. TARKHANOV and D. G. BAR<…>V (a Trotskyist) several times, and VARDIN twice. There were no anti-Party conversations between us; nevertheless, I consider it necessary to inform the investigation of the following: in a conversation with me in the summer of 1934, VARDIN asked me this question: “Is the muzhik satisfied with Soviet power?” I enthusiastically cited several facts from the prosperous life of collective farmers, after which VARDIN asked another question that rather surprised me: “What will the muzhiks do if the imperialists declare war on us?”

While in the opposition, I did not understand the meaning of the differences, and they did not trust me with much; I was an errand boy, yet the poison of Zinovievism poisoned me; as a result, I frequently observed a divided state within myself. A sense of grievance haunted me, and I sometimes made not merely anti-Party but outright counterrevolutionary attacks against the Party. For example, when the newspapers recently published a report on the administrative exile of the Zinovievist opposition's leaders, I made a most vile, provocative attack against the Party: I compared these effective Party measures for the final exposure and defeat of the actual inspirers of Comrade KIROV's murder to the fascist provocation of the Reichstag fire.

STREMYAKOV.

INTERROGATED BY: KORKIN.
