INTERROGATION RECORD

OF SAFAROV Georgy Ivanovich, 31 December 1934.

On the questions put to me by the investigation, I testify:

Artyom GERTIK — for a short time after the Fifteenth Party Congress belonged to the anti-Soviet group of the so-called “leaderless” oppositionists and sheltered me in his apartment on the premises of a cinema on the Arbat when I was liable to immediate deportation. He then joined the Zinovievist organization, becoming one of its principal organizers and a central figure in liaison. Until his exile to Kazakhstan in 1932–early 1933, his apartment served almost openly as the Zinovievists' headquarters. All Zinovievists arriving in Moscow from elsewhere constantly visited him first. He frequently traveled to Leningrad for communications with local Zinovievists and to convey instructions. In particular, he maintained especially close contact with Vladimir LEVIN. All the non-Moscow Zinovievists one encountered — Nikolai DMITRIEV, S. GESSEN, TIMOFEEV and GORDON from Rostov, TOLMAZOV, V. RUMYANTSEV and ANISHEV — always reported that they had already visited GERTIK, who shared “news” of the “situation in the Central Committee, the leaders' sentiments and views.” GERTIK's anti-party and anti-Soviet directives as a hundred-percent Zinovievist were widely known; as late as 1934 MAGYAR said with great sympathy: “although GERTIK has suffered harshly, he continues to live solely for Zinovievist interests.”

BAKAEV Ivan Petrovich — one of the Zinovievist organization's principal and permanent leaders, always extremely hostile to the party leadership, both in 1932 when I once met him at the Kremlin Hospital and subsequently until 1934. After Artyom GERTIK's exile, his apartment, according to MAGYAR and the GERTSBERGs, became the Zinovievists' meeting place in Moscow. According to MAGYAR's testimony, a meeting there discussed how to react to G. ZINOVIEV's expulsion. He met EVDOKIMOV almost daily, and KUKLIN somewhat less but quite frequently; he considered himself a Zinovievist “pillar,” and ZINOVIEV himself encouraged BAKAEV's closeness in every way, lavishing sugary praise on this politically very limited supporter. BAKAEV considered himself and EVDOKIMOV the guarantee of the “leaders' political steadfastness.” He was convinced that precisely he and EVDOKIMOV sustained the “courage” of “leaders” always as prone to faintheartedness as to struggle against the party and Soviet power. For most Zinovievists in and outside Moscow, BAKAEV was always a principal support. GORSHENIN, who performed all kinds of assignments for him and EVDOKIMOV, was especially close to BAKAEV.

Yakov SHAROV — an active Zinovievist, constantly in a trio with EVDOKIMOV and BAKAEV, maintaining “contact” with them primarily through the Council of People's Commissars dining hall. He frequently visited Leningrad and met Zinovievists connected with textile enterprises, particularly Krasnaya Nit, as I know from conversations with Zinovievists, though I cannot recall with whom personally. Meeting me at the Kremlin Hospital in 1933, he expressed malicious indignation at being held responsible for some economic offense, characterizing this as “persecution of former oppositionists,” “arranged from above.” ZINOVIEV always spoke of SHAROV as his “loyal man.”

Grigory FEDOROV — an old Zinovievist who invariably enjoyed ZINOVIEV's great trust. In 1931 and again in 1933 I visited him on personal business (once seeking bicycle accessories, when he chaired Glavshveimashina, and once seeking maps of the Turkestan Range at Geokartprom). Both times he persistently asked whether I had seen ZINOVIEV, saying: “I have not seen Grigory for a couple of weeks.” All indications suggest that he visited ZINOVIEV quite frequently. He was connected with EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and SHAROV, primarily through the Council of People's Commissars dining hall.

BASHKIROV — a Leningrad Zinovievist who also worked at Leningradskaya Pravda, a close friend of BRAVO, one of GERTIK's “aides-de-camp,” who visited him in Leningrad. As I recall, BRAVO spoke of these meetings with BASHKIROV when I encountered him at NAUMOV's in either 1932 or 1933.

ANISHEV — a Leningrad Zinovievist, husband of M. NATANSON. Worked in Central Asia and then Leningrad. Meeting me at the Congress of Agrarians in 1930, he said they had a “Zinovievist colony” in Tashkent, mentioning himself and Mikhail TSVIBAK. Visiting me in Moscow in mid-1932, he expressed counterrevolutionary views, prophesying accumulating economic difficulties and a rupture with the peasantry, and declaring that the party had no line. On every visit to Moscow, he said, he visited GERTIK and could not have been unaware of M. NATANSON's counterrevolutionary activity. In conversation with me in the second half of 1932, NATANSON spoke of ANISHEV as a Zinovievist.

Vasily LUKIN — a Trotskyist, close friend of V. RUMYANTSEV and KOTOLYNOV, exiled and subsequently, apparently in 1932, returned to Leningrad. In 1933, when visiting me at the Comintern, TOLMAZOV and SEREDOKHIN described his views, sharply hostile to the party and Soviet power, characterizing him as having gone entirely over to the Trotskyists. LUKIN frequented S. MANDELSHTAM's company; he is an extremely degenerate person politically and personally, capable of the vilest actions. He was a Trotskyist in 1923 as well.

KARPOV — a Trotskyist, Trotsky's plenipotentiary representative in Leningrad during the anti-party struggle of 1925–1927. Always distinguished by extreme devotion to his counterrevolutionary leader, he continued underground counterrevolutionary struggle against the party after the Fifteenth Congress in 1928, when he was arrested and exiled. Apparently he returned to Leningrad in 1931. RUMYANTSEV mentioned this when meeting me at the Communist International in 1931, in a manner suggesting that he had met KARPOV. KARPOV is unquestionably an incorrigible counterrevolutionary Trotskyist who fought the party with the utmost bitterness from 1928 onward.

Moisei YAKOVLEV — led the anti-party struggle in the Vasileostrov District in 1925–1927. In 1928–1931 he belonged to the Zinovievist group in Leningrad, according to V. RUMYANTSEV. I met him from 1934 in Leningrad and understood from conversations that his relations with the Zinovievists had cooled and, according to him, he did not see them.

Written in my own hand — G. SAFAROV.

Interrogated by:

Head of the 1st Section of the SPO, GUGB, NKVD of the USSR — PETROVSKY.
