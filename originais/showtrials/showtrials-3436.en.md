INTERROGATION RECORD

Of Ivan Stepanovich GORSHENIN

December 25, 1934.

QUESTION: In your testimony of 21/XII of this year, you indicated that throughout its existence, until its liquidation in December of this year, the Moscow Zinovievist center maintained a critical attitude toward decisions of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and did not recognize the party leadership. Describe in greater detail the political directives guiding your organization's center and the goals it pursued.

ANSWER: Supplementing my testimony of 21/XII of this year, in which I already informed the investigation of a number of facts concerning the counterrevolutionary directives of the Moscow Zinovievist center, expressed at different times by center members KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, SHAROV and chiefly G. E. ZINOVIEV himself, I must report the following:

Although the Moscow center of the Zinovievist organization had no definite program of its own to counterpose to the party's general line, its critical attitude toward Central Committee decisions on most questions of domestic and foreign policy nevertheless created unity of opinion among our organization's members and an entire system of antiparty, counterrevolutionary views.

Every fact, every piece of information about achievements in any field, whether politics, economics, culture or international relations, was subjected to doubt and critical assessment.

We considered the figures for the results of the five-year plan in four years strained and manipulated, and considered the plan unfulfilled in most fields, such as pig iron, rolled metal, consumer goods, textiles, food products, livestock and railway transport.

Since we declared the listed sections of the five-year plan decisive for the entire national economy, we considered the five-year plan to have failed hopelessly.

Even where the five-year plan was fulfilled (sown areas, gross grain harvests), the methods of calculation and quality of the indicators were questioned. We believed that not all sown areas could be counted, since plowing was done to count hectares rather than to obtain a harvest; that gross grain harvests had begun to be calculated in a new way, counting the crop standing in the fields instead of the actual grain collected in the barns. Moreover, we considered that under the present policy the government had gone so far as to squander all reserves (the entire stock) of selected seed, and that a decade would now be required to restore that stock.

We took a critical and skeptical attitude toward the decisions of the Seventeenth Party Conference and Seventeenth Party Congress on building a classless socialist society during the second five-year plan.

We denied the socialist character of the great majority of collective farms on the grounds that, in our opinion, famine prevailed in the country and collective farmers were starving (1932–33), plowing with cows and liable to disperse at any moment.

Where the achievements of socialist construction were too obvious (for example, Dneprostroy, Magnitka, the Kuznetsk Metallurgical Plant and others), a new point was advanced: excessive, unproductive costs entailing wholly unjustified privations and suffering for the working masses.

It was said that enormous financial and material resources were being thrown away because people did not know how to build, projects were not thought through and preliminary preparation for construction was not carried out. Hence the many mismatches and disproportions in construction. For example, a metallurgical plant was being built while mining facilities were unprepared; transport construction in the country lagged sharply behind industrial construction, hence new and additional difficulties that could have been avoided under different leadership.

We drew the conclusion that the present leadership was unfit from every situation.

We attached great importance to housing construction and provision for workers' material and everyday needs. We believed that the Central Committee ignored these fields, and numerous facts were cited as illustrations: workers were kept in barracks and dugouts, in insanitary conditions, while provision for their material and everyday needs lagged sharply behind workers' output norms, which were systematically increased.

Phenomena such as falling labor productivity among Donbass workers were characterized by ZINOVIEV as a form of workers' protest against the present party leadership.

Criticism of the Central Committee's international policy proceeded chiefly, as I have already testified, along the lines of condemning Comintern activities.

Comintern policy was characterized as “inactive” and “sluggish,” and conversations invariably ended in attacks, corresponding to our outlook, on the leadership and personally on Comrade STALIN.

Underlying our criticism of the Central Committee's international policy was the premise that Comrade STALIN deliberately failed to intensify Comintern activity, shifted the entire focus of attention to the official diplomacy of the People's Commissariat for Foreign Affairs, and essentially sacrificed the interests of world revolution to the idea of building socialism in one country. The fundamental basis of all our criticism of the Central Committee line, and consequently of the present party leadership, was disbelief in the possibility of building socialism in one country, preserved from the old platform of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc.

The goal of our Zinovievist organization was, by gathering forces and preparing suitable conditions within the party, to secure the return to leadership of our organization's leaders, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV.

QUESTION: The investigation materials in this case show that your organization's center possessed information about the condition and work of the Comintern. State exactly from whom this information was received.

ANSWER: Information about Comintern work was received chiefly from Ludwig Ignatievich MAGYAR, who systematically met ZINOVIEV personally and conveyed information concerning Comintern activities and the condition of the fraternal Communist parties. He kept ZINOVIEV informed of events in individual countries and even of the state of affairs and personnel in the Comintern apparatus.

I know personally that MAGYAR visited ZINOVIEV not only at his apartment but also at his workplace, in the editorial offices of “BOLSHEVIK.”

I have personally known of ZINOVIEV's close connection with MAGYAR since 1932, when ZINOVIEV introduced me to MAGYAR, from whom I subsequently obtained foreign newspapers and journals to forward to ZINOVIEV in Kustanai.

Incidentally, on one occasion, around August of this year (1934), I was personally present at ZINOVIEV's apartment when MAGYAR reported on the situation within the Comintern apparatus in connection with Comrade DIMITROV's arrival and preparations for the forthcoming congress. He then reported on the attitudes that had developed among Comrades KNORIN, PYATNITSKY and MANUILSKY. In addition, MAGYAR described the attitudes of certain leading officials from fraternal Communist parties who interested ZINOVIEV to one degree or another.

QUESTION: What do you know about the Moscow Zinovievist center's connections with other cities of the USSR (apart from Leningrad), with whom personally were these connections maintained, and what did they consist of?

ANSWER: The following persons were connected with our organization's Moscow center recently:

1. Olga RAVICH — Voronezh, head of a food-industry institution. She came to Moscow and met ZINOVIEV personally.

2. Nikolai GORDON — Rostov-on-Don. During visits to Moscow he met GERTIK, KUKLIN and myself. We exchanged information about the internal party situation. He is chairman of the Regional Bureau of the Red Cross Society.

3. Yakov Rafailovich ELKOVICH [1] — Sverdlovsk. During his visits to Moscow he usually stayed with me; he met BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV and KUKLIN. ELKOVICH is editor of the regional collective-farm newspaper.

4. Ivan Petrovich KAPITONOV — Novosibirsk. During his visits he met only me. KAPITONOV is deputy chairman of the regional planning body.

5. Yakov Abramovich FURTICHEV — Gorky. In Moscow he met me. He is a professor at local higher educational institutions (in philosophy).

6. Mechislav ROZNER — Arkhangelsk. During visits to Moscow he met KUKLIN and me. ROZNER is chairman of the Northern and Territorial Planning Commission.

7. Sergei Mikhailovich GESSEN — Smolensk. During his frequent visits to Moscow he always met EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, Sergei ZORIN, Vasily DYAKOV and me. He is the People's Commissariat for Heavy Industry's representative for the Western Region.

8. Aleksandr KOROLEV — former director of the aluminum plant. I do not know his present whereabouts. He was personally connected with Grigory Eremeevich EVDOKIMOV.

9. VUYOVICH — Tashkent. During his visits to Moscow he met ZORIN, NAUMOV and me.

All the persons listed above are former prominent participants in the Zinovievist opposition who continued to maintain ideological and organizational connections with the Moscow center until December of this year.

I sign to confirm the correctness of my testimony; I have read the record.

GORSHENIN.

INTERROGATED BY: DEPUTY HEAD OF THE SPO OF THE UGB — STROMIN.
