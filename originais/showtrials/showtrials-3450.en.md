INTERROGATION RECORD

Of SAFAROV G. I., December 26, 1934.

To the questions put to me by the investigation, I testify:

In 1932, in the summer as I remember, MAGYAR told me about his conversation with a Comintern official, KREPS, who was in charge of the foreign workers' publishing house. According to him, KREPS told him that the situation in the country was desperate, that the Central Committee had no line, that the workers' situation was extremely difficult, but that nothing could be said. MAGYAR cited this conversation as an example that “the most rock-solid are wavering.” I also consider it necessary to report that in March 1928, Ilya BYALY, who still works in Irkutsk, sent to me in Achinsk a representative of the group of oppositionist railway workers in Irkutsk. I do not remember his surname, but he was an elderly worker of nearly fifty, of medium height, stocky, and broad-shouldered. Most of this group did not return to the Party and were infected with sharply anti-Party and anti-Soviet sentiments. Subsequently, BYALY visited me several times in Moscow; in 1932, when I expounded my anti-Party views to him, he broke with me.

I met KHARITONOV in the summer of 1933 at the dacha by Otdykh station. He lived at the neighboring dacha. In conversation, he complained about the position of “former oppositionists”; I made several anti-Party remarks about Comintern affairs. KHARITONOV agreed, and the conversation ended there.

Nikolay DMITRIEV, who previously worked in Saratov and subsequently secured his return to Leningrad, undoubtedly belonged and belongs among the active Zinovievists. During visits to Moscow, as I know, he constantly called on EVDOKIMOV and others.

Vladimir LEVIN carried out the work of an active Zinovievist organizer in Leningrad. V. RUMYANTSEV was connected with him. By all indications, he was the contact person for most Zinovievists in Leningrad.

Viktor BULAKH, from the group of Zinovievists who were former Komsomol members, is also a participant in the Zinovievist organization, always blindly believing in his “leader.”

As I remember, when TOLMAZOV and SEREDOKHIN visited me at the Comintern in 1933, SEREDOKHIN said of GREMZHE, a participant in the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc, that he was “on the old positions”

In M. NATANSON's group, GEDVABNY \[1\] and ZHULIN played an active role, distinguishing themselves by their especially anti-Party sentiments and views; in 1928, they were extremely indignant at the capitulation and disintegration of the “leaderless” group.

Andrey MUSHTAKOV, S. GESSEN's constant drinking companion and accomplice in his counterrevolutionary outpourings during drinking bouts, degenerate both politically and in his personal life. “Officially,” he stood for the Party, but in fact all his drunken, rowdy meetings with GESSEN, often mentioned in Zinovievist conversations, were usually accompanied by counterrevolutionary chatter, readings of GESSEN's lampoons, and the like.

In 1932, the Urals Trotskyists Sergey BARANOV and Matvey BELYAEV were connected with MRACHKOVSKY. I saw the former in mid-1932 at UFIMTSEV's apartment, where I had been invited by MRACHKOVSKY for a meeting. He complained, in a counterrevolutionary spirit, about the “oppression of the peasantry.” —

Written in my own hand. — G. SAFAROV.

Interrogated by:

DEPUTY HEAD OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE USSR NKVD — LYUSHKOV

HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE USSR NKVD — PETROVSKY
