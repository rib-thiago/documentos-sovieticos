CONFRONTATION RECORD

Between ZINOVIEV Grigory Evseevich and KHARITONOV Moisei Markovich.

Dated January 11, 1935.

Question to KHARITONOV: What do you know about the composition of the Moscow center of the Zinovievist organization and about ZINOVIEV's role in it?

Answer: ZINOVIEV played a leading role in this center. Besides ZINOVIEV, it included KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, SHAROV, KUKLIN and, I think, GORSHENIN as well, although he did not belong to the center that existed before the Fifteenth Congress. I personally also belonged to the center until 1931.

Question to KHARITONOV: What do you know about the existence of the Moscow center in the subsequent period?

Answer: Personally at that time I stopped attending the center's meetings, so I cannot say precisely, but I know, for example, that EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and oth\<ers\> were connected with the same anti-party work after 1931 as well.

Question to ZINOVIEV: What can you say regarding KHARITONOV's testimony?

Answer: Before the Fifteenth Congress and, in particular, during the Kaluga period, the group of persons KHARITONOV named was indeed the center of a fighting anti-party faction. After the Fifteenth Congress it was already a disintegrating group, bound together by the old struggle, partly by personal ties and partly by the revival of one or another hope for discussion within the party.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Answer the substance of the question put to you: do you acknowledge that after the Fifteenth Congress there existed a center of the organization and that both KHARITONOV himself and the persons he—KHARITONOV—listed belonged to that center?

Answer: Before the Fifteenth Congress we had a genuine center of a fighting anti-party faction. It was in alliance with the Trotskyists. The center's composition was approximately that which KHARITONOV has just announced. During the Kaluga period it was already something different. Of course I do not mean that it was something good, since in essence it was a continuation of work of an anti-party character.

Question to ZINOVIEV: This group did not dissolve the underground?

Answer: After the Fifteenth Congress there were individual ties. In 1932, as I have already testified, a “revival” occurred also in the leadership group KHARITONOV named.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm your testimony that in 1928–1929 there existed a counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization?

Answer: I do not, of course, mean that we were completely free of counterrevolutionary views at that time, but there was a great difference. Until 1929 it was an organization, whereas now it was not what could be called a real center.

Question to ZINOVIEV: The testimony of EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and SHAROV establishes that the center existed not only before the Fifteenth Party Congress but also in 1928, 1929 and until recently. Do you confirm the existence of the Moscow center until recently?

Answer: Our group could be regarded as a center; it could be regarded as a group morally representing a certain organization of f\<ormer\> participants in the Leningrad opposition, not without a definite permanent membership.

Question to KHARITONOV: Tell us what you know about the Moscow center's connection with the counterrevolutionary organization in Leningrad.

Answer: I remember the following individual facts: once, at a meeting at ZINOVIEV's, where besides him and myself several other people were present, someone reported that in Leningrad our like-minded associates were carrying on work while maintaining connections with the districts, and the discussion chiefly concerned young people, and that this connection took the form of a “chain.” This was either in late 1929 or in 1930.

I remember someone relating—and this greatly interested ZINOVIEV—that a group of young people who had withdrawn during the Fifteenth Congress period were returning to us, and that contact had been established between these two sections, that is, the section which had followed us all along and the section which had followed SAFAROV. Among the people who had succeeded in organizing this, LEVIN and YAKOVLEV Moisei were named at the time. Among the young people, I remember, RUMYANTSEV Vladimir was mentioned.

I also remember a conversation among a smaller group: ZINOVIEV spoke about the return to Leningrad of f\<ormer\> participants in the Zinovievist opposition who had previously been expelled from Leningrad. ZINOVIEV viewed this fact with joy and, as we all understood at the time, the political meaning of this assessment was that here, in Leningrad, cadres were accumulating who would be useful for the anti-party struggle when a “suitable situation” arose. I tried to recall dates from the facts. People who had previously been transferred out of Leningrad spent two to two and a half years in the provinces, so this conversation about their return to Leningrad belongs to 1930–1931. RUMYANTSEV's return to Leningrad was particularly emphasized by ZINOVIEV.

Everyone's opinion of RUMYANTSEV, and ZINOVIEV's first and foremost, was very good; it was considered that if it became necessary to enter an open struggle against the party, this man would play a great role, since he was resolute, a man of deeds rather than words.

Of course, the work was not conceived as it had been before the congress; rather, it was envisaged as one-to-one meetings assessing chiefly the political life of the country and the party leadership, in particular STALIN, in an anti-party, counterrevolutionary sense.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm KHARITONOV's testimony?

Answer: I may have spoken about rapprochement with former “leaderless” young people. At that time this could have been said in connection with the appearance of the Right; there were great hopes that a discussion would begin, and I probably did speak that way, especially as some rapprochement with the “leaderless” did take place. This may have been in 1929. As for the return to Leningrad of f\<ormer\> opposition participants, the very fact of their return to Leningrad might have caused joy on my part, particularly in the case of RUMYANTSEV, but I do not remember and do not even know whether he had left. In general terms I may have spoken of the return as a fact I assessed positively. I think this was discussed in 1929, because afterward it interested me little.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm KHARITONOV's testimony that in 1930–1931 you spoke to him about the accumulation of forces in Leningrad for the anti-party struggle?

Answer: In that form I cannot confirm it. I regarded the return to Leningrad of f\<ormer\> participants in the so-called Zinovievist opposition as a positive fact in the sense that, if there were a discussion, which I hoped for, these people would help us.

Question to KHARITONOV: You testified to the investigation that the Zinovievist organization and ZINOVIEV systematically spread slanderous rumors against the party leaders, educated the organization's members in a spirit hostile to the leadership, and that all this generated terrorist attitudes among the organization's participants. Do you confirm this?

Answer: Yes, I confirm this testimony. It happened as follows: every time I met ZINOVIEV and we spoke on party subjects, in most cases ZINOVIEV took a sharply negative attitude toward party decisions and the party leadership, and always at the end of the conversation ZINOVIEV spoke very sharply, slanderously—to speak honestly, vilely slanderously—and provocatively about the leadership, especially Comrade STALIN. I remember an occasion when I spoke with ZINOVIEV on the question of collectivization. At that moment ZINOVIEV approved the line of collectivizing the countryside, saying that this was precisely what we Zinovievists had long proposed, and then, turning to STALIN and the leadership generally, gave them the most hostile, counterrevolutionary assessment. I said to ZINOVIEV: “How is this to be understood? Fifteen minutes ago you were talking about collectivization and approving it, and now you suddenly attack STALIN and the leadership sharply?”

In one of my conversations with ZINOVIEV in 1930, at the height of the collectivization of the countryside, he said that the present party leadership was costing the country very dearly, and that we Zinovievists would never have carried out collectivization that way.

Regarding the assessment of the Comintern's position, ZINOVIEV repeatedly said that there was no leadership there, that the Comintern was neglected, that this organization was not receiving the attention it deserved; on this question, as always, he indulged in the most bitter counterrevolutionary attacks against Comrade STALIN.

In 1931 ZINOVIEV said to me at his apartment: “Well, damn it, are we Bolsheviks or not? How long are we going to keep silent?”

Such bitter counterrevolutionary conversations were an everyday occurrence during my meetings with ZINOVIEV. Through counterrevolutionary conversations ZINOVIEV cultivated among the organization's members a sharply hostile attitude toward the party leaders, especially Comrade STALIN. And it is no accident that the Leningrad counterrevolutionary organization, which was the principal base in ZINOVIEV's struggle against the party, sank to betrayal of the proletarian revolution and to outright fascist methods of struggle against the party leadership. This is a direct consequence of the activity of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization and of ZINOVIEV himself, who displayed an acutely hostile attitude toward the party leadership and thereby inflamed terrorist attitudes among the organization's participants.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm KHARITONOV's testimony?

Answer: On collectivization and the attitude toward the leadership. Of course, I do not conceal that my attitude was very ambivalent; I cannot conceal that. That the bitterest expressions were used in this connection is true, I confirm it, but all this was diminishing. This is explained, of course, by my being a person of inconclusive decisions. Everyone knows that. I find it difficult to recall dates. When collectivization began, I really may have spoken in the spirit of KHARITONOV's testimony, that is, that we had long proposed this, and so on. At the same time I must say that when I went to the North Caucasus (Kislovodsk—1930), EVDOKIMOV was there too; I observed for myself, and EVDOKIMOV told me much about what was happening in the countryside. Under the impression of all this I wrote an article, dating to 1930, from which it is apparent that my line coincided with the CC's line and that the whole matter, in my opinion at the time as well, concerned only excesses.

Question to KHARITONOV: What else can you testify about ZINOVIEV's counterrevolutionary activity?

Answer: I remember one fact: when it emerged that KAMENEV's record with BUKHARIN had fallen into the CC's hands, BOGDAN, ZINOVIEV's secretary, summoned me and conveyed ZINOVIEV's instruction that I take part in investigating how this document could have disappeared from KAMENEV's desk.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm KHARITONOV's testimony?

Answer: In connection with this affair we had a joking conversation: “It would not be a bad idea for us Zinovievists to have our own TsKK.”

Question to KHARITONOV: Was the assignment to investigate the disappearance of the documents given to you jokingly or seriously?

Answer: Entirely seriously, since BOGDAN specially summoned me to his apartment about it.

Question to ZINOVIEV: Do you confirm that you gave KHARITONOV the assignment to investigate the disappearance of documents from KAMENEV?

Answer: Yes, I do.

My answers to the investigation's questions have been recorded correctly.

KHARITONOV.

My answers to the investigation's questions have been recorded correctly.

ZINOVIEV.
