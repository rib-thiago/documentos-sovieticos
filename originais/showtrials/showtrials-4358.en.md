INTERROGATION RECORD

Of KAMENEV, Lev Borisovich, January 9, 1935.

To the questions put to me by the investigation, I reply:

I acknowledge that, at the time of drafting and submitting the statement to the Fifteenth Congress, neither I nor my like-minded associates of that time stood on the Party's standpoint or acknowledged the unconditional correctness of the Party line and Party leadership in the struggle against our group. On the contrary. Acknowledging our “submission” to the Party in our statement, we actually hoped that our “line” would ultimately triumph and that we would yet return to the leadership of the Party. Since we concealed from the Party this conviction in the correctness of our line and these hopes of returning to the leadership, I acknowledge this conduct to have been double-dealing and deeply hostile to the Party's interests. From this followed the preservation of old contacts, hopes that the struggle within the Party leadership would facilitate our group's return to the leadership, and, finally, the desire to establish contacts with any anti-Party grouping insofar as its struggle weakened the unity and authority of the Party leadership.

Along the three lines indicated, I can present the investigation with the following facts, relying, unfortunately, exclusively on memory, which is weak in organizational matters:

1\) The persons who signed the statement of the “23,” at the Fifteenth Congress and after the congress, continued to regard themselves as a distinct group with a common fate and mutual responsibility, sought to maintain contact with one another, and sought to work out common viewpoints on current political questions. These tendencies were particularly strong and alive immediately after the Fifteenth Congress, during ZINOVIEV's and my stay in Kaluga, and immediately after our return from Kaluga. Almost everyone who signed the statement of the “23” visited us in Kaluga, as did many of those who refused to sign it, the so-called “Leftists.” Heated arguments took place over whether we had acted correctly in submitting the statement, whether the bloc with TROTSKY and then the break with him toward the end of the congress had been correct; efforts were made to collect information about the mood and opinions of the Party leadership and, on that basis, determine what prospects awaited us. It was taken for granted that the leading role in all this work belonged to the group that had led the “Leningrad opposition” from the Fourteenth to the Fifteenth Congress, that is, ZINOVIEV, myself, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, and others. Work of this kind continued after we moved from Kaluga to Moscow in mid-1928. To maintain contacts, exchange information, and work out a common viewpoint on current questions, “seminars” were organized: one political, which ZINOVIEV was to lead, and another economic, which I led. They did, it is true, soon fall apart, and mine met no more than two or three times, but contact continued to be maintained through smaller and larger gatherings. Admittedly, I cannot now recall any meeting at which general political questions or a general political line were discussed, but every event concerning one or another member of our group — an appointment, a move to another city, a proposed speech — was discussed. I remember, for example, a number of meetings connected with ZALUTSKY's expulsion, with the Central Control Commission's accusation against ZINOVIEV of negotiations with a certain CHERNY, and with our proposed appearance at the Sixteenth Congress. Of discussions of more general questions, I remember that a number of meetings were prompted by the tactics of the Right (BUKHARIN and others) and our negotiations with them. All this activity was intensive in 1928–1929. From 1930, it seems to me, it began noticeably to subside and die away. I shall indicate below the reasons for this impression of mine.

For now I shall merely point out that the two facts indicated to me by the investigation also belong to this kind of activity: a meeting of a group of Leningraders with me during my visit to Leningrad in 1928, and a discussion with RUMYANTSEV of his application for admission to the Party, which also dates to 1928 but, in my opinion, was later. As for the meeting in Leningrad, it took place not on my initiative but at the insistence of the Leningraders. As far as I remember, they came to me to declare that they continued to disagree with the tactics of the “23,” that the “23” had “betrayed” the platform, that we had “roused” them to struggle and then “abandoned” them, and so on. I argued with them, tried to convince them of the need to submit to the Party, and pointed out that they were in fact crossing into TROTSKY's camp, but they remained dissatisfied with my arguments and left, it seems to me, discontented. I found this meeting very unpleasant and tried to shorten it as much as possible. It lasted no more than an hour. There were five or six people. It was unpleasant to me because I was already very “Right”-minded at the time, and arguments with young “Leftists” seemed fruitless and uninteresting to me. Essentially, I reacted just as indifferently when, a few weeks later (or a couple of months — I do not remember), RUMYANTSEV called on me in Moscow and said that he had come to Moscow to submit an application to the Central Control Commission to return to the Party. Naturally, I approved his decision. I do not remember whether I made any amendments to the text of his application on that occasion.

2\) After the ideological and organizational defeat at the Fifteenth Congress, our group — and I in particular — by no means immediately concluded that our political role in the Party was over once and for all. On the contrary. For a long time we continued to live under the illusion that the Party would still need us, precisely in leading positions, and that the Party would “call upon” us. In the arguments with TROTSKY at the end of the Fifteenth Congress, it became clear to us that his “irreconcilable” position rested precisely on the conviction that he had nothing more to do in that Party, that his role in it was over. We, however, were convinced that we were still destined to play a leading political role in the Party that had just defeated us but had not finally closed its doors to us.

This hope rested on two main thoughts. First, on the thought of the inevitable difficulties then facing the Party. Second, on the rumors reaching us (admittedly still very vague at the time) of friction within the leadership. As for the difficulties, in our view at the time, the defeat of the opposition by no means resolved the objective difficulties facing the Party both in the town and in the countryside. I was still following the course of grain procurements closely and concluded that the Party faced accumulating difficulties, especially in the countryside, difficulties that would require some important, decisive measures. This was also our group's general conviction, and we expected that these difficulties, this need to undertake some new, very substantial measures, would prompt the Party and leadership to “call upon” us for some leading work. As I have already mentioned, this same thought was sustained in us by rumors of friction in the Central Committee. The rumors were vague, but we eagerly caught and combined them. Among us, for example, there circulated a rumor (how correct it was, I do not know even now) that there was some letter from Comrade STALIN, almost to the editors of “Komsomolskaya pravda,” directed against SLEPKOV and the “Bukharin little school” generally. This accorded with our general conception, in which Comrade STALIN was already distinguished and singled out, as an old Bolshevik-Leninist who had never deviated to the Right, from BUKHARIN and RYKOV, whose vacillations and wavering we knew well from the past.

Thus, reliance on objective difficulties and disagreements in the leadership undoubtedly underlay the tactics of “waiting” that our group adopted in 1928–1929.

3\) Hence also the attitude toward other anti-Party groups and groupings which characterize \[1\] our group's conduct. The investigation knows of my and ZINOVIEV's negotiations with the “Right” (with BUKHARIN and TOMSKY). The criminal tactic of silence before our position. What was essential in the negotiations themselves was that we assessed all the groups, all the persons, and all the sentiments with which we came into contact not according to the substance of their political line, not from the standpoint of their harmfulness and anti-Party character, but exclusively from the standpoint of their ability to weaken the Party leadership and thereby, as it seemed to us, facilitate our return to a leading role. This attitude is precisely what the Party justly branded with the name “unprincipled blocs.” This unprincipled attitude on our part toward any anti-Party group could not, of course, remain a secret to them and created a situation in which every group, large or small, that raised its head against the Party leadership could count, if not on our active assistance, then at least on sympathy or, in any event, silence. This, of course, explains why ultimately even the Ryutin group, an openly counterrevolutionary group, decided to approach us with its document, if only indirectly.

Comparing all the above-mentioned features of the activity of the “Leningrad opposition” after the Fifteenth Congress — preservation of contacts after the dissolution of the factional organization, discussion of questions of political life separately from the Party, reliance on difficulties and disagreements in the Party leadership, “unprincipled blocs” with anti-Party groups — comparing all this with the difficult and responsible work the Party was carrying out at the time, which required from every Party member iron discipline and complete devotion to the Party without any ulterior thoughts, I cannot assess all this activity otherwise than as harmful, disintegrating to everyone drawn into it, and counterrevolutionary.

I have already said that the intensive period of this activity falls, in my opinion, in 1928–1929, and that from 1930 it began to die away. Naturally, I can assert this only on the basis of what I personally saw and knew. Therefore, my further explanations require clarification of my personal role and position from that time onward. The fact is that by 1930 (approximately; I cannot reconstruct the exact date from memory), the process of my internal “disarmament,” that is, my inner conviction that there was neither sense nor purpose in my participation in any struggle against the Party and leadership, had already gone very far. This had begun long before, immediately after the Fifteenth Congress. Even during the fiercest struggle, I had always stood, in factional terminology, on the furthest “Right” wing of the Leningraders. I did not have their group psychology, their personal attachment to ZINOVIEV as a wronged “leader” who had to be restored to his rights at any cost. The defeat at the Fifteenth Congress was an unexpected catastrophe for me, since until the last moment I hoped for some “agreement.” Toward the end of the congress, I stood with all my strength for “submission” to the Party and defended this formula not because I personally considered it sufficient, but only because no other formula, more complete and more in accord with the Party, could pass at the heated meetings, half of which refused even to hear of “withdrawing the platform,” while the other half (I remember, headed by LASHEVICH) berated me for weakness and pliancy. After the congress, I repeatedly said that “one does not swing one's fists after the fight,” that we had no other path than systematically working to regain the Party's trust. But in the period immediately after the congress, I still continued in the common ranks, lacking, I shall say frankly, the moral courage to break with yesterday's like-minded associates, who continued to count me among their leaders. Rumors of difficulties and disagreements in the Central Control Commission acted in the same direction, holding me back from a decisive step. But more and more facts accumulated that forced me to reconsider my positions. I count four facts that influenced me most strongly.

1\) The policy of industrialization, against which I could not raise a single word of objection; 2) collectivization, which at first astonished me by its boldness and scope, which initially seemed linked to enormous dangers, but in which I soon recognized the only correct, the only possible way out of the difficulties that I myself saw facing the Party; 3) the defeat of the Right, which demonstrated more clearly than anything, and once and for all, that in fighting the Party's enemies, the Central Committee needed neither our help nor our assistance in any measure; 4) the definitive consolidation of Party unity under the leadership of Comrade STALIN, who alone among all the former “claimants” showed himself in deed, in practice and in theory, to be a worthy continuer of LENIN's cause. What became increasingly clear to me was what I later formulated in an article printed in “Pravda” \[2\]: that the country and the Party had entered a new epoch that had relegated all the old “platforms” and assessments to the distant past, and that the Party was passing victoriously through this new epoch under the leadership it had consciously chosen and supported.

Compared with this, everything with which the remnants of the Leningrad opposition occupied themselves — their stewing in their own juice, rehashing old grievances, discussing gossip over and over, waiting for something that would suddenly restore ZINOVIEV to the post of chairman of the Comintern — all this increasingly began to seem harmful, idle, and fruitless to me. It simply ceased to interest me. I still met with them, especially at the dacha, where in both 1931 and 1932 I lived next to ZINOVIEV, who was frequently visited by EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, and KUKLIN. But I did not become involved in political conversations with them; when we met, I spoke on everyday subjects or about literature and the like. I knew that ZINOVIEV continued to speak in this milieu on political topics, set forth his considerations, and so on. But politically I already regarded him as a spent politician, incapable of pursuing any definite line, subject to personal moods and momentary impressions.

My personal state of mind was heavy. I was clearly moving away from my former like-minded associates, losing interest in them and contact with them; the Party's doors, in the sense of broad Party intercourse and active participation in Party life, were half-closed to me — and legitimately so, of course. I worked in the Main Concessions Committee, whose activity was diminishing with the liquidation of a number of concessions. This work could not occupy all my time and attention. There was no other work. To occupy my time and mind, I took up literature, and when the Central Committee appointed me head of the “Academia” publishing house, I joyfully threw myself wholeheartedly into this work. I no longer had any political disagreements with the Party, nor did any remnants of malice, irritation, or resentment toward the leadership remain. The only thing I hoped for was that, after some time had passed, I would be given more responsible work as well. In this respect, I no longer placed any hopes in any disagreements in the Central Committee or objective difficulties, and still less, of course, in any activity of the remnants of the former opposition, but exclusively in my own work and the Central Committee's trust. It was in this position and state of mind that the affair of the Ryutin followers' document caught me. In Minusinsk, I reflected on the lesson of everything that had happened. It was clear to me. The fault was my own, my passivity and the preserved contacts with the former Zinovievist group, although already politically empty of content. I then wrote to the Central Committee in complete sincerity that nothing connected me either with the Zinovievists or with ZINOVIEV, that “during” \[3\] all feelings of struggle or hostility had finally burnt themselves out, and that I asked to be given the opportunity to prove, through work under the direct supervision and leadership of the Central Committee, the sincerity of my new views and sentiments. Having been given the opportunity to return to Moscow, in several articles in “Pravda” I outlined how I conceived the new stage in the history of the Party and country through which it was passing under Comrade STALIN's leadership, and indicated why all the attacks to which Comrade STALIN had been subjected by the opposition merely repeated the attacks of all the Party's enemies against LENIN and were acts of counterrevolution \[4\]. I believed that with these articles and my speech at the Seventeenth Congress, I had theoretically exposed and summed up my errors and crimes against the Party. In practice, I broke all contacts with the Zinovievists, not only political but personal as well. Since then I have not participated in a single meeting, taken part in a single political conversation, or held any political conversations at all with any Zinovievist; I neither knew nor cared to know what was happening among them. The only person I met with was ZINOVIEV. I have already informed the investigation of the reasons for this, of how I intended to stop these meetings as well, and also that I regard this as an error and inconsistency. I consider that this was what exposed me to the blow, an irreparable blow, at precisely the moment when nothing remained in my mind and heart except the desire, through direct and faithful service to the Party under Comrade STALIN's leadership, to wash away my old sins and errors before the Party and finally break with the group whose alliance had befouled the last ten years of my life and now finally ruined me. I must state once again that, from the moment of my return to Moscow, I have not deviated in word or deed from what I wrote from Minusinsk to the Central Committee, what I wrote in my articles in “Pravda,” and what I said at the Seventeenth Congress, because all this expressed my genuine conviction, and that I cannot bear responsibility, for this period, for what the Zinovievists did or thought.

January 9, 1935.

The testimony was written in my own hand — L. KAMENEV.
