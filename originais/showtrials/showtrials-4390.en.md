TO THE CC OF THE AUCP(b), Comrade STALIN.

Great and wise Comrade STALIN!

I have committed an immense crime against the party and deserve the gravest punishment. Knowing of the existence of the anti-party, counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization, taking an active part in its work in the first years after the Fifteenth Congress, I subsequently failed to find the strength to report it to the CC, and this places upon me responsibility equal to everyone else's for its work and the grave consequences of that work.

The vile, base murder of Comrade KIROV was committed by former most active members of the Zinovievist opposition. It resulted from the treacherous, double-dealing position of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and others, which pushed adventurers who had lost any perspective, broken every thread linking them to the party and working class, onto the path of fascist terror against the genuine leaders of the party and working class, onto the path of treason against the proletarian state, of direct contact with foreign counterintelligence and the White Guards. The soil infected by the rotting stench of Zinovievist disintegration brought forth White-Guard seeds. The path indicated by ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, KHARITONOV, EVDOKIMOV, ZALUTSKY, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, SAFAROV, FEDOROV, and others led to direct betrayal of the party, working class, and motherland.

Comrade KIROV's murder was prepared, organized, and committed by their closest pupils and assistants, such as the former secretary of the Leningrad Provincial Youth Committee, V. RUMYANTSEV, and one of the principal leaders of the Leningrad opposition center in 1927, V. LEVIN.

How could this have happened?

Most former Zinovievists, myself included, reacted sharply negatively to the Fifteenth Party Congress's decision to expel the leaders of the “Leningrad” opposition, although almost all, when admitted to the party or questioned in control commissions, categorically and in a double-dealing fashion declared that they considered this decision entirely correct. The principal slogan of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, and others after the Fifteenth Congress was “acknowledge everything” and “crawl on one's belly into the party.” Obeying this slogan, people lied, deceived the party, and entered it without severing connections among themselves. The former “leaders'” return to the party gave birth to new dreams: that they would be “advanced” into power, that the so-called “Leninist CC” would be revived, that is, as the Zinovievists imagined, a CC whose Politburo would include ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. These hopes were not fulfilled. The former leaders were quite correctly invited to begin with modest work and prove and show in practice, through practical work, that they were devoted to the party, had understood their mistakes in substance, and were capable of implementing the party line. In response, while verbally singing hymns to Comrade Stalin, in practice they shut themselves in the old opposition shell and, instead of merging with the party membership and performing the common task, essentially preserved their old opposition center, went underground, and in that center's composition (ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, FEDOROV, KHARITONOV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, and others) gathered and discussed what was happening in the party, Comintern, and country. Instead of switching to new party tracks, in reality there was still greater isolation, favoritism and mutual cover-up, cowardice and double-dealing on the part of the former leaders who had taken the counterrevolutionary path, which led to the creation inside the party of a little group emerging from the Zinovievist organization, finally breaking with Marxism and taking the path of contact with inveterate White-Guardism and counterrevolutionary terror against the party's best people.

Working in Samara after the Fifteenth Congress, in 1928 I came into close contact with EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, YAROSLAV, and NAUMOV. Our households became close and we frequently visited each other. Everyone's main desire then was to get to Moscow quickly, to come closer to political work. The principal calculation was to come closer to political leadership. The main aim was to secure ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's participation in the political leadership of party and country. In assessing the situation of that period I consider it necessary to dwell on two points: 1) the struggle against the kulak. On this question it was said that the party had moved onto the platform's tracks and was implementing it. 2) The Comintern leadership: policy was insufficiently active, there was no one to lead the Comintern, there was disarray in a number of Communist parties, nothing worked without ZINOVIEV. This question was discussed and posed in approximately the same light in subsequent years in conversations by all the former leaders of the Zinovievist opposition which I happened to hear.

My personal contact with EVDOKIMOV and KUKLIN was interrupted from their departure from Samara until my arrival in Moscow in mid-1929. Arriving in Moscow, having no acquaintances apart from those listed, I stayed with KUKLIN and lived with him for about three months until I obtained an apartment and my family arrived. I visited EVDOKIMOV, GERTIK, NAUMOV, YAROSLAV, and REPNIKOV. At this time the Zinovievist organization had not ceased to exist and had actually gone underground. Only its tasks and working methods had changed. The main tasks were: 1) preserving cadres and 2) politically educating these cadres in a spirit different from what the CC taught. These tasks were performed through conversations “in passing” within a narrow circle, over a glass of tea or a card game. The Zinovievist organization became a narrow, closed little group, and political conversations were conducted openly only inside it. Former Zinovievists gathered chiefly at GERTIK's. KHARITONOV, FEDOROV, GORSHENIN, S. GESSEN, YAROSLAV, BRAVO, BAKAEV, NAUMOV, BRAUDE, REPNIKOV, KOZHURO, less frequently EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and many others came there. Besides, all kinds of visitors and people passing through Moscow were often there. Among them I remember M. NATANSON, ZILBERMAN, ANISHEV, GERTSBERG, DMITRIEV T., and DMITRIEV N. Everyone coming felt at home there: they ate, slept, drank, told counterrevolutionary jokes and gossip. The principal political conversations of this period amounted to the following:

1) everyone, myself included at that time, was dissatisfied that former opposition leaders were not brought into leading work;

2) Comintern policy was assessed as inactive and ineffective; it was asserted that this caused disarray in a number of Communist parties;

3) slanderous claims were made that the struggle against the Rightists and the struggle against the kulak had been “borrowed” from the opposition platform;

4) all sorts of slanderous, counterrevolutionary rumors about supposed disagreements in the Politburo were supported and spread;

5) there was much talk about the difficult food situation in the country, about excesses in the countryside. Comrade Stalin's letter “Dizzy with Success” was regarded as the result of inability to ensure the full implementation of a correct policy, as a result of which retreat became necessary. All these individual conversations, sometimes phrases, were understandably not accidental: they were links in one common chain, the result of dissatisfaction with the CC line. All these counterrevolutionary conversations were pointed against the CC, against Comrade Stalin, and all were conducted by Zinovievists with party cards in their pockets. And now, analyzing memories of these conversations in light of the entire subsequent course of ZINOVIEV and others, one involuntarily concludes that ultimately all of them were artificially inspired by the former “leaders,” not only for reasons of principle: they needed to sustain their like-minded associates' mood somehow, and therefore seized on each difficulty, interpreted in their own way every difficulty standing in the party's victorious path. These sentiments originated with ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV and spread among their supporters.

In 1931 I stopped going to GERTIK's. This was preceded by a conversation with NAUMOV and LEPESHINSKAYA at their apartment. I remember her words that you lads have had enough of “living and getting entangled with all kinds of scum.” By “scum” she meant all kinds of little-known or wholly unknown people whom we sometimes met at GERTIK's. In particular I remember LEPESHINSKAYA saying that all sorts of rabble visited GERTIK, sometimes including former Trotskyists. The main thrust of this conversation among the three of us was that these meetings were degenerating into drinking sessions accompanied by counterrevolutionary whispering and chatter. Essentially my withdrawal from the Zinovievist organization's work began at this point.

My anti-party, counterrevolutionary work consisted in systematic contacts and meetings with the people I listed and in sustaining counterrevolutionary conversations and gossip together with them. My main trouble lay in being unable to tear myself away from some of them even when I had firmly decided to break with the Zinovievist past.

I visited ZINOVIEV twice: 1) the first meeting took place in late 1929 or early 1930. From this conversation I remember three points ZINOVIEV spoke of: a) he said he had stopped being given permits to attend meetings and that this must be happening because workers had received him very well; b) on the question of the Rightists the CC pursued a correct policy, but “we spoke and wrote about it much earlier”; c) nothing was heard of the Comintern, and it had no people capable of leading the work. The conversation lasted twenty or thirty minutes; KAMENEV came in, whom I met then for the first and last time, and I immediately left.

2) The second meeting occurred in autumn 1932. I learned that the Central Control Commission was considering ZINOVIEV's ties to some Rightist grouping and that ZINOVIEV was being exiled. These rumors made a great impression on me. I went to ZINOVIEV, related what I had heard, and asked what was happening. As I understood, ZINOVIEV already knew everything; he struck me as completely bewildered and frightened, gave no articulate answer on the substance, saying only that apparently he would have to leave. It was quite clear that he was afraid to tell me his opinion on the substance of the matter. I left ZINOVIEV with a heavy feeling of complete emptiness concerning him and my anti-party past.

I never visited ZINOVIEV again, and when after his return I once met him in the street, I crossed to the other side.

ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's ties with the Ryutinists, when I learned the details, made a sharply negative impression on me; it became entirely clear that these were not accidental but flowed directly from the slogan proclaimed by ZINOVIEV at the Fourteenth Congress on factions.

I began working actively in the party but did not sever all personal ties.

A complete change of views and sentiments occurs slowly and painfully. It must be accompanied by facts refuting the former sentiments and visibly demonstrating their previous error; only then will it be final and serious. Since 1932 I traveled throughout the Soviet Union, visited the Caucasus, Urals, Siberia, Gorky, Kazakhstan, Leningrad, all the Volga cities, and still more places. I visibly saw how STALIN's great genius and iron will put party decisions into practice, with what enthusiasm they were implemented; I saw the building of the industrial Soviet Union, how the kulaks were liquidated in the fire of class struggle, how collective farms became Bolshevik and collective farmers prosperous, how all the fabrications about the impossibility of building socialism in one country were shattered and destroyed. I observed all this not as a spectator but personally participated in the great transformations taking place in the sector of work entrusted to me. Alongside this, directly participating in all this, I could not fail to see how nothing but dust remained of everything Zinovievist. I arrived at this because I myself actively participated in work and struggle, not sitting it out but actively working, coming to love my work, implementing the party line, and providing good examples of work. But the heavy burden of personal ties entangled my feet, and I lacked the strength to throw it off, could not fully escape the Zinovievist swamp, break definitively with everyone stuck in it.

The Zinovievist organization, which existed inside the party for several years, not only failed to recruit a single new member but gradually saw its ranks thin. The exceptional strength, monolithic cohesion, and unity of the party achieved under the great STALIN's leadership turned it into such an iron organism that rust proved powerless to affect it even to an infinitesimal extent.

My assessment of ZINOVIEV as a leader seems a distant, painful past. One feels ashamed and distressed when recalling that I once compared STALIN and ZINOVIEV. Genius, greatness, monolithic cohesion, and purposefulness in STALIN; base politicking, double-dealing, cowardice, the corpse poison of disintegration in ZINOVIEV. A mighty flight of thought into the expanse and heights on powerful eagle wings in one, and the leaps of a frog inevitably doomed to fall back into the old swamp in the other. From the “baggage-train forces,” ZINOVIEV and his like finally slid into history's rubbish pit, and he will always be written about in textbooks as a classic model of betrayal and double-dealing. But the party, under the great STALIN's leadership, achieved grandiose, decisive historical victories, crushing everything harmful and rotten in its path, without for a minute halting its offensive movement forward and upward.

On 1 April 1934 I was transferred to work in the Northern Territory. I immediately took my family so as not to be drawn to Moscow and worked in earnest, obtaining great satisfaction in work. The Northern Territorial Committee knows how I worked. The good business relations I established in Arkhangelsk enabled me finally to work in a new way. I also established personal ties only with people of a different disposition and cast. AUSTRIN visited my apartment most often; Vl. Iv. IVANOV, PRYADCHENKO, and SHIIRON came. I thought the whole counterrevolutionary, anti-party past was crossed out, that at last the heavy burden had fallen away. It proved otherwise: whoever was insincere inside the party, whoever double-dealt, did harm on the sly or remained silent when he should have shouted, must answer before the party, and this is entirely correct. I am certain the CC will correctly decide my case and assess my actions and work, an assessment which can only be the most severe. Naturally I want to work, want through work to make amends for all my criminal anti-party acts. One cannot help dreaming and thinking of this when looking at the colossal construction taking place in the country; and this is all the easier for me because the party line is the only correct line for me, all the easier because the great leader of our party, Comrade STALIN, his every word and action, is for me the fundamental and sole guidance on what to do and how to work.

Great and wise Comrade Stalin! I turn to you for help: help me prove in practice, in work, that I have overcome the accursed past definitively and irrevocably, that I am devoted to the party and capable of sincerely fighting with all my strength to implement its decisions in the most menial work, in the hardest sector.

SAKHOV.

10/1-35.

certified true: Biryukov
