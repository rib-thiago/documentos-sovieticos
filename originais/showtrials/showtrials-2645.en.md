\<SESSION OF JANUARY 16, 1935\>

PRESIDING JUDGE: The session resumes (10:40 a.m., January 16). Defendant ANISHEV, do you wish to say something?

Def\<endant\> ANISHEV: I have two new facts to report to the court; I believe this is necessary in the course of the proceedings.

The first fact was mentioned by TARASOV, concerning the slanderous attack we made against the Party and Comrade STALIN in connection with the construction of VIEM—the Institute of Experimental Medicine. I was the original source of that attack. I heard a vile piece of gossip from one of our leading specialists concerning the construction decision. I took up this gossip, gave it the appropriate coloring, and spread it both in Leningrad and later at BAKAEV’s apartment.

Three conclusions follow from this: first, I was not merely a transmitter of rumors and an inciter of hatred, but also showed initiative of my own; second, this concerns the mechanism of the connections that existed. I do not remember whether I saw TARASOV anywhere, but it has now become clear that TARASOV knows about this very matter. I must report the attitude toward this gossip that TARASOV described. I encountered the same attitude at BAKAEV’s own apartment. I do not remember whether anyone else was there.

The second fact. At first I told the investigator about my conversations with RUMIANTSEV concerning conditions at Leningrad factories. When SAFAROV recounted ZINOVIEV’s conversations about the degeneration of the proletariat, I recalled in much greater detail what RUMIANTSEV had said about conditions in Leningrad factories. There is no need to repeat it, because what RUMIANTSEV said about conditions at those factories was the same as what ZINOVIEV said about the degeneration of the proletariat, apart from that generalization about proletarian degeneration. I do not know whether there was an organizational connection here, but I consider it necessary to report this fact to the court.

I described this during the preliminary investigation. I believe it was wrong of me to try to divide my activities into stages during the preliminary investigation. Every stage of my activity while participating in the counterrevolutionary organization was active. My activity sometimes increased and sometimes decreased, but this did not diminish its overall counterrevolutionary character.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Do you confirm your last testimony, dated December 30?

Def\<endant\> PERIMOV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Do you also confirm that of January 10?

Def\<endant\> PERIMOV: Yes. SAFAROV spoke about information in  \[1\] Kazakhstan, saying that ZINOVIEV had received it from SAKHOV. There is an error here. I remember that I personally gave ZINOVIEV the information about Kazakhstan. So this is an error.

PERIMOV: It was approximately in 1931–1932; I cannot give the exact dates. I am referring to the words SAFAROV quoted about the arrested persons; the periods coincide.

This is because I did not immediately grasp or appreciate the enormous counterrevolutionary danger and the harm our group represented to the proletariat and the Party. I fully agree with the assessment of our activities given here by EVDOKIMOV, and references to objective circumstances cannot in any way lessen or mitigate our guilt.

I would like to dwell on a few minor details. During the judicial examination we were asked about IONOV; I forgot about it and said nothing. He broke off contact and stopped meeting with us and sharing news and information, nothing more.

PERIMOV: Yes.

EXAMINATION

## OF GRIGORI FEDOROVICH FEDOROV.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Defendant FEDOROV, do you confirm your testimony?

FEDOROV: I confirm it in full.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What would you like to add?

FEDOROV: I would like to begin with a brief statement: I fully and completely agree with the observations and everything Comrade EVDOKIMOV said here about the methods of political struggle employed by the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary center. A whole series of political facts were not only sought out for their negative aspects, and various difficulties were not merely left unjustified; facts were deliberately sought in order to discredit the work of the Central Committee and the Party leadership.

I want to say that the question of difficulties, which has been raised here repeatedly, has not been pursued to its logical conclusion. This struck me especially after Lev Borisovich KAMENEV’s speech yesterday. According to his view, the picture is this: if certain difficulties were to mount in the country, then at some stage the Party Central Committee would consider it prudent and necessary to invite the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization to help resolve them. I believe that such an interpretation of the question of difficulties corresponds neither to a logical understanding of the problem nor to the tactics employed by the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization.

The point is that one cannot understand a difficulty as a process that continues up to a certain limit, stops there, and is followed by an agreement with particular organizations; those organizations make some magical proposals, and the difficulty then begins to resolve itself. I do not think that is how it works.

To pursue the discussion of difficulties to its conclusion, one must understand the following: the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization believed that conditions might arise in our country under which famine, war, uprisings, and so forth would become realities, and that those events or the consequences of those difficulties could provide the basis, as the organization supposed (according to its own conjectures), for its inclusion in the Party’s leading bodies.

I believe it is impossible to theorize about difficulties without bringing them onto concrete ground. At the Fifteenth Party Congress, or around the time of that congress, I clearly remember KAMENEV, in analyzing our agricultural situation, saying that the Party had adopted a breakneck pace of agricultural collectivization; that this pace would ruin peasant farms; and that the ruin of peasant farms would bring about a reduction in cultivated acreage and crop yields. “Thus,” he said, “the country is heading toward famine.” It seems quite clear to me that one must put an equal sign between the word “difficulties” and famine. The opposition was not banking on abstract difficulties, on difficulties with no known endpoint, but on a definite goal: the country is heading toward famine, and we must exploit that famine. It was through this famine that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were supposed to come to the Party leadership—that was how the question was posed, and allow me to say so plainly.

As for questions of another sort, those of war, I think that if war broke out and our victorious Red Army fought it successfully, we may suppose there would be no particular difficulties and no special need for the Party to invite ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the leadership. Thus, for the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization it would not be enough for war to break out; that war would also have to bring defeat. Only as a consequence of such difficulties could the question again arise of the Party Central Committee and Soviet power, in uniting all forces to repel the enemy, inviting ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into leading roles.

I believe we can say here, in proletarian terms that every worker can understand, that an equal sign must be placed between difficulties of a theoretical kind, as they have been discussed here so far, and the difficulties of famine and war accompanied by defeats.

In the preliminary investigation I gave a fairly detailed account of how our Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization negotiated the formation of a bloc with the Rightists. I stated in my testimony that no one—not KAMENEV, not ZINOVIEV, nor any of us—seriously raised the question of the principles underlying that bloc. When, at the end of 1929, I attended the negotiations in Sochi between ZINOVIEV and TOMSKY concerning the bloc, I saw that the sole concern of both parties was how many forces each had, how those forces could be assessed, and whether, through certain efforts of the Rightists and the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization, STALIN could be defeated. That was the only question, and it was decisive in determining whether or not to form a bloc. Thus this example shows that the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization attached no importance whatever to questions of principle; it had no such principles. In confirmation of this, I have learned here at the trial that ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and EVDOKIMOV were negotiating new blocs. And with whom? With the Trotskyists, with LOMINADZE, SHATSKIN, and SHLYAPNIKOV. What is this? An attempt to organize against the Central Committee some sort of invasion of twelve tongues. In short, it is apparent that our organization had no principles and held nothing sacred. There was only one desire: at any cost, by any means, to obstruct and influence the Party leadership; to secure the admission of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the leading bodies. Of course, one must understand that it was not merely a matter of securing posts on the Central Committee or its Politburo. That was not all: we all understood that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV’s arrival in the Politburo leadership would weaken Comrade STALIN’s leading role and facilitate the implementation of the counterrevolutionary positions held by the Zinovievite organization. I believe that ZINOVIEV’s speech here yesterday characterizes, in my opinion, the entire conduct of the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization over those ten years.

Instability, uncertainty, lack of principle—in short, every vice and not a single positive quality.

Look at what he said yesterday. If we had accepted DZERZHINSKY’s proposal before the Fourteenth Congress, we would not have carried on this struggle, nor would we be sitting here in the dock. And why did we not do so? Because, you see, we felt sorry for SAFAROV and SARKIS. Is that any way to pose the question? Could proletarian revolutionaries pose it that way? No.

Next, ZINOVIEV tries to blur the organization’s activities throughout those years. ZINOVIEV presented matters as though those activities could be divided into distinct periods. I could understand such periods if, say, we had struggled until the Fifteenth Congress, then stopped the struggle, and after some interval resumed it in one form or another. Unfortunately, comrades, that is not what happened.

Unfortunately, from 1925 through the end of 1934 the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization systematically waged, day after day and year after year, a sustained struggle undermining the authority of the Party and Soviet power.

The fact that at particular moments this work subsided somewhat or, conversely, reached a certain intensity was no positive quality of the Zinovievite organization and no achievement of its own. This happened because the Party situation at the time was such that the organization could not show itself. Observe: as soon as the slightest possibility of disagreement with the Central Committee arose, the activity of the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary party revived and the curve rose. Then, in 1932, as has been recounted here, the question of raising this activity to a certain level arose once more. Thus, these are not separate periods: there is a definite objective, a particular phase of this work, a phase of persistent work from the Fourteenth Congress to 1935. We must say plainly that there are no distinct phases and periods; there is a definite line of conduct that continued for years, for which we must answer.

In my testimony I acknowledged the existence of the Moscow Center and called it a political center, for that is indeed what it was. The Moscow Center was not merely concerned with the work of a personnel department or an organizational department, shifting workers from one post to another. NAUMOV handled that, but it is a secondary matter. The essential point is that the Moscow Center was a political center, issuing political directives on every question. Comrade EVDOKIMOV described this vividly, and I also described it in my preliminary testimony.

EVDOKIMOV and ZINOVIEV are granting me a sort of amnesty here, in the sense that I was not recently a member of the center. I believe this is incorrect. In my view, the point is that I visited ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV several times; in this “Moscow Center,” among its members, the directive issued immediately after the Fifteenth Congress to preserve the cadres was being carried out. The purpose of this organization, this “center,” was to include people tested over years and by the experience of struggle, so that, if circumstances required, at any moment (I do not know whether all of them or only a majority) they could again become the center of a new struggle. That, so to speak, was the purpose of the entire counterrevolutionary organization’s continued existence until the very last. It was a cell or “center,” so to speak, for any new struggle that might break out as a result of one or another of the difficulties discussed here.

In conclusion, I would simply like to confirm what I have already stated in my testimony: the members of the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization were disseminators of the vilest slander, lies, and gossip about the Party leadership. Throughout the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization’s existence, malice and hatred toward the Party leadership were cultivated in its ranks. The organization did not hesitate to employ slander, lies, deceit, and distortion of facts. That is to say, it used the most despicable methods drawn from the arsenal of struggle against the Party. I confirm all this and believe that these methods of work, these vile methods, ultimately trained and produced scoundrels such as NIKOLAEV. These methods led to NIKOLAEV firing at Comrade KIROV. I believe all of us, myself included, must answer fully and completely for the vile work we have carried out until now.

EXAMINATION

## OF ALEKSANDR SERGEYEVICH KUKLIN

PRESIDING JUDGE: Defendant KUKLIN, there are certain ambiguities in your testimony. We should probably clarify them. Nowhere in your testimony is it stated that you were a member of the “Moscow Center,” whereas a number of your comrades say that you were a member of that center for many years. Were you a member of the Moscow Center of the counterrevolutionary organization led by ZINOVIEV?

KUKLIN: First, I would like permission to state that I agree with EVDOKIMOV’s assessment of our work, and I believe that in our activities we deceived the Party while also deceiving ourselves and even the members of the center. At first I resisted, saying that I had not been a member of that center. I said that we deceived ourselves. Until yesterday I did not know that I really was a member of the center. Only yesterday did I hear from ZINOVIEV that I had been a member. No one invited me, and I would hardly have accepted such a proposal; it would hardly have been made to me. For we had already decided that after our statements we would dissolve the organization completely and sever all ties.

If we had wanted to abolish all this, of course we could have made every effort to do so, especially if our leaders had not simply abandoned it in such a commonplace fashion; they just walked away and the young people were left behind. Had our leaders spoken with the Central Committee, I am quite certain they would have been permitted to disperse all our people to posts and jobs where we would do no harm and, on the other hand, would be educated. The same would have been done with those who remained obstinate: they could have been persuaded, and if that failed, referred to the appropriate authorities. None of this was done. I had expected that when I came to Moscow. Seven to nine people visit ZINOVIEV. They talk and talk, but nothing comes of it. Here we see an entirely different picture: it turns out that we all maintained contacts, that there were people everywhere. Clearly some sort of center was needed. I do not mean to say that I knew nothing of the secret agreements. I say there were such agreements. Worse still, there were agreements with other groups. And SAFAROV, like a lawyer acting as a broker, brought parties together and separated them, etc\<etera\>. But did everyone know about this? No, comrades, not everyone knew. I did not know about these agreements, although I often visited ZINOVIEV. All this was done to fight the Central Committee. With anyone at all, without principles, as long as they opposed the Central Committee, as long as they were against it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you often travel from Moscow to Leningrad to maintain contact with your Leningrad comrades?

Def\<endant\> KUKLIN: I was there three times: in 1931, in 1932—you have 1933 written down there, but I was there in 1932—and in 1934. In 1931 I went on business and stayed about two days. I saw no one that year. In 1932 I went on personal business to a resort at Sestroretsk. At that time I met LEVIN and got his apartment address and telephone number. The following day I went to Sestroretsk, where I stayed an entire month. I went nowhere else during that time. A month later I left Sestroretsk for my sister’s apartment.

PRESIDING JUDGE: We are not interested in where you were or with whom you drank tea, with or without sugar. Something else interests me.

KUKLIN: I met LEVIN. I had to spend the night there out of necessity, because the apartment where I was supposed to sleep was occupied (inaudible). I remember that MIASNIKOV came, KORSHUNOV was there, and then some relatives. In 1934 I met RUMIANTSEV at my sister’s apartment and spoke about establishing contact with the “ILICH” factory. We discussed it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What matters to me is what you discussed with him.

KUKLIN: Since outsiders were present, we had no particular conversations.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you convey to RUMIANTSEV ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV’s instruction that the struggle must continue?

KUKLIN: I said nothing about that.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Your testimony states that you had that conversation with RUMIANTSEV. I shall read it: \*(reads testimony)\* “The directives of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were in turn passed on to their associates, by me, for example, when meeting HERTSBERG and, as far as I remember, in Leningrad with Vladimir RUMIANTSEV and others.”

That is what your testimony of January 14 says.

KUKLIN: I confirm it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In what year did you ask LEVIN about the archive  \[2\]?

KUKLIN: In 1933. He said he did not have the archive, that he had handed it over to EMELIANOV.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you not approach EMELIANOV?

KUKLIN: No.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Why were you interested in the archive? Had you been assigned to find it?

KUKLIN: I had no assignment. I was interested in it myself.

PRESIDING JUDGE: You discussed the archive with LEVIN and FEDOROV. Why did you take it from FEDOROV? Did you consider FEDOROV unreliable?

KUKLIN: He simply left it with me.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Why?

KUKLIN: Because he could not leave it where he was. I took it for a while.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In which month did you take the archive?

KUKLIN: I do not remember the month.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In which year?

KUKLIN: (gives no answer).

PRESIDING JUDGE: You have got everything mixed up.

KUKLIN: I think it was in 1933.

FEDOROV: In early 1934 or late 1933.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Where did you send it?

KUKLIN: It lay in the apartment.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What was in the archive?

KUKLIN: I do not know; I did not look.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What happened to the archive?

KUKLIN: I destroyed it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: When did you destroy it?

KUKLIN: On the tenth.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Which month? Why are you speaking as though you had only just arrived from the countryside?

KUKLIN: Before my arrest.

PRESIDING JUDGE: December 10. Why did you destroy it on December 10?

KUKLIN: I do not know... Perhaps out of an old habit; I simply did not want to leave it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Why precisely on December 10?

KUKLIN: Because I learned of EVDOKIMOV’s arrest.

PRESIDING JUDGE: When was EVDOKIMOV arrested?

KUKLIN: The sixth... the seventh.

A VOICE FROM THE AUDIENCE: On the night of the eighth to the ninth.

KUKLIN: I learned about it on the tenth.

PRESIDING JUDGE: From whom did you hear?

KUKLIN: From... (inaudible).

PRESIDING JUDGE: From whom did you hear?

A VOICE FROM THE AUDIENCE: ...EVDOKIMOV’s wife telephoned my apartment. I learned of EVDOKIMOV’s arrest and BAKAEV’s arrest from his wife.

PRESIDING JUDGE: And you concluded that the arrest was connected with your organizational work?

KUKLIN: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: And you decided to destroy the archive?

KUKLIN: Yes... I thought so.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did the archive contain incriminating documents?

KUKLIN: I really do not know.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Why did you destroy it, then, if you had seen nothing and did not know what was in the archive? Why did you decide to destroy it in advance, without knowing what it contained? Presumably you knew what was there.

KUKLIN: I was told there were some documents.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What kind?

KUKLIN: Various Zinovievite materials.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Why did you decide to destroy materials that were no secret and posed no danger to anyone? What did you need to do that for?

KUKLIN: (remains silent).

PRESIDING JUDGE: Your testimony is not entirely sincere. Think it over before your final statement. You can add something then. You are confused and trying to obscure something. Sit down.

EXAMINATION

## OF LEONID YAKOVLEVICH FAIVILOVICH.

PRESIDING JUDGE: FAIVILOVICH, please tell us what your work in the organization consisted of. Give us a brief account.

FAIVILOVICH: My role as an active participant in the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization consisted, first, in retaining certain contacts among former oppositionists; second, in taking part in the conversation TARASOV spoke about yesterday; and, third, in believing that ZINOVIEV could be brought into the Party leadership.

That is what enabled me to declare that I was a member of the organization and bore full responsibility for its activities.

I would like to make a few remarks in addition to what I said during the investigation.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Do you confirm the testimony you gave during the investigation?

FAIVILOVICH: I confirm it in full. I would like to make three remarks. The first concerns what Comrade TARASOV said yesterday about the conversations regarding the leadership of our Party. Both at the confrontation and here I must testify before the court that the issue was not replacing Comrade STALIN but bringing ZINOVIEV into the leadership. I defended that position and told TARASOV so. I do not absolve myself of guilt in the matter and accept full responsibility for the conversation I had. But the truth must be established, and I must confirm what I said during the investigation.

My second remark concerns ZINOVIEV’s statement about responsibility. It would appear—and I dwell on this because ZINOVIEV tried in some way to evade responsibility and recognition of the painful truth for each of us—that we bear responsibility for the murder that was committed. This is a very painful realization, but nothing can be done. Such is our lot. We must frankly say that we bear moral and political responsibility for this affair. I am one of those who began the struggle against the Party alongside ZINOVIEV, and in analyzing how we reached this point I must say that, before the Fourteenth Congress, ZINOVIEV and all of us relied on that same group in Leningrad and waged an active struggle against the Party with it. Between the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Congresses we again relied on it and conducted a counterrevolutionary struggle against the Party with that group.

After the Fifteenth Congress—as TARASOV described—all that is known. And you yourself, Comrade ZINOVIEV, gave directives on how to retreat and how to act duplicitously toward that group. How, after that, can one cast a shadow of doubt by saying they were the same people and yet not the same, Zinovievites yet not those Zinovievites but others?

I consider it necessary to say that all of us, and I personally, bear moral and political responsibility for the murder of Comrade KIROV.

Those scoundrels, that fascist gang, grew out of us. And however painful it may be, it was not only you, Comrade ZINOVIEV, who experienced this so painfully—I think we all did. I must speak for myself: it has cost me dearly to reach the point of saying here in court that I bear political and moral responsibility for the murder committed. But that is how it is. There is no escape from it. Therefore we must admit everything to the end and draw conclusions not just for ourselves but for the entire Party, so that those who read the trial reports may learn from it and this may become widely known. That is what I wanted to add to my investigative testimony.

One more remark. I said that I bear responsibility for the group as a whole. Although I had no contact with the center, did not know it existed, and did not know who belonged to it, there was a period when I wrote to the Central Committee sincerely stating that I believed I had broken with the opposition. The work entrusted to me by the Party, which I carried out, completely absorbed me. The restoration of contacts actually began after my arrival in Moscow. Although I did not actively seek out contacts myself, I met with people and must bear full responsibility for that. The Soviet court, our proletarian court, must decide what responsibility I deserve. That is what I wanted to say.

EXAMINATION

## OF NIKOLAI ALEKSEYEVICH TSARKOV.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Defendant TSARKOV, tell us about your work in the organization.

TSARKOV: My work consisted of reestablishing contact with the former Zinovievite opposition in Leningrad. After a long break, this once again defined my political affiliation with the Zinovievite opposition.

My work in the Zinovievite organization in Leningrad consisted of establishing contact with Zinovievites, and this politically defined my participation in the organization.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What directives did your group receive from your leader RUMIANTSEV?

TSARKOV: After 1929 I had no conversations with RUMIANTSEV. When I came to Leningrad in 1933, RUMIANTSEV invited me to his apartment, saying he had news from Moscow. I did not go to his apartment or speak with him on that subject.

From what RUMIANTSEV said, it became clear to me that he maintained contact with Moscow. We knew that the leaders of the Zinovievite group lived in Moscow and that RUMIANTSEV, as far as I knew, was influential and very close to ZINOVIEV. So in 1933 I did not meet him at his apartment or discuss politics with him.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you meet RUMIANTSEV many times in 1934?

TSARKOV: I met him in the m\<onth\> of November 1934 when I returned after an interval from the Tikhvin Aluminum Combine. I spoke with him about other matters, not political affairs.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Let me remind you: in your testimony of December 18 you described in detail the directives you received from your leadership, apparently from RUMIANTSEV.

TSARKOV: During the investigation I said that the directives called for struggle against the present Party leadership and for bringing ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the leadership. That was the basic directive.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Apparently you have forgotten. I shall read some excerpts from your testimony of December 18: “...into whose consciousness (the non-Party masses) we, myself included, drove the same hatred of STALIN. We were taught to act in this direction by the methods of a genuine underground organization... We were especially hostile toward KIROV as well, because he directly defeated our leaders in open struggle before the broad masses. Our attitude toward KIROV was roughly the same as that of the Mensheviks toward the Bolsheviks when the latter, in 1917, wrested the masses away from Menshevik influence.” Do you confirm that testimony?

TSARKOV: Yes, I confirm it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Further: “The directives coming from the center explained that the possibility of the leaders of the Zinovievite organization—ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and oth\<ers\>—returning to the Party leadership depended on just two circumstances:

1\) An attack on the USSR by imperialist countries... In that case our directives coincided with the hopes and aspirations of all counterrevolutionary and fascist forces inside and outside the country.

2\) The removal of STALIN from the Party leadership... On this basis the most extreme extremist sentiments arose and grew among us young people. NIKOLAEV’s shot at KIROV is thus a direct and immediate act of the sentiments prevailing in the organization.” Did you say that?

TSARKOV: I was asked what the directives were during the period of open struggle concerning a change in Party leadership. I said what you have just read.

PRESIDING JUDGE: To which period does that refer?

TSARKOV: After ZINOVIEV was readmitted to the Party...

PRESIDING JUDGE: I am asking who gave these directives: RUMIANTSEV or someone else?

TSARKOV: Yes, they were passed on by RUMIANTSEV.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What could the removal of STALIN from the Party leadership consist of, and how did you understand it when you spoke about extremist sentiments and NIKOLAEV’s shot? How can this be explained so that everyone understands?

TSARKOV: Sharply hostile... Well, personal relations, personal assessments of the leaders... of each one, very sharply.

PRESIDING JUDGE: NIKOLAEV and several others said openly at the trial that they were preparing to murder STALIN and wanted to travel to Moscow for that purpose. They said so two weeks ago in this very courtroom. That is how they understood extremist sentiments. How did you understand them when you wrote that?

TSARKOV: I mean sharply hostile sentiments.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Hostility can vary in degree. One may merely criticize, curse, or demand removal from office; others would “only” shoot, like NIKOLAEV, who also began with talk and criticism.

TSARKOV: When I said that, I was not referring to terrorist methods of struggle against the leadership.

PRESIDING JUDGE: I shall read your words: “...therefore, in the arsenal of struggle against the Party, only one effective argument remained: to remove STALIN. On this basis the most extreme extremist sentiments arose and grew among us young people.”

The following sentence: “NIKOLAEV’s shot at KIROV is thus a direct and immediate act of the sentiments prevailing in the organization.”

You call terrorist sentiments extremist sentiments.

TSARKOV: I state before the court that I did not have terrorist methods of struggle in mind.

PRESIDING JUDGE: But in the next sentence you speak about KIROV’s murder.

TSARKOV: I mean that these sentiments developed into terrorist sentiments among the young people, as a result of which KIROV was murdered.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In another of your statements, when asked what you knew of the terrorist activity of your organization, you answered: “I have already testified about this (what I have just read); I confirm that in the organization, over

a number of years, hatred of the Party leaders was cultivated, and the question of methods of removing STALIN from the leadership was raised and discussed. On that basis terrorist sentiments grew, leading to the murder of one of the Party leaders—KIROV.”

So you discussed methods of removing STALIN, and one of those methods, apparently, must be considered terror. How else can one understand it?

EXAMINATION

## OF BORIS NAUMOVICH SAKHOV.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant SAKHOV, tell us about your work in the counterrevolutionary organization.

SAKHOV: I should like to ask the court's permission to address three questions and to begin with my attitude toward what has been said here, because I do not endorse what has been said here; I do not endorse what the former leaders of the opposition have said here. I believe that is where I must begin.

CHAIRMAN: Please proceed.

SAKHOV: I followed ZINOVIEV from 1925, from the Fourteenth Congress. In 1927 I actively participated in the opposition. Clandestine meetings were held in my apartment. ZINOVIEV visited my apartment. In the days before November 7, the greatest coordination among the groups in Leningrad took place, in particular in my apartment. So it seemed.

After that I took part in the events of November 7, and not simply as the others did, standing in the square. Together with ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, RADEK and four others, I found myself obliged to go to the Tolmachev Academy, where we stayed because they would not let us leave until three in the afternoon. There, with ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, RADEK and three or four others, I signed a counterrevolutionary leaflet entitled “The Truth About the Events of November 7.” This shows that I was fairly active throughout that period. I recounted all this to the Control Commission when I was called to account for my Party activities before the Fifteenth Congress. All this is listed and recorded in the decision of the Central Commission.

In the period before the Congress, for about ten days, I joined the group of the “leaderless”; in fact, I joined almost from its inception, when SARKIS and SAFAROV were its leaders. I say this for the following reason: for several days I struggled painfully to remember what I had said to SAFAROV… Only when I met him on Vozdvizhenka Street, and when people began speaking here of the leaderless, did I recall that conversation. SAFAROV lied about Kazakhstan, and I would not have been able to refute him if PERIMOV had not stated that he himself had spoken to ZINOVIEV about it. Just as SAFAROV lied in saying that I spoke to ZINOVIEV about Kazakhstan, he was also untruthful when he said that I met him in 1932 and spoke to him about ABRAMSON. That did not happen. I met him once, on the street, in 1930. We asked each other where we worked, and then he brought up the history of the leaderless, recalling a fact which, in my opinion, neither the Party nor the GPU knows from that period. When we proclaimed ourselves leaderless and began denouncing ZINOVIEV and all the others, a group of 15–20 peop\<le\> declared that we should honestly state our views to the Central Committee. We wrote a statement to the Central Committee signed by 15–20 peop\<le\>, and among the scoundrels who signed it was LEVIN, I think LEVIN, although I hesitate to say. No, it could not have been LEVIN. So there was only RUMIANTSEV. I think I never met him again afterward. In that statement we wrote: first, we do not repent; second, we continue the struggle; third, we oppose TROTSKY; fourth, we oppose ZINOVIEV as a traitor and self-seeking opportunist. I sent that statement to Moscow. Unless I am mistaken now, the statement was initiated either by SARKIS or by SAFAROV; I hesitate to say exactly, lest I say something false.

SAFAROV did not submit the statement to the Central Committee. At first we decided to submit it, but when it came time, he was frightened and lost his nerve. He met me on Vozdvizhenka Street; we had not seen each other all that time. “What, you weren't even expelled from the Party?” — “No, I wasn't expelled.” — “Well, if we'd submitted that statement, do you think that would have been right? They would have purged you from the Party and sent you somewhere in Siberia, and so forth.” Then he asked: “Do you go to Leningrad?” — “I do.” — “Come and see me.” I said: “All right, perhaps I will.” That was the whole conversation with SAFAROV. Where did all the rest come from? I cannot understand how he knows about ABRAMSON. It was in 1930. My visual memory is good, but my auditory memory is poor. I distinctly remember going from Comintern Street toward the People's Commissariat of Justice and meeting SAFAROV. And the People's Commissariat of Justice moved at the end of 1930.

A word, before I go on, about my remaining in the Party. On January 28, 1928, I accounted to the Samara Control Commission for my anti-Party activities of the counterrevolutionary period, October–December 1927. I did not lie there when I declared that I regarded the Congress decisions as correct, unlike a number of other comrades; I made a statement for which I could have been expelled from the Party. I remember that some comrades were surprised by how matters turned out.

I stated that I considered all the decisions correct in substance. My case was heard by the Control Commission in the presence of many workers, as many as a hundred people. I considered that the leaders of the opposition should not be expelled from the Party (it is on record; you can read it), because I believed the leaders of the opposition could remain in the Party and be absorbed into it. Despite my statement, I was kept in the Party and received only a severe reprimand with a warning.

A member of the Control Commission remarked: “Perhaps we should say that at least one of them speaks honestly about what he thinks.”

In 1928, in Samara, I met KUKLIN, EVDOKIMOV, Yasha YAROSLAVSKY and NAUMOV, who had arrived there. We established very close relations, and from then on everything started again, everything began anew.

I will not speak about the entire past; I will dwell on just three points.

1\. This concerns what has been said here. It is difficult for me to speak here, especially because I was very closely connected with EVDOKIMOV and KUKLIN, but I must say that EVDOKIMOV used an expression here that I cannot pass over. He said: “Sometimes a lie is told for salvation.” Why was it necessary to say that, and for whom? I speak only the truth, about myself and others, regardless of whether it is to my advantage. Who needs that? Acting on this maxim that a lie may be told for salvation, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV submitted a statement to the Fifteenth Congress. They lied when they rejoined the Party, and they lied afterward. Why was that necessary? To show which path we should take?

2\. I find it necessary to declare that I align myself with no one. I consider doing so impermissible, a throwback to the past, a following of leaders. Let each person speak for himself and assess the organization and his own work, but there is no reason to say: “I associate myself with this, I agree with so-and-so.” This seems to me a path that will not sever all our ties to the past. Therefore I do not wish to associate myself with anyone, even though EVDOKIMOV has given a fuller account of everything that actually occurred than anyone else.

SAFAROV stated in his testimony and said here at the trial that the Zinovievite opposition was the dregs of the White Guard émigrés. He was wrong. It is not so. What we had here cannot be compared to the dregs of the White Guard émigrés: things are better there. I did not know much of what I heard at this trial. I knew nothing of any negotiations with MRACHKOVSKY, SMIRNOV and many others; although I was close to EVDOKIMOV, I did not even know BRAVO, who visited EVDOKIMOV every day. On the other hand, I heard for the first time here such claims as that we joyfully seized on every setback. I do not subscribe to that: it is slander against me. I believe ANISHEV said it. Or: “We waited for the Party to break its neck,” “we staked our hopes on the failure of Soviet power”; such statements are slander, at least concerning me. And if there were people among us who staked their work on the failure of Soviet power, they never spoke of it anywhere, even by implication. If they say it, they can only be shot. I shall also make an assessment of myself and what I deserve.

What did we have here, and why, as I now understand it, was the Zinovievite organization worse than the White Guard émigrés? At least the latter openly said they opposed Soviet power and the Party. What did we have here? Double duplicity, triple duplicity. We not only deceived the Party and opposed it, but it now emerges that a number of our leaders concealed from their closest comrades, who followed them on various questions, some of the vilest counterrevolutionary negotiations. These stemmed directly from ZINOVIEV's entire former position, from what he said at the Fourteenth Party Congress on the faction, when he spoke of the need to draw absolutely everyone close…

That is why, when I read in the indictment that ZINOVIEV, whom I had followed, merely stood with bowed head, or that domestic circumstances had prevented KAMENEV — the leader KAMENEV, whom I did not meet — from breaking his connection with ZINOVIEV, I could not help feeling deeply indignant. KAMENEV stresses his utter sincerity here and pulls from his waistcoat pocket a new piece of information, the latest and devastating one, about ZINOVIEV. I do not believe that it is the last piece of news; I believe no one. I think that after what I have heard here, new revelations may appear again and again, that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV may have further revelations, and perhaps others as well. After everything one hears, no one can be believed. I give my opinion. It is for the court to believe me or not.

CHAIRMAN: But tell us about yourself, too. So far you have not spoken about yourself. You are speaking as though you were a witness.

SAKHOV: No, I am not speaking as a witness, for I shall answer for all this. Allow me to continue.

CHAIRMAN: Keep closer to your own part in this. You wanted to speak about your part, but you are criticizing and defending yesterday's speakers.

SAKHOV: I shall speak. I say this simply because I regard it as everyone's duty to characterize what is here and how it should be understood. And I understand what has happened here as a display, in essence, of an amalgam of cowardice, duplicity, vileness and baseness of the lowest order,

That is the first question. I want to speak here only of my connections with those sitting in the dock. It seems to me that Ivan TARASOV spoke more honestly and directly than the others, and a number of his positions on matters of principle seem correct. I shall not repeat them. GERTIK said here that after his return from exile all the Zinovievites visited his apartment and he told them all about his testimony to the GPU. I did not visit GERTIK's apartment after he came back from exile, and GERTIK did not tell me personally about that testimony. As for the other defendants, apart from TARASOV I know no one in the so-called active Leningrad group and see them here for the first time. Perhaps I saw them briefly in 1927–1928. In the so-called active Moscow group I never exchanged two words with FAIVILOVICH. I met BRAVO at EVDOKIMOV's apartment. I do not know who else is here. As for members of the center, I met Grigory FEDOROV more than once. I cannot recall a single anti-Party conversation with him in the past four years since we met at GERTIK's apartment; there were none, and I maintain that there were none.

As to my own part, what was my part? I consider that I told the investigator, without sufficient thought and in a politically incorrect manner, that my active role and work in the opposition, as I said earlier, lasted until 1931, that is, the period when I met with several comrades in what was essentially a political club of Zinovievites that met on the Arbat, for my participation in which I assume full responsibility. I think it would be more correct to say that I bear full responsibility, from beginning to end, for everything that happened in the Zinovievite opposition, whether I participated actively or passively at any given time. For a simple reason: if I did not announce that I was leaving, if I did not tell the Party about everything that had happened, that means I did not break away; I retained ties, deceived myself, and went on hiding my head under my wing like an ostrich, even though for several years I sincerely convinced myself that I no longer had anything in common with the opposition. What did my work consist of? Especially at the beginning, I engaged in and maintained counterrevolutionary conversations against the Party. I mean the period when we met in apartments, particularly at Artiom's place. Thereafter the number of meetings decreased considerably. There were conversations, but they became more occasional and isolated. I continued, in one way or another, to maintain regular links with certain comrades, with EVDOKIMOV and KUKLIN. I shall not speak of personal relations, which played a major role, but that is not the point and is not what this trial is about.

Second: I categorically reject the assertion that I deliberately gathered any material, for example, for the opposition. There was no such case. But in conversations I talked and blurted out what I knew from my work, what I sometimes heard, and what I had seen myself. My misfortune was that I saw EVDOKIMOV and KUKLIN above all as comrades, old Bolsheviks whose Party seniority was practically equal to my age.

Third: the question whether I spread counterrevolutionary rumors or engaged in such conversations. I maintain that there were no such cases except for conversations with these people and perhaps on isolated occasions with one or another representative of the former Leningrad opposition whom I happened to meet. The next question concerns my trips to Leningrad. I have been to Leningrad more times than all the Muscovites present here put together. I went to Leningrad more than thirty times during these years. I categorically maintain that I never went there on factional or opposition business. It is enough to say that on all those thirty or more visits I never saw a single member of the so-called “Leningrad center”; not only did I not see them, but I tried to avoid certain meetings. The only place I visited — speaking of oppositionists — was the home of my wife's brother, an old friend whom I had known since 1916, the former oppositionist SHTEINBERG, now deceased. Perhaps there was talk about him, perhaps claims that he was in the opposition in recent years, but this was not true. What I am about to say will count against me, but I do not want to lie about a dead man. I maintain that in recent years SHTEINBERG took no part in the Zinovievite opposition and, far from taking part, criticized ZINOVIEV and said that he was no leader.

I know people may object to this because he appears on the list of oppositionists, but I state what actually happened. He repeatedly told me: “Give up all these meetings; they will lead to nothing good.”

I declare that since I did not break away, since I continued meeting them, since I talked about various matters, since at one time I engaged in counterrevolutionary conversations, since I visited ZINOVIEV (I believe I went there twice), I will not repeat all the conversations, for I have written them down; I know I bear full responsibility for all this.

As for trips to other cities, I visited a number of places where there were oppositionists, but I did not see them. For example, when I was in Voronezh I did not see RAVICH, although RAVICH and SARKIS were\<both\> there; I did visit Sarkis.

I was in Rostov in 1930, 1931 and 1932. In 1931 I saw DMITRIEV there and spoke about my views, which mainly came down to bringing ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV and EVDOKIMOV into leading positions. I confirm that I never thought of that as organizing a coup, a palace coup or any other kind. I maintain that I conceived it as involving them in Party work. That is how I understood it. But for everything that happened to me, and the way it happened, I declare once again that I bear full responsibility if I did not speak or declare it, though I knew about all these conversations, as did the others.

The final question on which I wish to dwell briefly, the hardest one, is the murder of KIROV. News of the identity of the killer reached me in the vicinity of the town of Kharovsk \[4\], where I was attending a district congress of Soviets. There I received a newspaper from which the names of those involved in the murder could be learned. I delivered the closing address at the congress (the court may verify this if it considers it necessary; it is a matter of an hour or two), and at that congress I declared that the Zinovievite organization was responsible for the affair from beginning to end (I said this after reading the names of LEVIN and RUMIANTSEV, as I knew the others less well). In my speech there (it was on the twenty-third), I said that ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and all members of the organization bore responsibility for the murder. It is true that I did not mention my own name. I made that statement in Kharovsk.

At the same time, when I arrived in Moscow I went to see AKULOV and VYSHINSKY, and later spoke here with EZHOV. During those first two days I could not say it; I could not utter the words “double-dealer and counterrevolutionary” about myself. I am telling it as it was. I could not say it because, one way or another, I did not regard myself as such; more precisely, it was painful to regard myself as such. I could not fully analyze all the circumstances as they had been; I was incapable of doing so.

Some comrades here said that they reassessed their views in prison. There can be no reassessment of views in prison; it is not worth a farthing. A reassessment can come through practical work, when one sees what has been achieved here in 1933 and 1934. I traveled throughout the Union, saw what was happening everywhere, and came to work in the Party, to normal Party work as required, sincerely and wholeheartedly. Yet some feeling continued to draw me toward personal connections, which invariably became, or could become, political meetings whenever we met. That is how it was in fact. Otherwise I could not have produced the work results that I did. I further maintain that more than one memorandum was written to the Politburo, both earlier and now, on matters I had examined and reported. I maintain that in no case was there any falsehood or exaggeration. That was the case. In particular, when the latest memoranda were written and I brought the material here twice, on those visits I called on no one\{. No one and\}\*,\* frankly, especially lately, it personally seemed to me that, even if it was not honest, I could quietly withdraw rather than do what was called “informing,” against which my liberal intellectual soul rebelled, without openly declaring this to the Party. I am working honestly now, and the past remains the past. It turned out that things do not work that way. I now conclude my statement with this (if there are questions, I shall answer them at the court's order and direction). I want only to say that I personally want to live and work; I want to show and prove in practice that I can work and carry out what the Party demands, what STALIN demands, and do so honestly and sincerely. I declare that this is so. If the court concludes that I am not sincere even now, that I am still concealing something — I am not speaking about a number of questions, I am setting out the essentials; more or less is not important, the issue is quality rather than quantity — but if it considers that I am lying even now, speaking untruthfully even now, that in recent years I too have not worked honestly, that I really am an enemy, as one of \{those who\} \*those who\* interrogated me called me, then perhaps, if the court adopts that view, there can be only one punishment for me: execution by shooting.

## EXAMINATION OF WITNESS YAKOV RAFAILOVICH ELKOVICH.

CHAIRMAN: You have been summoned as a witness in this case. What do you know of the counterrevolutionary work of the Moscow Center, the Moscow Center's connections with provincial organizations, the main directives you received from the Moscow Center, and the tactics of the “Moscow Center”?

Witness ELKOVICH: Of course, I am not appearing here today as an outsider witness. Having been an active participant in the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization throughout its existence, I myself shall soon face stern accountability for my gravest crimes against Soviet power.

But the fact that, from my very first statements upon arriving in Leningrad, I was merciless toward myself and others in exposing the vile counterrevolutionary role of Zinovievism entitles me to assure the court that today I shall be just as truthful and unsparing, even though those being tried today include many who were my closest friends throughout those years.

Throughout its existence, the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization educated all its members in the spirit of the most unrestrained hostility and hatred toward the leadership of the Party and the country, particularly Comrade STALIN.

The shot fired on December 1 by NIKOLAEV, a member of the Zinovievite organization, was the direct result and immediate consequence of that slanderous and hateful policy toward the Part\<y\> leadership, which was continually cultivated, instilled, and incited among the Zinovievite organization's members. Discussion of every issue, of any fundamental question of Party and Soviet policy, among the organization's members was accompanied by the most malicious attacks on the leadership. All this was combined with malicious relish, uncontrolled inflation, and monstrous exaggeration of the difficulties of soc\<ialist\> construction, turning the Zinovievite organization, of which I too am an active participant, into a kind of internal White emigration.

It was not easy for me to say these words. But I give this characterization with full conviction of its correctness. I have honestly thought through to the end the shameful path of counterrevolutionary transformation and degeneration that I traveled together with the Zinovievite organization. The campaign of slander, hatred, and hostility toward the leadership was headed and organized by ZINOVIEV.

I remember my repeated meetings and conversations with ZINOVIEV in 1929, 1930, 1931, and 1932. That entire campaign of slander and hatred was led by ZINOVIEV and conducted under the banner of accusing the Party leadership as a whole of empiricism, of lacking Marxist-Leninist foresight, and thus of “costing the country dearly.”

These conversations were taking place as early as 1929–1930 in connection with collectivization as well. This slander against the Party leadership, above all against STALIN, ran like a thread through all the counterrevolutionary activity of the Zinovievite organization.

In this connection I recall my repeated meetings with EVDOKIMOV in 1931–1932 at the Hotel “Paris,” where he was staying. We talked particularly often about agriculture. Our conversations were accompanied by poisoned, slanderous slogans and charges that the leadership was empiricist, that it was feeling its way blindly. These accusations were discussed with particular intensity among members of the Zinovievite organization. All my conversations with EVDOKIMOV in 1931–1932 centered on this.

EVDOKIMOV devoted special attention to agricultural questions and was regarded as a particular expert on the matter. By analyzing and deliberately selecting and manipulating figures and facts, EVDOKIMOV arrived at the following conclusions: agriculture was inexorably declining; sowing that year (1931–1932) was worse than in previous years; the pace was slower; the collective farms were in very bad shape; collective farmers had no interest in the fate of their farms; livestock farming was being destroyed.

All my meetings with members of the center during that period, especially in 1932, with EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, GORSHENIN, and GERTIK, and all the separate gatherings I shall mention, were permeated by such conversations. During those years, particularly in 1932, the difficulties of the collective-farm movement were monstrously exaggerated, as were, especially, the extent of crop failures in various regions of the Union. I recall one such gathering at BAKAEV's home in 1932. Present were BAKAEV, KOSTINA, myself, GORSHENIN, KOZHURO, ANISHEV, NATANSON, and NAUMOV. It is difficult for me now to reproduce individual remarks precisely. They were not formal reports; the discussions usually shifted from one topic to another. I remember, in particular, malicious talk about the lag in ferrous metallurgy and the difficulties of mastering new production. But by far the most numerous and most vicious conversations — bourgeois-restorationist, I would now call them — were about collectivization: claims that the collective-farm movement had reached a dead end, been thrown far backward, that collective farmers lacked any personal stake in their farms, and that counterplans had knocked the ground out from under their feet. I emphasize here before the court that I too actively participated in these counterrevolutionary statements.

It was at precisely this time, amid talk and slander about famine, monstrous exaggeration of difficulties, and all sorts of panicky, capitulationist and defeatist claims about the complete collapse of livestock farming, that conversations took place about the possibility of war, at a time when the international situation was sufficiently tense. All this treacherous talk led to the conclusion that, amid famine in certain regions and the alleged lack of any interest among collective farmers in the fate of the collective farms, a peasant would first ask himself whether he would fight. The conclusion was that the peasant probably would not fight. I too repeated this vicious slander against the leadership, this essentially revived Clemenceauist thesis. In particular, I repeated it when I came to Leningrad in late 1932, in conversation with members of the organization ANISHEV and NATANSON. During that trip to Leningrad I had received direct instructions from GORSHENIN to meet ANISHEV and NATANSON because NATANSON was allegedly reproaching ZINOVIEV for his bloc with RYUTIN and STEN. I conducted that conversation on direct instructions. I shall return to it.

In late 1932, before ZINOVIEV was sent to Kustanai, I had numerous meetings and conversations with him. All ZINOVIEV's conversations with me at that time amounted to the claim that the Party and state leadership headed by Comrade STALIN had seized upon the STEN affair as a pretext; that everyone undoubtedly knew about RYUTIN's platform; and that the leadership had used this affair to settle accounts with him and to cover up the most serious agricultural failures. This is ZINOVIEV's almost verbatim expression, which I consider it necessary to reproduce here.

I repeated these assessments by ZINOVIEV when I came to Leningrad in December 1932, in conversations with NATANSON, ANISHEV, and SHURYGIN, members of the organization. In general, everyone knew — and ZINOVIEV above all ought to have known — that every conversation with ZINOVIEV, with other members of the Moscow Center, and with its closest associates was passed on to members of the Zinovievite organization, including those in Leningrad. During those same visits to ZINOVIEV, we discussed the possibility of maintaining contact after he was sent to Kustanai. ZINOVIEV raised the issue roughly as follows: perhaps in certain exceptional cases, if he needed special contacts, would I agree to arrange them, since I was his closest neighbor to Kustanai (I was working in Sverdlovsk throughout). I agreed. I should note that, at our next meeting the following day, apparently after consulting someone, ZINOVIEV asked me to forget this conversation and regard it as though it had never taken place.

In 1933, the incitement of hatred and slander against the Party leadership was conducted mainly through discussion of the Party purge. The purge was depicted as though the Party leadership headed by Comrade STALIN had organized it almost expressly to settle scores with everyone it disliked, above all the Zinovievites. Such talk was literally on the lips of every member of the organization. I recall, in particular, a gathering at which the conversation centered on this question. It was at FEDOROV's home in 1933; GORSHENIN, KUKLIN, GESSEN, and I were present. The purge was discussed in connection with the expulsion of GORSHENIN and FEDOROV, and once again depicted as a reprisal. At the end of 1934 I came to Moscow again. When I met GORSHENIN and KOZHURO, we touched on ZINOVIEV's removal from the journal “Bolshevik.” We, like members of the Zinovievite organization generally, interpreted it as an act of reprisal against ZINOVIEV. Finally, in November 1934, while on leave, I met ZINOVIEV in Kislovodsk. It was an unarranged meeting, but a highly characteristic and revealing one. During a walk, when at my initiative the subject turned to ZINOVIEV's removal from “Bolshevik,” he formulated the matter approximately as follows: ADORATSKY \[8\] had, of course, let him down, but that was not the main thing. According to ZINOVIEV, the essence was that he had gained enormous popularity at “Bolshevik,” that he received large numbers of letters addressed to him there, and that “Bolshevik” had become too large and important a platform for him (its circulation exceeded 500 thous\<and\> copies) for the leadership to leave him there. Hence the pretext, and another reprisal. There is no need to stress here the provocative character that these conversations about reprisals assumed within the Zinovievite organization. NIKOLAEV's shot showed above all the direction in which such provocations led the thinking and aspirations of its members.

I repeat that NIKOLAEV's shot was the natural, inevitable consequence of that entire systematic, persistent, unrelenting campaign to incite hatred toward the leadership, conducted by the Zinovievite organization day after day, year after year. In the overheated atmosphere of a closed organization, so much explosive material accumulated that an explosion was inevitable. Every participant in the Zinovievite organization ought to have known this, and ZINOVIEV above all, for he, more than anyone else, should have remembered the sole historical precedent of this kind.

I mean the murder of members of the Finnish Central Committee that took place in Leningrad \[9\]. It is not an entirely comparable event. The circumstances and significance of Comrade KIROV's murder were incomparably greater and more vile. But there are many similarities between the two cases: behind the murderers of the Finnish Central Committee members, led by RAHJA \[10\], stood agents of the Finnish government; here, behind Comrade KIROV's murderers, stood “the little consul and his big masters,” the imperialists, and the White Guard rabble from Belgrade and Berlin.

I consider it necessary to emphasize, in the degeneration and corruption of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization, the role of a peculiar theory of the chosenness and irreplaceability of Zinovievite cadres. This theory of chosenness ran like a thread through all the organization's counterrevolutionary activity. Every Zinovievite, every active member, was told and had it drummed into him that he was unique and indispensable, the true bearer of Leninist traditions, the only person endowed with Marxist-Leninist foresight; that the flower of the Party was concentrated among us and that each person must save the Party. Hence the conclusion: if you are chosen and indispensable, then everything is permitted. From that to NIKOLAEV's shot is only a short distance. This theory of chosenness became especially clear to me when, before my arrest, I read the article “The Little Consul and His Big Masters” \[11\]. The close kinship between Hitler's theory and the theory of the chosenness of Zinovievite cadres became entirely clear to me.

The counterrevolutionary activity of the Zinovievite cadres was most dangerous because it was hidden from everyone behind a thick, impenetrable veil of political duplicity, refined to an art. The Zinovievite counterrevolutionary organization's duplicity was developed into a coherent system, into a rule for its active members. Only active participants in the organization could have helped uncover and liquidate the entire criminal organization in time. That was not done. As a result, NIKOLAEV's shot rang out.

For my crimes against the Party I shall bear severe responsibility, but today I consider it necessary to help the court and the investigation root out every last remnant of counterrevolutionary Zinovievism, which has degenerated into an internal White emigration.

EXAMINATION

## OF WITNESS MOISEI (MIKHAIL) MARKOVICH KHARITONOV.

CHAIRMAN: The court has decided to question witness KHARITONOV. In this case, you, KHARITONOV, are appearing as a witness. Tell us what you know and remember about the counterrevolutionary work of the so-called “Moscow Center” and its individual members and participants. Concentrate on the principal and most important points.

KHARITONOV: After their admission to the Party (after the Fifteenth Congress), the central group of Zinovievites returned to Moscow from various parts of the Union. This was in the middle of 1928, in the second half of the year. ZINOVIEV convened a meeting at his home, mainly of people who belonged to the center of that counterrevolutionary organization. Present were ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, KUKLIN, SHAROV, BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, the late LASHEVICH, myself, and others. There were about twelve to fifteen people in all.

After all the crimes we had committed, after such an unprecedented, vile counterrevolutionary demonstration as that of November 7, the Party placed the greatest trust in us and admitted us into its ranks. Yet instead of justifying that trust and dissolving the underground organization completely, we left that meeting with ZINOVIEV's directives that underground counterrevolutionary work would continue and that the political center would remain composed of the same people as before the congress and as when it formed part of the united center with the Trotskyists before the Fifteenth Congress. It had become impossible to carry on the struggle as openly as before the congress; it was therefore decided that, while withdrawing into the background, we should preserve the cadres around us, keep in contact with people (mainly in Leningrad), and continue working with a view to further struggle. ZINOVIEV's position and calculations were roughly that open struggle would soon begin owing to major economic and political complications within the country or to disagreements within the Party Central Committee. The picture envisaged was that difficulties in the country would lead to a struggle within the Central Committee, in which case we Zinovievites would be called upon for help.

It was in order to retain cadres of like-minded supporters that ZINOVIEV considered it necessary to preserve the local cells in Leningrad.

From that moment began a period of political double-dealing of the vilest and basest kind. I use the word “base” without hesitation, because the situation was this: on the one hand, articles were written for “Pravda,” without assignment, commission, compulsion, or pressure, entirely voluntarily, expressing solidarity with the Party's general line, approving its leadership, and paying tribute to the wise, skillful, iron leadership of Comrade STALIN; at the same time, an hour earlier or later, or even at that very moment, in the circle of their own associates, precisely the opposite was said and done. The basest and vilest slander imaginable was then put into circulation.

How did things develop afterward? I personally remember two meetings in which I participated, in 1928–1929. There were probably more meetings, but I was not present because in 1929 I spent a considerable part of my time in the countryside, leaving for two or three months at a time. One meeting was connected with TROTSKY's exile. ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV attended, as did SHAROV, I think KUKLIN, EVDOKIMOV, and myself. There were seven or eight of us altogether. ZINOVIEV gave a report on the decision to exile TROTSKY and described that decision in the harshest terms. I remember his using the expression “a spicy dish.” Then BAKAEV took the floor and, partly influenced by that report, proposed that we — that is, ZINOVIEV's group — protest against the exile. I was the first to speak afterward and firmly opposed BAKAEV's proposal.

Among the arguments I advanced was this: how could we stand up for TROTSKY when we did not know what TROTSKY would do tomorrow? By defending him, we would assume full political responsibility for everything he might do tomorrow. Everyone knows that very soon after TROTSKY found himself abroad, he and his group became the vanguard of international counterrevolution. SHAROV supported me and, unless I am mistaken, ZINOVIEV also inclined to this position. So no decision was made.

At another meeting, approximately in early 1929, which I attended, the composition was the same. Someone arrived from Leningrad and reported with satisfaction that work there was about to resume, connections were being restored, and, most importantly, the two wings of the Zinovievites were drawing closer together: the wing which had broken with ZINOVIEV during the Fifteenth Congress and followed SAFAROV, the so-called “leaderless,” and the wing which had followed ZINOVIEV. It was said that contacts were being established district by district, with one person from each side, in a “chain” arrangement, and the names of those who would direct this work were mentioned. As far as I remember, Vladimir LEVIN and Moisei YAKOVLEV were named on one side, and Vladimir RUMYANTSEV on the other. With a few exceptions, the participants in the meeting welcomed the news. ZINOVIEV was particularly pleased and later cited the example to the Muscovites: “Look at you Muscovites (that is, those who live in Moscow and have connections with Moscow) — you do nothing, while the Leningrad opposition continues to work.” ZINOVIEV held RUMYANTSEV in especially high esteem, regarding him as very capable, determined, energetic, a man of action rather than words. I did not know RUMYANTSEV personally and saw him only once, but I remember the impression I formed of him from those conversations.

The rest I can relate only from personal conversations with ZINOVIEV, since I did not attend further meetings, or at any rate do not remember them. But I had quite a number of conversations with him afterward, in 1929 and also in 1930. I remember ZINOVIEV speaking about the return of former oppositionists to Leningrad. This was in 1930 or perhaps even early 1931; I cannot establish the precise date. He regarded the Leningraders' return to Leningrad very positively, because it meant the accumulation of forces. One must bear in mind that ZINOVIEV's only, or virtually principal, hope for the future lay in Leningrad: by then Moscow had almost no connections; beyond that political counterrevolutionary center, the Muscovites had scarcely any connections in Moscow, whereas in Leningrad there were contacts in the districts, with individuals, and so forth.

In our conversations ZINOVIEV discussed political developments inside the country, rural questions, chiefly collectivization, grain procurements, the state of livestock breeding, and so on. We generally spoke alone, though occasionally someone who often visited ZINOVIEV was present, such as BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, or KUKLIN. ZINOVIEV's mood in 1929–1930 was not always the same, but hostility toward the whole policy and the entire Party line predominated. Even when ZINOVIEV had a moment of clarity and approved the Party line, he invariably turned at the end of the conversation to its leadership, and then attacks, condemnation, and slander emerged in their most striking form — the vilest slander, as I said at the outset. Once I asked ZINOVIEV directly: “You have spent fifteen minutes discussing the Party line with me and approving it, and then, when you turn to the leadership, you deliver such devastating criticism, such a sharply negative assessment, that the two things simply cannot be reconciled.” ZINOVIEV usually replied: “All this costs the country far too dearly.”

I have asked myself before, later, and especially now why ZINOVIEV, in our conversations, shifted increasingly from criticism of the general line to personal attacks and slander against the Party leadership. I explain it this way: the further we moved away from the Fifteenth Congress, the harder it became to attack the Party line, because the facts of life, everything happening in the country, and the victories already achieved on the economic front made attacks on the Party line increasingly difficult, especially when talking to a person who was participating in the struggle himself and was to some extent caught up in the enthusiasm of construction. Hence the center of gravity of anti-Party activity shifted toward slander against the Party leadership, and if even a small flame of anti-Party attitudes still burned in ZINOVIEV's interlocutor, ZINOVIEV always tried to fan and enlarge that flame; the best method was a system of slander. For a time ZINOVIEV had varying moods, when, as it seemed to me, he had moments of clarity regarding the Party and its leadership. Once he burst out: “How long are we going to put up with this? Are we Bolsheviks, damn it, or what are we?” I remember that phrase because it was the last impulse that finally pushed me away from him, for I realized that I was moving around a very dangerous man.

What is the political assessment of all this?

Double-dealing and the malicious, vile, base slander spread within that circle, principally by ZINOVIEV, could produce nothing in minds such as those of RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV, and others already hostile to the Party except the nurturing of fascist and terrorist sentiments and designs. It is no accident that this group gave birth to the vile, provocative “idea” they carried out on December 1 of last year.

And if we tell the truth — and we are obliged to tell the court the whole truth to the end, however bitter it may be for all of us — then it must be said that there is a direct thread between ZINOVIEV's apartment and the shot of December 1, if not a physical one, then a political one. However bitter and painful this fact may be, it remains, and none of us can escape it. Political responsibility must fall on all those who, from 1928 onward, continued counterrevolutionary work and spun a web of exceptionally base slander against the Party leadership and its individual representatives.

I do not remember whether I said this to ZINOVIEV personally or to someone else in the group, but when they said that the Party line was correct but its leadership was unfit, I told them that this position did not differ from MILYUKOV's during the Kronstadt uprising, when he raised the slogan “For Soviets, but without Bolsheviks.” MILYUKOV understood perfectly well that, if the Bolsheviks were overthrown, the Soviets would disappear as well. The position “for the general line, but against the leadership” contained the same rot and falsity, a continuation of the counterrevolutionary struggle against both the general line and the Party leadership. I once visited ZINOVIEV's apartment, I think in 1931, and he showed me an article he had written on fascism, which he had also shown to others. On the whole I liked it, made a few minor comments, and asked why he did not send it to “Pravda” or “Bolshevik.” He answered literally: “You are naive. Do you think the Party Central Committee and Comrade STALIN do not know what is happening in Germany? Can they not see that the fascists are coming to power, yet are taking no measures?” (The article was in the nature of a directive.) “If I push forward with this article, they will say, 'Where do you think you are barging in? Nobody asked for your advice,' and it will cause nothing but irritation.”

That is approximately what I can remember.

I emphasize, some justification, because I am answerable not for 1934, but for nine years — more than half of the whole post-October period, nine years out of seventeen. Therefore I shall not speak in my defense, but to accuse myself.

Here I must first declare to the court that I do not associate myself with EVDOKIMOV's speech, and not for the reason SAKHOV gives, but because I consider it insufficient and not in agreement with the Party's assessment of us.

It seems to me that two points are missing from EVDOKIMOV's speech. There is no assessment of our leaders. We are now defendants, not delegates at a Party congress. We do not grasp this firmly enough.

Why, then, am I making such an extraordinarily weighty declaration, for the first time in my life? Why do I plead guilty? Because I, among others, was one link in the chain along which inciting hatred of the Party leaders, and especially of Comrade KIROV, was passed.

The organization of the Moscow Center led directly to NIKOLAEV's revolver.

That is why I plead guilty. If I knew that this guilt was not of an inciting character and that I had not myself shown initiative in the matter, I would say that it was not my fault and that I did not accept that guilt.

It seems to me that ZINOVIEV wants to confuse the court on this matter.

You, ZINOVIEV, say that the organization's assessment of KIROV changed over the past years. I maintained connections with LEVIN, SAFAROV, and RUMYANTSEV. I personally believe that there were no two organizations, that I belonged not to two organizations but to one. Everyone from ZINOVIEV down to NIKOLAEV belonged to one Zinovievite organization. When ZINOVIEV says in his testimony, “they are scoundrels,” that term belongs to the realm of morality, not to politics and judicial responsibility. The hardest thing for me was to recognize that I was guilty before the Party and the country. I came to understand this gradually, in stages, but before my arrest. That is how I understand my first words at this trial, when I said I pleaded guilty.

That is the first point. And by the successes of socialist construction — that is the second. In other words, we were defeated as an unprincipled group of enemies of socialist construction. And when KAMENEV corrected me here, asking where this reliance on difficulties, on uprisings, and other things had been, I insist again: KAMENEV knew that we counted on difficulties, that we saw in them a means of carrying out our program.

What kind of difficulties were we counting on? STALIN's personal difficulties, or difficulties for Soviet power?

Some minor commodity missing from the market? That was not what we meant. We meant difficulties that might result in a rupture of the alliance between the working class and the peasantry, giving rise to peasant uprisings. And secondly, as FEDOROV said, a rupture of the bond between the working class and the Party.

As a result, defeatist attitudes regarding the military defense of the Union. That conclusion must be acknowledged.

I also believe the matter must be formulated this way, and this is how I formulate it: I was an enemy of socialism, of socialist construction. That says everything. Should we talk about subjective and objective attitudes and experiences? Let the psychologists deal with that.

When I say that we have been defeated, I do not mean that the Party can now lay down its arms. I think there is one more thing we must say here. We have been utterly defeated. But in the country and in the Party there still remain remnants of our organization, hidden Trotskyists, Rightists, and many others with whom we tried, in one form or another, to form blocs. I am finishing now. In saying all this, I must give the following assessment: it is perfectly clear to me that, whatever the court's verdict, the mark of counterrevolution is on me and I shall never again belong to the Party. Yet I feel myself being cleansed of the filth in which I was immersed all that time, and this eases my burden and enables me to face firmly what awaits me.

It is shameful, but I must say that only in prison did I comprehend the full depth of my downfall, and not even then immediately. The first feeling that seized me was resentment, and that resentment grew into bitterness.

I was inclined to explain my arrest as the settling of scores with old political opponents. I had to sit in prison, think, and analyze my past in order to understand how low I had fallen and that my entire past over the previous nine years had conditioned the tragic event in Leningrad, the bloody denouement.

I am dissatisfied with my testimony before the court. It may have given the impression that I tried to establish shades of difference in the degree of responsibility for all the years in which I belonged to the Zinovievite center. Here ANISHEV flung in my face the grave accusation of bestial hatred toward STALIN. ANISHEV did not offer a single concrete fact in support of that accusation. I assure the court it is untrue. My entire past, everything best in that past, is connected personally with STALIN. That is how it was. Both the prisons (Andrei VYSHINSKY remembers this) \[15\] and the illegal Party work are connected with him.

These are my best memories.

At the most heated moments of the factional struggle, when demagoguery often served as one of the strongest arguments against opponents, I never allowed myself attacks on STALIN.

What brought me before the bar of a proletarian court? I shall explain it by two reasons:

1\) fanatical anti-Party struggle (which I waged beginning with the Fourteenth Party Congress, when all Party boundaries were cast aside, when we set both feet on the road toward a second party and had, in fact, already adopted the methods and forms of political struggle that was essentially anti-Soviet. There is no point in citing specific facts. They are clear to everyone (November 7, illegal meetings, and so forth);

2\) blind and boundless faith in the leaders. I knew ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV long before 1917. I knew of them before I met them. I learned Bolshevism from their writings in Social Democratic and other Party publications. I believed in them without reservation. I was accustomed to regarding them as unquestionable authorities. I considered them pupils of LENIN.

I want to say that I was mistaken. LENIN never taught us to do what ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV did.

During the investigation I learned that KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV began negotiations with the Rightists about a bloc almost on the \<on\>second day after the Fifteenth Congress. They told me they met with the Rightists because that was the only window through which one could see what was happening in the Central Committee and its Politburo.

Our leaders, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, had in fact already begun bargaining with the Rightists (the very issue over which the inner-Party struggle with the Leningrad Opposition had especially begun), in effect forming a bloc with them and supporting all the capitulationist ideas leading to the restoration of capitalism in our country. LENIN did not teach us that.

Our leaders were exposed as political profiteers, ever ready to bargain away ideas at any moment, if only they could get their hands on the helm of the country's political life.

This is the conclusion I must draw before the court.

I was mistaken about the leaders; I was mistaken in my attitude toward the Party leadership. I committed a most grievous crime against the party of the working class and its government. I ask to be given an opportunity to prove through deeds that I can devote all my remaining strength to the cause of proletarian revolution.

GORSHENIN: Once again, I plead guilty to the crimes charged against me in the indictment.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant GESSEN.

GESSEN: I plead guilty to the full extent of the charges brought against me and acknowledge my share of responsibility for everything that occurred.

To determine my personal share of responsibility \<in\>the entire course of events, I must make one observation:

The investigative materials contain different testimonies concerning me. In some lists of the center's membership, submitted by defendants in this case, I appear as a member of that center. In other lists I am described as an active Zinovievite closely connected with the center. In ZINOVIEV's testimony I appear as someone close to the center. This discrepancy is understandable, since there were no formal criteria for membership in the center and interpretations may differ. To move this question from the formal-organizational sphere into that of political responsibility, I must make the following statement to the court:

I acknowledge that almost every time I was in Moscow I took part in the center's work or met with members of the center and participated in discussions of the Zinovievite organization's tactics and activities.

I acknowledge that, whenever I came to Moscow, I learned from members of the center everything that had occurred between my visits and, consequently, although I may not have known certain details, even important ones, all the center's principal political attitudes, decisions, and directives were known to me.

I acknowledge that on particular occasions, enumerated in my statements during the preliminary investigation, I acted as the center's official representative, carrying out its assignments.

Finally, I acknowledge that the very limitations on my ability to participate in the work of the “Moscow Center,” whether on its behalf or under its instructions, arose from circumstances beyond my control: my absence from Moscow.

All this compels me to recognize that, politically speaking, however the court may ultimately decide this secondary, formal question, I must bear the same responsibility as a number of my comrades in this case who have been recognized as members of the “Moscow Center.”

I must make one correction to my testimony during the preliminary investigation, not with regard to factual content but to my interpretation of the course of events. In my statements I especially stressed, returning repeatedly to the point, that in 1933–34 neither I nor those members of the center whom I met—EVDOKIMOV, GORSHENIN and others—had any practical directives to renew the struggle against the Party; that our conversations were simply “domestic” gatherings of like-minded people who could not talk freely elsewhere.

I do not now dispute my assertion from a factual standpoint, but politically the entire connection between events appears different to me. During the preliminary investigation I thought NIKOLAEV's shot was the result of the action of a force we had set in motion earlier, up to the end of 1932, and that thereafter continued operating automatically without us. Although I did not conclude that this relieved us of responsibility, it nevertheless seemed some sort of moral consolation that toward the end we set ourselves no immediate practical tasks and were, frankly speaking, merely gossiping in a domestic setting.

I now think that matters were altogether different and that perhaps the most harmful period of our activity, for which we must bear the heaviest responsibility, was precisely the last two years.

Previously I put the question like this: I had not met Vladimir RUMYANTSEV for about five years, had not seen Ivan KOTOLYNOV for about six, and had not seen Sergei MANDELSHTAM for a very long time either. Consequently, the ideological developments they experienced proceeded independently of me and of those with whom I maintained regular contact. Now I think differently. There was a link between us and them. I talked in Moscow with EVDOKIMOV, GORSHENIN or others, and given the close interweaving of our connections, those intimate, domestic, almost family relationships within our milieu that have been sufficiently revealed here at the trial, these conversations reached—could not fail to reach—RUMYANTSEV, MANDELSHTAM and others through a succession of intermediaries. They knew that we remained bitterly hostile to the Party leadership. They also knew that we had set ourselves no practical tasks. But when these conversations, with precisely both sides of the matter, traveled from EVDOKIMOV's apartment in Moscow to RUMYANTSEV's apartment in Leningrad, when they reached people of a different disposition, temperament and past, the same words began to sound different. What must they have said to themselves, hearing what we said among ourselves? In the immediate circle of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, people say that everything asserted before remains true, yet the “old men” offer no prospects and draw no practical conclusions; then people with a different disposition began to seek practical conclusions themselves, to pose the question their own way, in the way that was realized on December 1.

That is why I believe it is no salvation for us that we set ourselves no practical tasks. All this prolonged, interminable counterrevolutionary cud-chewing of ours, however purposeless and fruitless, coupled with the complete absence of political prospects, paths or ways out, was the most poisonous influence imaginable; perhaps precisely the combination of these two factors makes clearest what happened here in Leningrad on December 1.

I must dissociate myself from some assertions made by several of my fellow defendants. Some say it was only at the trial that they first heard about the talks with LOMINADZE and SHATSKI\<N\>; others were indignant that meetings with I. N. SMIRNOV and MRACHKOVSKY took place without their knowledge.

Such assertions were made even by certain members of the center or people close to it, from which they concluded that not only relations with the Party but also relations within the Zinovievite organization rested on deception, that the leaders deceived even their closest adherents. That is untrue. I do not say this to justify the leaders of the Zinovievite group, nor to suggest my comrades are deceiving the court. I think something else: they are deceiving themselves. Just think! Was everyone aware of BUKHARIN's conversation with KAMENEV? They were. Since when? Since January 1929 at the latest—exactly six years ago. Was it common knowledge that the platform written by Ryutin was immediately passed through STEN into ZINOVIEV's hands? It was. Since when? Since December 1932. You knew that, comrades, and drew no conclusions from it.

If you knew there were forms of rapprochement with yesterday's opponents, how can you be astonished at the search for rapprochement with yesterday's allies? You knew STEN was a regular visitor to ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, yet now a conversation with SHATSKIN comes as a surprise to you. I declare to the court: I may have been—and was—unaware of certain details, particulars and extremes, of some truly monstrous, openly White-Guard conversations held among individual Zinovievites; but I knew the essential content, the spirit of all the activities of our Zinovievite group. Consequently, I must bear full and complete responsibility, without alleging that anyone deceived me.

All the more firmly, therefore, must I condemn the course adopted by the former leaders of our Zinovievite organization during the preliminary investigation and here, at the judicial inquiry. I understood that their position, like that of all of us, was extremely difficult and hopeless. It is impossible to undo what happened, what came before. But at least I expected that at the very end, here at the dock, they would resemble, if only a little, the men we had once known; that the proletarian revolutionary would awaken in each of them; that they would manage to end their political existence with at least some dignity. I cannot refrain from condemning such things as ZINOVIEV's attempt to stratify the Zinovievite organization into the young “leaderless” group and our principal group of “23.” Factually it is true; I shall not argue—such strata did exist. Politically, however, it is fundamentally false. We are not at a meeting of the Zinovievite center discussing our organization's internal, domestic affairs. The question has been placed on political ground, before the eyes of the whole world, after all. And on that world stage to raise the question that RUMYANTSEV was putting on airs within the Zinovievite organization, that he went to neither EVDOKIMOV's nor BAKAEV's home, and that this somehow explains what occurred—I cannot recognize in this a single ounce of political dignity. We had our internal frictions, certainly. But whose young people were they? Whose offspring? Whose progeny? Who brought them up? We did. It was in January 1925, during the Party Central Committee plenum, that we began assembling the first young cadres in our organization—TARASOV, FAIVILOVICH, RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV and oth\<ers\>. Ten years—an “anniversary” indeed. Who led them to the demonstration on November 7, 1927? We did. After the Fifteenth Congress, when they first became entangled with SAFAROV, who taught them they could return to the Party without reconsidering their convictions, that the Party could be deceived? That was our idea—we taught them. From whom did they receive their political judgments and attitudes during the last period, even if these were sometimes distorted or altered as they passed through intermediaries? They drew that nourishment from us. These are the young people we brought up and for whom we must accept full political responsibility.

I cannot recognize even one ounce of political dignity in KAMENEV's statement that he was guilty of blindness, that he failed to notice the Moscow Center meeting in his own apartment.

I shall not enter into any further polemic with him; I do not know who would need such a polemic now, or why. I shall say just a few words in conclusion.

I wanted to say this yesterday after giving my testimony; then postponed it until today and thought of doing it now. I wanted to offer, if only in brief, an analysis and assessment of what the group or organization of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV had been as a weapon of counterrevolution, as a magnet for all counterrevolutionary forces inside and outside the Party, as a breeding ground for NIKOLAEVs and others like him. I have decided against doing so. What restrains me is not only that so much has already been said here on the subject and that the court has no interest in hearing the same thing repeated. Something else restrains me. The court has the right to ask me a simple but merciless question: “Why have you realized all this only now? Why did it take the affair of December 1 and, most shameful of all, a month in solitary confinement for the light of awareness to dawn on you? Why have you now begun making the speeches we are hearing?”

Not only the court but others elsewhere could ask me that question. Over the past month I have thought more than once that, for men in our position, solitary confinement is a salvation. What would we be doing under the present political circumstances if we were free? The Party and the workers would put that question to us, not in the calm judicial tone used here, but more inexorably, harshly and sharply still.

When I think that I shall be asked this simple but inexorable question—why have you, a man not old but middle-aged, with two decades of participation in the political movement behind you, only now and under these circumstances arrived at these ideas?—I can find no remotely satisfactory answer, either for the court, for the Party or even for myself.

That is all I can say.

In the October days and during wartime the Party sent me to do the highly responsible work of organizing uniforms for our army. I carried out this task brilliantly. During the subsequent years of reconstruction our Party placed me in a trust in Leningrad, where I also fulfilled my duties and the Party directive, for which I was rewarded with a personal letter from Comrade LENIN.

Comrades judges, when deciding upon the crimes I committed over these last eight years, I ask you to consider my first twenty-two years, my militant, revolutionary work in the Party and among the working class.

Comrades judges, my guilt is grave; I am a criminal.

This takes me away from the general subject, but I think it is better to address these incidents nevertheless.

The first concerns how I reacted (and I was not alone) to my removal from the editorial board of Bolshevik. The matter has been discussed here in part. But it also has a purely theoretical side with which it would be superfluous to occupy the court. STALIN taught me—and through me many others—a new lesson, a major lesson in Marxist theory and history, concerning how one of the founders of scientific communism, ENGELS, viewed the question of war at different times in his life, particularly toward his death, and what LENIN added to that teaching. Instead of understanding the importance of that lesson, I immediately, and of course KAMENEV along with me, began seeking other explanations and leafing through LENIN's old writings to find passages where LENIN treated the matter differently. It is true that I did not speak of this during the investigation. It is true that in this question, too, I must say I was completely wrong.

The second matter is more important. It concerns my assessments of TROTSKY's writings. Recently I became particularly familiar with the works of the counterrevolutionary TROTSKY published abroad. To avoid misunderstandings, insignificant though the point may be in view of all the charges hanging over me, I must emphasize that I read these materials entirely legally, not in some hiding place but at the LENIN Institute, where the material was always made available to me. I chiefly consulted foreign-language materials there and later Russian ones, and recently became interested in them after learning that TROTSKY and his group had entered the Second International. As I read all this, even during the reading, I shared my views with KAMENEV. Our conversations were almost a matter of thinking aloud and, if someone tried to portray them in any fashion, volumes would have to be written. In those conversations our assessment was the same as the one we generally gave of TROTSKY.

That is why I think that, small though the matter is, it is still worth mentioning because it has some historical significance. The assessment was this: here is a man who did not wish it but has ended up in the counterrevolution and is now objectively engaged in counterrevolutionary work; we always said this, and I say it here too. Yet we would add: at the same time he says many correct things in his criticism. And we savored them. I say the same thing about this instance. I regarded TROTSKY's and his group's entry into the Second International as a political finale of the utmost disgrace for him, but at the same time I spread slander against the Party by asking: “Well, what does this mean? Why is there silence about it here? Why not speak openly?” I insinuated that the silence arose from the desire to establish a united front with the French at all costs. You know my positions on this from the testimony of others and from my own testimony. I shall not elaborate further. What has already been said is quite enough.

Now I want to say a few concluding words \<about\> what you have had to listen to here. Of course I cannot conceal anything essential in what was said here, and only a number of details were new to me. But these are details of colossal importance. When, for example, TARASOV said yesterday that several years ago he had heard from people whose names meant nothing either to the court or to me—ordinary oppositionists, young people from the provinces—that they spoke about dynamite even at a stage of the struggle when it had not acquired the scope and importance that it has now; when he described the mentality of some representatives of the youth in Leningrad itself, it was precisely these points that made it clearest to me that I had no right to make the reservation or reproach that I made yesterday concerning the wording of the indictment.

It has become painfully and concretely clear to me what these transmission mechanisms were between our center and, above all, myself, and people less experienced, less hardened in politics, sometimes only touching it on the surface, young people who naturally understood the matter in their own way.

I am profoundly, terribly sorry that my attempt yesterday to describe the “leaderless” group is being interpreted as an attempt to cast off responsibility and so forth. Yesterday I said perfectly clearly that of course I bear political, moral and legal responsibility for them and cannot fail to bear it; nevertheless I wanted this subgroup to be known as a subgroup, this chapter as a separate chapter. These are particulars compared with everything we have heard here.

Shivers run through my body and my hair stands on end when I see what company I am keeping, what sorts of people have crept in here, what company I have landed among. There is nothing I can say in my defense. It would be ridiculous to plead lack of political experience, ridiculous to plead youth, ridiculous to plead insufficient warnings from the Party. That would be ridiculous and criminal, and it is at this very moment that I experience my greatest punishment, seeing with my own eyes, seeing clearly where we have arrived.

Is it accidental that the moment of our trial, the moment of our inglorious political end, coincides, if one considers the country's fate as a whole, with the moment of its greatest rise, flourishing, joy of life, the introduction of truly new millions upon millions to the new socialist culture, to life and construction? Of course it is no accident. One followed from the other. And it was precisely because we were utterly wrong from our very first assessments, precisely because we sank deeper and deeper, precisely because we were drawn ever further into the counterrevolutionary current, becoming the mouthpiece of anti-socialist, anti-proletarian forces, that things had to turn out this way. As everything rose higher and higher, we rolled further and further down into this abyss. That is precisely why our outcome has taken this form. It will stand out especially clearly in the consciousness of workers, collective farmers and the proletariat throughout the world.

I saw in the newspapers and heard here at the trial that abroad, in bourgeois countries, defenders of ours, so to speak, are emerging. Well, it is clear from what circles they come: circles that do not care what they shoot with, so long as they shoot against Soviet power. They range as far as the fascists and, of course, the ranks of the social fascists and, as I saw in a Pravda editorial, Trotsky himself.

Even without me, the Party will know how to kick all this rabble aside and put it in its place. But if my voice could reach anywhere, I would naturally say that our defense by these people, parties and elements merely underlines once again, in italics, just how far we have fallen, into what counterrevolutionary pit.

If our trial becomes known to the broad working masses (I do not know how that will happen, though I think it probably will), I want everyone who reads or hears of it to know that I, the principal defendant, at the very last moment saw with absolute clarity, fully and completely, \*and acknowledged before the proletarian court fully and completely\* all my guilt; I saw and acknowledged fully and completely that my path from the moment I set myself against the Party was anti-proletarian, anti-Soviet and counterrevolutionary.

I want everyone who becomes acquainted with this and asks, “And what did the principal defendant say?” to know that I said it of my own free will, after mature reflection, after many painful hesitations, after tens of thousands of attempts to find some shadow of justification. I did not dwell and shall not dwell today on such elementary questions as our being wrong about industrialization, collectivization, inner-Party democracy and so forth. Every Young Pioneer now understands that. And I share, in the main, what EVDOKIMOV said on those questions.

I considered it my task to recount the history of how I and those who believed in me came to this downfall. That is why my statement yesterday took the form of a long account, delving into my own psychology at different moments in the struggle.

I am finishing, and want to say this: when I reflect upon my life, it occurs to me that, taken as a whole, I have lived three lives. The proletarian court knows my biography very well. I shall not dwell on it.

Life has been lived. One life was my youth before joining the Party; another was my first acquaintance with Vladimir Ilyich (83–901–903); another life was the period from joining the Party until 1924.

And the third life. Here it is quite appropriate to put that word in quotation marks. It was this decade, a cursed decade for me, when I strayed from the path, when the noose tightened and then I rushed headlong lower and lower. This is the life that brought me into the position in which I deservedly find myself today.

The only consolation that may remain to me is that second period, of which I sometimes dare to think. That period allowed me to merge with the masses, gave me the chance at times to voice the opinions of the working class. If I had even a small opportunity to begin some fourth life, which is extremely doubtful in every sense, I would try, perhaps I might find the strength, above all through my own terrible example, perhaps unprecedented in a proletarian party until now, to show how one error developed into another, how this grew into an entire system of errors, how errors became crimes, and how crimes became counterrevolution.

It goes without saying that this court's sentence will be the sentence of the working class, of the Party and of the world proletariat; and it goes without saying that, as I wrote and now say, I shall consider that whatever is done to me will be right, just, appropriate and, above all, in the interests of the working class's cause.

As for myself, remembering that second period which I dared mention in your presence, for whatever span of life may be allotted me I shall always remain devoted with all my soul precisely to that second period, when I was not languishing, gossiping or assembling anti-Party cadres, but marching in step with the Party.

The terrible picture of an absence of ideas, of moral destruction, of the severing of every bond with people, not only political but human, the transformation of a political tendency or political group into an assembly of people without principles, who have lost all bearings and all moral steadfastness, into a conglomeration of living corpses—that is the picture we have seen here over these two days. Among these people are some who were once good, honest proletarian revolutionaries, yet we, their leaders, through the poison with which we infected them for ten years, turned this group into the pitiful, utterly rotten filth now represented by the so-called former Zinovievite Opposition. For me the observations of these days were a moral horror equal to what I experienced when I learned that KIROV's murderers were Zinovievites, members of our organization. I saw, and I think everyone saw along with me, how deeply the absence of principles had penetrated, how far moral disintegration had advanced. And those people considered me their leader to the very last moment. They believed I shared their ideas, that their task was to get ZINOVIEV and me into the Politburo! How could we have brought our group to such a downfall? The question of admitting us to the Politburo might still have been an admissible subject of political discussion some ten years ago.

But that all of you sitting in the dock should then have lived with this idea for eight years, devoting all your political energy and political purpose to bringing ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the Politburo, without reflecting on what they could bring to that center of the communist movement—this is such an absence of principle combined with political irresponsibility that the picture says only one thing: this group is rotten through and through, finished.

I said in yesterday's speech that we cannot sum up what happened either by a theory of stages in the history of our counterrevolutionary group, or by separating our subjective wishes from the objective significance of our activities, or through inquiries into personal psychology, of which there was again far too much in ZINOVIEV's speeches. All this can only cast a new fog between the real essence of our activities and what the Party and the proletariat must know about them.

Only history explains the past. The history of our counterrevolutionary group spans ten years. In history ten years is little, but in the life of a revolutionary ten years is a great deal. After all, he may devote two or three decades of work to the revolution and the working class. Therefore those interested in the Party's history, the history of the first country of proletarian revolution and the history of the struggle against the Party and against proletarian revolution must be interested in these ten years of our political biography.

Ten momentous years have passed. I have nothing in those ten years to be proud of, but there is one thing concerning which, it seems to me, I may admit for some inner consolation that I acted correctly. I consider that one glimmer in the decade to be an article of mine in which I believe I was the first to try to demonstrate—and I think did demonstrate—that these ten years were not just any ten years, but a new stage in the history of the proletarian revolution, a new epoch connected with STALIN's name, just as the first ten years were connected with LENIN's epoch \[17\]. This is a new epoch, these last nine years with all their immense world-historical content. The decade unfolded and now ends in these January days of 1935. Our activities unfolded in parallel, and now they are ending too. In my cell I have before me Comrade KUIBYSHEV's report on the results of this epoch.

I do not read it as EVDOKIMOV said he reads KATAEV's novel. This simple, unadorned account reads like a novel far more gripping than all the novels of Leo TOLSTOY and the tragedies of SHAKESPEARE. And so, on one side in my cell lies the indictment in the case of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV's counterrevolutionary group, and on the other this report, these dry statistics, the figures summing up ten years of the Party's work. That is the first result, general and theoretical; the second, practical one, is the corpse of Sergei Mironovich KIROV. Those are the two results. Neither can be disowned. That is the result of our activity: a complete separation from the great construction of socialism, from all the political, economic, cultural and ideological work accomplished by the Party in ten years; complete separation and the transformation of our group into a bucket of human remains, at best the remains of human beings—a bucket of filth.

And alongside it, KIROV's pure blood. These are our results, and they must be spoken of. This will be a reckoning without subjectivism, without the story of what I wanted, how I hesitated, left and came back. It will be the reckoning rendered by ten years of history. Those who were sent to the scaffold during the great French Revolution of the eighteenth century, first the DANTON\<S\>, the Héberts \[18\], then the Robespierres and Saint-Justs, did not have several years in which to demand that their programs and tactics be tested. They were given days and weeks. Those who died in the shootings of the Paris Commune, Blanquists and Marxists who argued among themselves, were not fortunate enough to receive from history a period in which to test their tactics in practice. But we were given such a period—ten years, an enormous span in the history of a revolution. In those ten years we had a chance to show ourselves.

And after those ten years it would not have been surprising to come here with some positive results. But we? We brought a picture of the disintegration of the minds and wills of former proletarian revolutionaries—and KIROV's blood. Those are our only results\! A bucket of our own filth and KIROV's blood\! They cannot be remade. Ten years is an immense span in the history of a revolution, and those years put us to the test. We were tested, and so were those against whom we entered the struggle.

They were tested by gigantic labor, tested not merely by exercising state power along customary paths and in old forms, but through historical action requiring every day, every week and every month changes in the forms of struggle, forms of action and combinations of methods in international and domestic affairs. They were tested by tasks that would make even great men lose their breath and their resolve. Tested in industrialization, collectivization, the real construction of genuine socialism, international policy and threats of war; tested in building the Party we were tearing apart; tested in the struggle against all deviations, from the Trotskyists to us, the Rightists and so forth... That is what those men were tested by\! When we gray-haired men began to learn and teach socialism, we knew MARX's great doctrine and knew we must work along the path he indicated. But few of us hoped to live to see even part of the ideal Marx had depicted to humanity realized; certainly it was not the hope of living to see a socialist state achieved that guided us when we joined the first workers' circles. We were only preparing the future; this was theoretical preparation. Then LENIN came, and that theoretical preparation took on life, led the proletariat to seize power and create the starting points of socialism. Then, when time (the defendant weeps) took LENIN away, STALIN succeeded him, carrying on the work theoretically indicated by MARX and practically begun by LENIN. This led, for the first time in history, to the laying of socialism's foundations; for us who began the struggle for socialism, the issue lies not in isolated facts or individual figures in Comrade KUIBYSHEV's report, but in the overall results. The point is that those figures show that the foundations of a classless socialist society have truly been built according to the blueprint long ago given by Marx and the working plan given by LENIN—built by STALIN, the very man we wanted to fight and whom our supporters, almost until yesterday, wished either to replace or to supplement with ZINOVIEV and me.

Thus this mutual testing over ten years—a span that the history of world revolutions of every kind, pre-bourgeois, bourgeois and the first attempts at proletarian revolution, never granted to anyone, any faction, any group or tendency of the working-class movement—has taken place; history carried it out. It is immaterial that in particular years there were hesitations or that some people left and returned. LENIN brilliantly recounted, as in everything he wrote, the history of factions and groups within parties and of the parties themselves: how they arise from cells, then separate and reunite; how a genuine core subsequently crystallizes in struggle against other groups that begin to reflect tendencies outside the Party; how that core crystallizes and wins victory, how he won over the Social Democratic Party and brought the Bolshevik Party out of it; how STALIN defended the Leninist core of the Party against our vacillations and attacks.

All this took place in those ten years; we all had a chance to show ourselves. But nobody—and if anyone had doubts, these two days supplied the answer—can produce anything that would justify the ten-year existence of this faction, which afterward inevitably degenerated into a counterrevolutionary group. Nobody could say anything of the kind.

And so, on one side stands the work of the Party of LENIN and STALIN; on the other stands our fate, our complete separation from all the living forces of socialism and our transformation from degenerate former revolutionaries \<into\> genuine counterrevolutionaries, and KIROV's corpse. That is what ten years have brought us.

There is also a simple method long familiar to lawyers, one that MARX and LENIN repeatedly applied and taught us to apply in studying the results of social phenomena. It consists of asking: “\<Cui prodest?\>”—“Who benefits?” Ten years is no accident. There is a result. Who benefited from our work?

Whatever might have absorbed us, whatever we devised, whatever programs we wrote or names we gave ourselves, ten years answered that question too, clearly and unequivocally. They answered it in a world situation in which every event and movement in the only proletarian country is weighed in the balance of world public opinion, and cannot fail to be, because the question confronting them here is one of death and the guillotine.

For whose benefit, then, did we expend ten years of sweat, intellect, diplomatic ability and energy? For whose benefit did we employ everything we had learned over the preceding decades? Who benefited? There is only one answer. Of course there is no one in this courtroom, in the entire country or in the world proletariat—apart, perhaps, from certain groups that tomorrow will have to acknowledge their counterrevolutionary essence on the basis of what they hear here—no one who would not say: “Yes, you worked very well for the benefit of the bourgeoisie.”

It is fortunate that the force against which we worked was so great, farsighted and powerful that we were unable to bring the bourgeoisie all the benefits it could historically and politically have counted on from our work under ZINOVIEV's leadership and mine.

But how much strength did our so-called ten years of “activity” drain from the proletarian Party itself, from our proletarian state? How much trouble both the Party and Soviet power had with us\! How many meetings, how much tremendous energy, how much intellect, nerves and willpower\! How much of it all, measured in kilowatts of human and Party energy!

Perhaps another giant enterprise could have been created with it\! Perhaps, if the bourgeoisie had not been able to credit our activity to its own account, and perhaps if the Party and the country had not needed to expend energy refuting our views, then fighting us in workers' circles, then fighting us underground, then imprisoning and exiling you—today me, tomorrow ZINOVIEV, the day after GERTIK\<A\> and others—if all this had not happened, perhaps another step forward might have been taken in socialist construction. And undoubtedly it would have been.

Yes, not only the historical outcome of ten years but also the question of whose benefit we worked for is perfectly clear.

The past has the unpleasant quality of being unchangeable. Not one stroke of our past can be altered. It is beyond human strength\! Forgive me, but it is beyond even the powers of the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court to change anything in the past, especially in the past of this counterrevolutionary group. All that happened happened; it has already been entered into history and will be entered into the Supreme Court's sentence and the judgment of the history of proletarian revolution\!

I should only like permission, somewhere in that judgment, in small print, if only in consideration of my earlier work, to add that as I look through these ten years of my life, I read my own life with revulsion. Let those who seek to learn from the proletarian revolution also read it thus, with revulsion. Those ten years of my life deserve nothing more.

The past cannot be changed; I do not know the future. Today, at this instant, nobody knows that future. We shall find out after a time. But whatever the future holds, anyone interested in the attempt to create an opposition to the mighty proletarian revolution, the first revolution, should know that its leaders, along with everyone who traveled a substantial part of that counterrevolutionary road with them, look back in revulsion, read those ten years of their lives in revulsion and cannot themselves conceive a punishment that could correct or wipe away their guilt.

But punishment is not a question of law either, not a legal question: it is a historical one. My name and ZINOVIEV's cannot be expunged from the past. In the past those names enjoyed authority and were entrusted with high posts, making the depth of their fall all the greater. We cannot erase our names from history. Those who recount the first steps of the Bolshevik Party will have to mention us; and each time, on every page of past history where it is mentioned that ZINOVIEV was chairman of the Comintern or that KAMENEV held some position, every conscientious historian must add: and afterward, although they had learned from such a great teacher, from whom so much could be learned, these men became the actual leaders of a bandit counterrevolutionary group and ended their course ingloriously. (The defendant weeps.)

Two entirely personal words. I have acknowledged the falsehood of my whole life over ten years, of all my politics and my shameful role; but, comrades, there are two or three oral and written statements, two or three letters and conversations addressed by me to two people who politically and morally stand, and will always stand, at the very highest level in the estimation of the world proletariat. As I leave, I should like to say something about them so that those people in particular may be certain that in those letters and conversations I was truthful and sincere with them. Sincerity is not a political word; one cannot speak about one's own sincerity after abusing that word for ten years.

But in those documents I truthfully expressed my genuine convictions concerning the new epoch, the great work being accomplished by STALIN, his immense personal role and the counterrevolutionary meaning of every attack upon him. In practice he has demonstrated the most brilliant intellect and the unbreakable energy of a leader and builder of socialism. In my speech at the Seventeenth Congress and in the letters I speak of, I told the truth: what I really thought and still think now, at this very moment, about our socialist construction and Comrade STALIN.

And so, whatever your decision, the decision of history speaking through your mouths, I shall depart with the conviction that what is recorded in those documents expresses my genuine beliefs even now.

Thirty-two years in the Party, almost thirty-five years of revolutionary work (I was not in other parties, but during my first two years of revolutionary work I was not yet sufficiently developed politically and had not settled on a Party affiliation).

Three quarters of that conscious political life was spent building the Party, and the last quarter, literally the last quarter (I am in my fifty-sixth year and literally have only a handful of years left to live, even if I did not have to sit in the dock), was spent destroying what had been built during the first three quarters. What drove me to this? Difficulties? No.

When we worked underground, were there no difficulties, no hard and tragic moments? Was it easy to sit in prison, be beaten almost to death three times, be stood against a wall to be shot during Kerensky's rule? But then there was never any doubt about the leadership. It never even crossed our minds. No, I repeat, it was not difficulties: then I was a genuine Bolshevik Communist, and afterward I ceased to be one. The physically best period of my life was devoted to the Party, while the final quarter of my life was wasted in a struggle against it. That is the tragedy. It is deepened by our utter lack of ideas and principles. If only we had had some political program, some principled line, it would still have been easier. After all, a person may lose his mind, make a mistake, believe in some different truth, become disillusioned with Soviet power, devise a new theory and begin fighting for it, implementing it in practice. That would at least be a political identity, albeit counterrevolutionary, yet still an ideological, political identity. We would have come here as people who believed in something and were inwardly honest with themselves. But what do we see here? You, comrades judges, have listened to us for two days. What were we fighting for? EVDOKIMOV says for the platform; ZINOVIEV says for part of the platform; GESSEN and others say we did not fight against implementation of the general line, only against the methods of implementing it. KHARITONOV confirmed this too. What we principally fought for, seemingly beyond dispute, was to bring ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the Party leadership. But one of the men responsible, who fought alongside us and directed that struggle throughout seven years, KAMENEV, says: “This is the first I have heard of it. One would have to be an idiot or a madman to spend seven years fighting to bring ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the Party leadership.”

Today I agree with him: we were idiots and madmen. Why did KAMENEV not say that four or five years ago? Things would have been very different.

I cannot and do not wish to blame anyone or shift my guilt onto others. I was sufficiently adult and politically mature when I joined the Opposition, fought the Party and engaged in counterrevolutionary struggle. Nevertheless, I cannot refrain from saying that if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV had truly and honestly disarmed after the Fifteenth Congress—not to mention if they had accepted the compromise offered in the Politburo before the Fourteenth Congress, when they were asked to “surrender” SAFAROV and SARKIS, meaning merely to send them to another city—how many human lives would have been saved, how many hundreds of good workers we would have preserved in and for the Party!

A political leader, a title ZINOVIEV never relinquished, should have foreseen this. But even if after the Sixteenth Congress, after Comrade STALIN's famous, classic report which comprehensively summed up the past and outlined the future, ZINOVIEV had told us: “It's over, enough, we are disarming completely, without reserve,” it would not yet have been too late. Then Sergei Mironovich KIROV would not today be lying in Red Square, and we would not be sitting in this shameful dock. All that is true.

Yet there is something else I cannot conceal from you or myself. Although ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV continued deceiving the Party, there were nevertheless some people among us—few, it is true—who found the strength to leave us, disarm and work honestly in the Party. I did not find that strength in all seven years, nor after my arrest and exile in 1933–34, when the Party gave me a serious warning.

I do not want to detain your attention. I only wish to say that whatever your sentence, I recognize in advance that it is deserved. And whichever door I leave through, for the first time in these last seven years I shall depart relieved, aware that I have finally cast off all lies and filth, and for the first time in seven years I shall be able to look the Party straight in the eye, even as a convicted criminal, a grave criminal, but not as a deceiver, a thief or a hypocrite.

For the first time today in ten years I can finally exclaim honestly, without ulterior motive, lies or double-dealing: Long live the Communist Party\! Long live the Party leadership and its leader, Comrade STALIN\!

I also make this statement because even while at liberty, before my arrest, I was already reconsidering my views and moving away from the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization, yet I did not leave it. I reduced my activity and ceased to be active, but remained a Zinovievite and a member of the central Moscow bureau until the very last moment. This awareness of a complete break with my counterrevolutionary past and my thorough repentance gives me the right to ask the proletarian court for an opportunity to atone for my guilt before the Party and working class wherever the court considers it possible.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant KUKLIN.

KUKLIN: Comrade judges, I accept in full the charge brought against me and regard it as just. I shall accept whatever punishment is imposed on me for my counterrevolutionary activity.

Comrades, for us people with long Party service and much revolutionary work, it took an entire month in solitary confinement to understand our counterrevolutionary activities. I propose asking the Central Committee through the court to take immediate measures to prevent such a thing as happened to us from happening again, that is, to explain all our offenses and our descent into the counterrevolutionary swamp. And further... forgive me, I cannot speak, as I could not earlier, nor can I now, but I would nevertheless ask the Party, if possible, to give me an opportunity to atone for all this crime, for all the work I have done. I have been in the Party thirty-one years. I was on Senate Square on January 9, took part in the Sormovo uprising, was arrested during the February days together with the committee, was at the Eastern Front... (KUKLIN, overcome by emotion, sits down without finishing.)

CHAIRMAN: Defendant FAIVILOVICH\!

FAIVILOVICH: I want to make an observation concerning ZINOVIEV's speech.

It seems to me that in his final statement he made a great mistake: he again tried to talk his way out of responsibility for the “leaderless” group. I was not among the “leaderless” and bear no responsibility for any of their activities. When that group was organized, I was not in Leningrad. But if we look at the individual members of those who committed the villainous crime and recall our first oppositionist steps against the Party Central Committee and the Party in general, we must say this:

Take TOLMAZOV personally: he was secretary of the Leningrad Provincial Committee. RUMYANTSEV was one of the most prominent members of the Komsomol Central Committee and one of the leaders of the Leningrad organization. KOTOLYNOV worked under your direct leadership, ZINOVIEV, in the Comintern; he belonged to the Russian delegation and worked abroad for the Comintern, which you headed at the time. You do not want to understand the political responsibility and admit it frankly to the very end, however difficult this may be, not only for you. You have more years of Party service than we do, including extensive pre-revolutionary experience. Our age prevented us from having it. You were our leader and guide. We regarded you not only as a pupil of LENIN but also as an ideological leader; we trusted you. Now a heavy responsibility must be accepted before the proletarian court. There is no escaping it, it must be accepted; so why do you seek to get around this side of the question?

Comrade judges\! I must say that admitting my moral responsibility for the murder of Comrade KIROV was very difficult for me. The investigator present here \[22\] can tell you with what difficulty I arrived at that decision. It is very hard, but I must admit it; nothing can be done, it is necessary.

It is especially painful because I worked shoulder to shoulder for a long time with those fascist gangs that committed the act, not only while we fought against the Party but also while we built the organization. I mean the counterrevolutionary organization, not the Party organizations in Leningrad, and especially not the Komsomol organizations. I say this because I recognize my responsibility and do not want to lessen our guilt in any degree.

Now I shall turn to my actual final statement.

I committed a great crime against the Party and working class, for which I must receive deserved punishment. I must say that here, during the investigation and before the court, I said I had no connection with the center; that is true. I do not wish thereby to reduce my responsibility in any degree. I merely wish to draw the political conclusion that this was not sufficient for me to break all ties and refrain from making counterrevolutionary attacks on the Central Committee.

And when I thought about why this really happened, I must tell you it happened chiefly, I believe, because I failed to grasp the essential point after V. I. LENIN's death. Bear in mind that seated here are comrades before whose eyes I grew up and became a political worker. Partly because I trusted them (though this is a minor circumstance, imposing no obligation), but chiefly because I failed to understand who STALIN was and that Comrade STALIN himself is the faithful continuer of LENIN's cause, a pupil of LENIN and the leader of our Party. I slid, in fact, into the counterrevolutionary swamp.

Now I should like to say a couple of words about myself. I cannot say I have pre-revolutionary \*underground\* service; I am only thirty-four. I began my political activity (this may not be especially interesting) before I was eighteen. I am the son of a Leningrad worker who spent thirty to thirty-five years in industry.

I joined the Party as a proletarian at the \<former\> Siemens-Schuckert works beyond the Moscow Gate. I spent a good half of my life, since becoming more or less conscious, working with the Party. Later I became a hypocrite.

I shall tell you frankly that the hardest moments of my life were, first, receiving the extract from the Party Collegium's decision expelling me from the Party as a counterrevolutionary. I bore this with immense difficulty, terribly hard. Second was admitting moral responsibility for Comrade KIROV's murder. That was especially difficult. It does not diminish my guilt, my error, my crime against the Party and working class. And now, standing before you, I ask myself: if Comrade SHAROV can say he worked twenty-two years in the Party, if most comrades can take pride in their past, with what can I deserve leniency before the working class and our Party, the Party of LENIN–STALIN, for the colossal crimes I have committed? Have I the right to ask the proletarian court for an opportunity to atone for my guilt, to wash away the shameful stain I have brought upon myself before the Party through my crimes?

But I believe I am not yet finally lost to the working class and the Party, that I shall find within myself the strength, will and ability to atone for that truly enormous crime against the working class and Party and wash away that shameful stain.

I ask only that the proletarian court grant me such an opportunity—to become an honest worker and earn the trust of the working class and Party.

That is all I wanted to say in my final statement.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant TSARKOV.

TSARKOV: Once again I acknowledge the grave crime I committed against the Party and working class. It is entirely clear to me; I have understood the reasons that brought me into the dock. They consist in this: if you make mistakes, fail to heed the Party's warnings in time and do not draw the appropriate conclusions from the penalties imposed by the Party, whether you wish it or not the logic of events takes you where it took me: into the filthy pit of Zinovievite counterrevolution. To neutralize the poison that had infected my organism when I was still a young Komsomol worker, it took a whole month in prison and an appearance before the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court.

I must say quite sincerely that at first, before the investigative authorities, I did not attach political importance to a number of things, and only as a result of talking to the veteran Bolsheviks with whom I was able to speak during the investigation did I see many things that I had understood differently and should have assessed in another way. I told the investigators everything I knew, concealing absolutely nothing about the work of Zinovievite counterrevolution. I tell proletarian justice with a worker's honesty that I am sincerely glad to declare that Zinovievism has been finally destroyed, that I have torn my own Zinovievism from myself like a growth of flesh from my body.

I want to ask proletarian justice to give me, still a young worker, an opportunity to prove my devotion to the Party and its leader, Comrade STALIN. I have been connected with the Party since I was thirteen. While still an adolescent I was an equal member of the Party, and this is more precious to me than the loss of my wife and children. If the court gives me an opportunity, I shall prove that I can be useful to socialist construction.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant BASHKIROV.

BASHKIROV: What can I say? Of course I now understand the full depth and magnitude of my crimes against the Party and the proletarian state. I have said in full everything I knew about the organization.

When I learned that KIROV's murder had been committed by a member of a counterrevolutionary organization, I immediately stated that we must bear political responsibility for it. I sincerely recognized all this and believe that by this sincere recognition I did everything I could to mitigate my guilt.

At the same time, in addition to this crime, I admitted my earlier crimes: concealing my social background for almost my entire life. I left home very young, when I was fourteen. I spent my whole life in the Party and, to tell the truth, needed nothing else.

I shall regard any decision of the court concerning me as just.

I do not know whether I can ask the court for anything, because sitting here are people with great revolutionary merits in the past, whereas I have almost none. But if there is any possibility of sparing my life, I shall of course do everything I can to earn someday the right to be called an honest citizen of the Soviet country.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant SAKHOV.

SAKHOV: Here, after my statement, the presiding judge and one member of the court asked me about the political significance of everything I told members of the center. They helped me, in the following respect. It seems to me that at this one point in my statement I adopted not a political standpoint but the standpoint of an ordinary person, and therefore wrongly assessed what I told EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN and oth\<ers\>. My communications were, of course, informative in character, regardless of how I spoke or what I said. How did matters stand? Regardless of my personal attitudes and relationships, when I said these things I knew to whom I was saying them; therefore it was information. Did I present it tendentiously? I spoke about materials and information available to me through my work in the prosecutor's office. No tendentious presentation was needed, because they themselves showed only the negative sides. They contained none of the positive aspects of socialist construction; they concerned deficiencies, abuses and crimes. Thus my information was in fact tendentious, regardless of personal relations, moods or anything else. Clearly I thereby continued to engage in counterrevolutionary work. That is an additional point about my role. I did not spread rumors to anyone except three or four persons. Since 1930 nobody could ever, under any circumstances, say that I said anything anywhere, particularly against STALIN. That did not happen and could not have happened. I did listen, however. That was my guilt. I listened to KUKLIN's discontent, but regarded it as personal dissatisfaction, grumbling and an inability to turn to anything else.

He is no longer young, being over fifty. I was more closely connected with him than with the others. I visited him and he visited me. But I confirm that during the last two years we held absolutely no political conversations. Not once in those two years did I meet a single oppositionist while visiting him. When I once asked whether he met with ZINOVIEV—there was such a question—I was told no, he did not. That is all. I am trying to speak honestly here. I cannot fail to say one more word about TARASOV. We met and became more or less close in Samara in 1927. He visited my home. There was an occasion in 1932 or 1933 when he spent several nights at my home. He was apparently on practical training at Trekhgorka. When we talked, I categorically assert that there was nothing anti-Party.

What can I add? I have already given my assessment of what occurred, my opinion of what happened here at the trial, and I think I did so clearly enough. At the same time I want to repeat just one point. On the twenty-third, before the trial and before my arrest, I gave an assessment of the Zinovievite organization's participation and role in the whole affair. I gave the same assessment, I insist, as I give here before the court, but for a certain period I \*did not\* have the strength to speak about myself. Here, too, I lied for two days; I also lied to VYSHINSKY. In a conversation with him about the Zinovievite organization, my tongue would not turn to speak about myself, for a simple reason.

When I entered the struggle after the Fourteenth Party Congress, it seemed to me that I was fighting for the Party and for the working class. It turned out otherwise. It turned out that I arrived at counterrevolution; that is the point\! There is no need whatsoever to say anything in self-defense, and we shall not speak of justification.

All those now sitting in the dock are literally naked before the Party, Soviet power and the court; not merely naked but examined more thoroughly than by X-rays. Everyone can see. And only now, when everyone stands naked, is the full depth of the downfall apparent.

There is another interesting question. Once we spoke of degeneration. Enough years have passed, and now perhaps a deserved answer can be given to that question we once raised, at least with regard to many.

I want to say that this trial, in my opinion, is politically important also because it has stripped the whole Opposition bare, precisely because it destroys the Opposition and helps the Party; without it, everything would have continued as before.

For my part I can say only one thing—I shall speak only of my responsibility as I understand it—that I must bear the strictest responsibility. I am finishing now.

Here in the dock, I and all those who take an honest approach to what they say must understand that we now stand, in effect, naked and on our knees before the Party, before Comrade STALIN, saying: “You were right and gave us many warnings; you told us where this road would lead; you treated us gently, hoping that many could be corrected and brought back, but people, rather than responding, for one reason or another—some perhaps because they were conscious enemies, others for various other reasons—went the other way.”

That is all.

CHAIRMAN: THE COURT RETIRES TO DELIBERATE.

At 11:20 p.m. on January 16, the sentence is read aloud, after which the court session is declared closed.
