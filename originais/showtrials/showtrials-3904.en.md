INTERROGATION RECORD

Of SAFAROV, Georgy Ivanovich,

December 27, 1934.

To the questions put to me by the investigation, I additionally testify:

SAKHOV, who worked in the Procuracy in Moscow and then somewhere outside Moscow, is a very active Zinovievist who regularly and extremely frequently visited Leningrad, linked Zinovievists in different places, and circulated information from the Moscow Zinovievist center. He very skillfully used his official position, removing from himself any shadow of suspicion of anti-Party subversive work. In Leningrad, to defend suspected and prosecuted Zinovievists, he even penetrated the Regional Control Commission and, as I remember, meeting me on Kominterna Street in 1931, boasted of how skillfully he had acted as advocate for ABRAMSON, who was then being held accountable as head of the Leningrad Soviet's Housing Department for a dirty apartment scheme. “You understand,” he said, “no one knows that I am a ‘former.’ I appeared before the Regional Control Commission as a representative of the central Procuracy.” When I questioned the decency of such conduct, he replied: “How could I not defend ABRAMSON against GRIBOV, who literally rages against all oppositionists? At the Regional Control Commission, I helped more than just ABRAMSON.” At the same time he related that he traveled to Leningrad almost every day off, saw “all the fellows,” and “shared news with them, receiving fresh information from them in turn.” Later, in 1932 and 1933, I heard from Zinovievists that SAKHOV regularly continued his “visits” and had even spent his leave in Leningrad despite illness. By all indications, SAKHOV played one of the foremost roles in the organizational anti-Party work of the Zinovievist organization until recently. He boasted that he had “remained what he had always been, and the title of procurator not only did not hinder him but helped him.” He is the type of inveterate double-dealer to the marrow, firmly entrenched and counting on complete impunity while enjoying all the comforts and advantages of official position.

I have known SOSITSKY since the anti-Party struggle of 1926–1927. When I came to Leningrad for anti-Party work, he assembled workers from “Krasny Vyborg,” insisted on a complete break with the Party, was indignant that we had parted with TROTSKY, and demanded active anti-Party and anti-Soviet work, including active assistance in arranging “stoppages” at factories. He constantly associated with Vladimir RUMYANTSEV and Sergey MANDELSHTAM; I believe in 1931 he conveyed through RUMYANTSEV that he was indignant that I had “lost my nerve,” that he had “far more in common” with S. MANDELSHTAM. He was a constant participant in and organizer of parties according to MANDELSHTAM's recipe: “vodka, women, politics.” In the Vyborg district, he was undoubtedly one of the leading organizers of the Zinovievist grouping and always had great influence over V. RUMYANTSEV.

Among the active Zinovievists devoted to ZINOVIEV and invariably ready to follow him must be counted Pyotr SOLOVYOV, who graduated from the Institute of Red Professors and worked in Rostov. During the anti-Party struggle in 1927, he intensively prepared materials for the opposition platform, for “agitators,” and for “links.” In 1931, GORDON told me of his being held accountable by the Rostov Party organization and mentioned that he was “still the same,” that is, a faithful Zinovievist.

He undoubtedly maintained contact with his Moscow center, and ZINOVIEV always said that he “showed great promise.”

I met TARTAKOVSKAYA only after returning from Achinsk to Moscow, in mid-1928. She and her husband, “Zhenya TIMOFEEV” (son of the late KAPELZON, an old Rabocheye delo supporter), were then extremely indignant at my statement to the Central Control Commission and put forward Trotskyist arguments: “There is no internal Party democracy, you are not the only one in exile, even ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV are given no work,” and so forth.

That conversation ended in a personal break after TARTAKOVSKAYA's declaration: “Despite the leaders' betrayal, we will go our own way.”

I have long known L. FAIVILOVICH as a convinced Zinovievist. I know for certain that in 1929–1930 he maintained contact with his “leaders.” The Zinovievists never spoke of him as having withdrawn from them, from which I conclude that he remained in their ranks and connected with them.

MYASNIKOV belongs among the particularly active Zinovievist organizers in Leningrad. In 1925–1927, he distinguished himself by especially emphatic loyalty to all the instructions of G. ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV in every dispute within the Zinovievist organization. Subsequently he was, one might say, the right-hand man of V. LEVIN, without consulting whom he did nothing. KOTOLYNOV, as I remember in 1932, said that RUMYANTSEV had become very close to V. LEVIN and MYASNIKOV and that V. LEVIN was triumphant over this, regarding it as the “restoration of Zinovievist unity in Leningrad.” KOTOLYNOV himself, knowing my unfriendly attitude toward LEVIN, did not speak of his own relations with him. Since the disintegration of the anti-Party “leaderless” group, after my return from Achinsk, LEVIN and his closest assistant MYASNIKOV made every effort to take control of the group of former Komsomol members headed by RUMYANTSEV and organize counterrevolutionary work jointly with it. I suppose the initial impetus for this was that the principal Zinovievist “political,” that is, counterrevolutionary, “information” came from Moscow through V. LEVIN. I recall that when TOLMAZOV and SEREDOKHIN visited me at the Comintern in 1933, one of them, I believe SEREDOKHIN, said that “Volodka LEVIN is Zinoviev's plenipotentiary in Leningrad.” I believe MYASNIKOV's name was not mentioned then as his “henchman.”

Written in my own hand — G. SAFAROV.

INTERROGATED BY:

DEPUTY HEAD OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE USSR NKVD — LYUSHKOV
