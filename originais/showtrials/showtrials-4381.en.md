TESTIMONY

OF EVDOKIMOV, Grigory Yeremeyevich,

dated January 10, 1935.

I consider it necessary to add the following to my previous testimony:

KAMENEV L. B., together with ZINOVIEV, directed the formulation of our attitude toward the situation in the country. On economic questions he had greater authority among the center's members than ZINOVIEV, followed them more attentively, and selected material carefully, at least in the first years following the formation of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist center after the Fifteenth Congress. I remember that while he was still in Kaluga, when I went there, he read me something like a review of the country's economic situation, in which he assessed the management of the national economy as a policy of vacillation and wavering, accusing the Party leadership of lacking a firm line. Like ZINOVIEV, he said both then, in Kaluga, and repeatedly in subsequent years that the Party was often being led blindly on the most important questions that should determine a correct class line; that even the Central Committee, on the most important questions of principle, such as the liquidation of the kulaks as a class (1930–1931), was often presented with a fait accompli, for which Comrade STALIN was blamed.

He regarded SYRTSOV's statement as an indication that the difficulties facing the Party and the country were so great that dissatisfaction with the leadership was embracing ever wider Party circles, already reaching part of the leading Soviet circles and affecting leading officials on whom the Central Committee Politburo and Comrade STALIN had previously been able to rely firmly. He apparently read the stenographic transcript of SYRTSOV's statement; in any event, he conveyed its main contents to us and considered SYRTSOV's description of the situation essentially correct.

On collectivization he held the same view that I described in my earlier testimony and which was the common position of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist center.

On industrialization he spoke of a lack of planning in the sense of accurately accounting for the country's resources, a lack of concentration on the principal projects, dispersal, and, as a consequence, overstretched plans.

On Soviet power's foreign policy he held that, unlike Leninist policy, when the Soviet Government's diplomacy had been closely linked to and determined by the interests of the international working-class movement, under STALIN's leadership diplomacy as such was being given overriding importance, and its interests determined the Comintern leadership's attitude toward the immediate tasks of the international working-class movement (“national narrowness,” see my previous testimony).

He differed from ZINOVIEV in that he always showed less initiative, was less active, and assessed more soberly the possibilities of active intervention by the Zinovievists; Zinovievists, especially those from Leningrad, visited him less often and in smaller numbers. But in general, in Moscow he was connected with the same circle of people as ZINOVIEV. I do not know which Zinovievists he met after his exile. I visited his Moscow apartment three times in 1933 and once in 1934 at the dacha where he lived with ZINOVIEV. The first two times I visited him alone, before ZINOVIEV's return.

There were no conversations on general politics (we talked about his reception by Comrade STALIN \[1\] and what work the Central Committee might give him), but neither were there conversations, or any inclination to converse, about revising our positions.

The third time I visited his Moscow apartment was in the winter of 1933, together with GORSHENIN. We stayed very briefly. GORSHENIN told him about some Gosplan business (I do not remember exactly). KAMENEV was in a great hurry. GORKY had summoned him. “A great pity; I would like to talk, but it will not be possible today. Come another time,” KAMENEV said; we even seem to have agreed on a day. But for some reason this meeting did not take place.

In 1934 I saw KAMENEV, as stated above, only once, at the dacha. On this occasion he struck me as a man reconciled to the role assigned to him and satisfied with the work entrusted to him; reconciled in the sense of those sentiments common to most of the center's members in recent times which I described in my latest testimony.

Written by me myself, EVDOKIMOV.

INTERROGATED BY: RUTKOVSKY

Certified true: A. Svetlova
