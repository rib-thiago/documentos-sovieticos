INTERROGATION RECORD

OF SAFAROV G.I., January 3, 1935.

QUESTION: Tell the investigation what you know about CHARNY.

ANSWER: CHARNY—one of the active Zinovievists of the Vasileostrovsky district. In 1928 and 1929, he maintained connections with other Zinovievists of his district, particularly the deceased SHENBERG \[2\], Moisei YAKOVLEV, and probably met A. GERTIK. At that time, the Zinovievists considered him “one of their own.” In 1930–1931, according to, I think, M. YAKOVLEV, he went over to the Trotskyists and, unless I am mistaken, was arrested with a group of Trotskyists. Later, I remember hearing about him in 1933 in connection with the following: GORDON from Rostov, calling at my apartment, indignantly told me that PEKAR-ORLOV had “informed on” CHARNY, who had come to “share his thoughts on the desirability of creating a society of old workers to combat the dilution of the working class's ranks by people from the countryside.” I also heard this from other Zinovievists, I think A. KUKLIN and Nik\<olai\> DMITRIYEV, in the same tone of sharp condemnation of PEKAR-ORLOV and sympathy for CHARNY. At that same time, the ZINOVIEV–SMILGA thesis about the peasantization of the proletariat and its loss of “class instinct” as a result of drawing new cadres into factories and plants circulated widely in Zinovievist circles. CHARNY, a first-rate chatterbox fond of being original, who undoubtedly remained faithful to the old anti-party and anti-Soviet orientations of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc, is unlikely to have “independently” arrived at the idea of the proletariat's “peasantization.” It seems more likely that, having picked up a notion of degeneration that appealed to him, he began looking for his own “original” practical ways of applying it. It is very likely that before approaching PEKAR-ORLOV, known as a sincere supporter of the party line, he consulted individual Zinovievists, particularly KUKLIN, reputed among them to know the long-established working masses. His conversation with PEKAR-ORLOV was probably an attempt to probe the possibilities of implementing the project of organizing a society of old workers. The Zinovievists' unanimous and rapid reaction to CHARNY's being brought before the Central Control Commission supports this. CHARNY constantly engaged in double-dealing, pretending to be a devoted supporter of the party's general line and using his friendship with ORLOVICH for this purpose, while on the other hand conducting counterrevolutionary work against the party. His closest friends were SHENBERG and SHAKHNOVICH \[2\], but of course he did not meet only them.

Written in my own hand—G. SAFAROV.

INTERROGATED BY:

HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD OF THE USSR—PETROVSKY
