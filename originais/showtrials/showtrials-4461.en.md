Comrade Agranov pointed out to me that the testimony I have given so far does not convey to the investigation an impression of complete and sincere repentance and does not tell everything that happened.

The investigation's deadlines are approaching their very end. The confrontations I have been given also, of course, have their effect on me. I must, I must tell the investigation everything to the end.

It is true that what I said in previous testimony contains more of what I could say in my defense than of what I must say for the full exposure of my guilt. I have indeed forgotten much, but there was much that I did not want to think through to the end, still less tell the investigation to the very end.

Yet I want to disarm completely.

Yet I recognize my enormous guilt before the Party and am filled with genuine repentance. I lacked the strength of spirit and the “physical strength” of mind to think everything through to the end.

At first it seemed that Comrade Kirov's murder and my implication in such a case were simply something like a collapsing cliff that accidentally, for some reason, buried me too beneath its debris. Only gradually did I clarify for myself the true meaning of events.

The issue is not individual details and episodes. The essence of the matter is that after the Fifteenth Congress we survived as a group, strictly speaking existing underground, believing that a number of the most important parts of the platform of 1925-1927 were nevertheless “correct” and that, sooner or later, the Party would acknowledge this “merit” of ours.

To remain in the Party with this conviction, we had to deceive the Party, that is, essentially, to practice double-dealing.

“Double-dealing” is a very hurtful and harsh term. One never wanted to “acknowledge” it.

But it is the correct term. Crudely but correctly, it tears the mask from the fictions and states “what there is.”

And everything else follows from this!

Of course, the development of events since the Fifteenth Congress was not entirely without influence on us either (on me in particular). The great success of the first five-year plan, then the successes of the first years of the second five-year plan, the growth of the economy, the growth of socialist culture, the growth of the Union's military might, the growth of the USSR's world influence, the growth of the Party, the successes of collectivization, and so on and so forth — all this could not fail to shake our own faith in our platform of 1925-1927.

But we “consoled ourselves” that in other parts we had nevertheless “proved right” (lately, regarding the Comintern's work), that the Party regime was supposedly “terrible,” and so on; we “consoled ourselves” that much since 1928 was supposedly being done “our way,” but with “high overhead costs,” and so on.

In accordance with all this, we harbored hostile feelings toward the Party leadership and Comrade Stalin.

We were not altogether blind to the fact that the Party leadership was doing great work. But some “argument” could always be found for nevertheless reaching a conclusion hostile to the Party's general line and its leadership, especially Comrade Stalin.

I personally am generally a man of great inner vacillations. I say this, of course, not as a “justification” or “mitigation,” but because 1) one must say what there is, and 2) this trait of mine had no small influence on the fate of the whole group.

I was not useless to the Party when I unhesitatingly helped bring adopted decisions to the masses. But when, after V. I.'s death, I myself had to make very important decisions in a very complex situation (and once during V. I.'s lifetime too, in October 1917), this trait of my character (vacillations, half-decisions) repeatedly played a very bad role.

Many times after the Fifteenth Congress, and especially after the Sixteenth Congress, I told myself: enough, it has been proved that the Central Committee and Comrade Stalin are right in everything; this must be acknowledged once and for all, and all the conclusions drawn inwardly. But with new turning-point events, new difficulties, and so on, new vacillations began. A striking example is 1932, whose events I described in greater detail in my testimony.

I again became a mouthpiece for anti-Party sentiments. Subjectively, of course, I did not want to harm the Party and the working class. In substance, however, during those years I became the mouthpiece of forces that wanted to halt the socialist offensive, that wanted to wreck socialism in the USSR.

I was sincere in my speech at the Seventeenth Congress and believed that I was “adapting” to the majority only in my manner of expression. In fact, two souls continued to live within me.

There were stronger characters than mine in the central group of former “Zinovievists.” But the whole trouble was that our entire position, since we had failed genuinely to submit to the Party, to merge with it completely, to become imbued toward Stalin with the feelings of full recognition that had imbued the entire Party and country, since we continued to look backward and live our own particular “stifling life” — our entire position condemned us to political ambiguity, from which double-dealing is born.

More than once we told ourselves: if the Party (we said: Stalin) would bring us into “real” work, probably everything would be smoothed over; we would help correct the “mistakes,” improve the regime, and so on, and everything would go well, and we ourselves would overcome our estrangement from the Party.

But the Party sensed that we concealed a stone against it and, naturally, could not restore any serious political and organizational influence to enemies or half-enemies of the Party's line and its leadership.

Looking back, one must say that in fact the Party treated us too considerately. But, of course, it could not really trust us.

And we continued to live with our own particular psychology, according to the “laws” of development of a “closed circle” of the “unrecognized,” the “injured,” those “who see better than anyone but are deprived of the opportunity to show others the way.”

Personal ties, too, increasingly narrowed to the circle of former Leningrad officials removed from Leningrad along with me.

I asserted during the investigation that since 1929 we had had no center of former “Zinovievists” in Moscow. And I often thought myself: what sort of “center” is this? It is simply Zinoviev, plus Kamenev, plus Evdokimov, plus another two or three people; and even they hardly see each other anymore and no longer conduct any systematic anti-Party factional work.

But in fact it was a center.

That was how these few people were regarded by the remnants of the cadres of former “Zinovievists” who had failed or refused genuinely to dissolve into the Party (above all the remnants of the “Leningrad people”).

That was how all the other anti-Party groups and little groups regarded them. Some of us (above all Kamenev and myself) had had major political names in the past. In 1932, when the “revival” of all the anti-Party groups began, talk immediately arose in these circles of a “Leninist Politburo” (that is, the Politburo supposedly existing under Lenin, including myself and Kamenev, and Rykov, Bukharin, and Tomsky). They magnanimously “agreed” that Comrade Stalin should be in it too.

All the anti-Party elements again advanced our “candidacies.” Ryutin's kulak counterrevolutionary platform abused Kamenev and me, placed its hopes on new people, on its own “practical men,” but ultimately did not rule out these “candidacies” either.

My former associates living in Moscow in recent years always voted for the Party's general line and spoke openly in the Party spirit, but “among themselves” criminally continued speaking, though not quite as before (life was knocking many “foundations” of the 1925-1927 platform out from under their feet), with hostility toward the Party's line and leadership.

And by this double-dealing behavior they set an example for others, those in whose eyes this group appeared “authoritative.”

Meetings of members of this central circle of former “Zinovievists” became increasingly rare and, above all, increasingly without purpose.

In 1933-1934 there were almost no such meetings anymore at my home or Kamenev's. But they nevertheless saw each other in various combinations (partly through family connections). I tried, as before, to find out everything: about Comintern affairs through Magyar (when I worked at “Bolshevik,” I also learned much through official sources), and about economic affairs partly through Gorshenin.

With Kamenev we now often spoke of other things: Pushkin, literary criticism, and so on, adding bitterly to ourselves: “This is our form of withdrawal from politics.” But we also told each other “news,” rumors, encounters, exchanged political considerations and observations, generally in an ambiguous spirit: much was supposedly going “splendidly” and well, but much badly, wrongly, with “overhead costs,” and so forth.

And in our relations with the Party leadership there was the same ambiguity, that is, from the standpoint of Party members, essentially hostility.

At the same time we dreamed of being “brought into” work; we clearly understood that we could be brought in only as individual “artisans.”

Of course, if the matter had been limited to conversations among two or three friends, if it had taken place in a vacuum, if waves of bourgeois hatred had not come from abroad, if there had not been certain anti-Party groups and little groups behind us, this would have been only the ambiguity (or double-dealing) of two or several people.

In fact, however, in the real circumstances of our reality, this was a crime against the Party, a deception of the Party.

And whether we wanted it or not, we in fact remained one of the centers of struggle against the Party and the great work it was doing and continues to do.

The investigation demands a direct answer: was there or was there not a center of the former “Zinovievist” group in Moscow? The answer must be: yes, there was, though little formalized, little active in recent years, without a clear platform, but it existed. And its role in practice was, of course, anti-Party, that is, counterrevolutionary.

Its initial composition was myself, Kamenev, Evdokimov, Bakaev, Kuklin, Sharov, Fedorov, and, up to a certain time, Zalutsky and Kharitonov. Then, in 1932, the composition is less definite. Generally, without the last two.

The question of the form of existence, the degree of formalization of this central group of former “Zinovievists” in Moscow in different years, the personal psychology and personal experiences and vacillations of each member of this group, and mine in particular, are of lesser importance.

The investigation poses the question: yes or no?

One has to answer: yes.

I turn to the question of Leningrad.

Comrade Agranov remarked to me that I display particular timidity when I reach this point during interrogations, and that this makes the investigation particularly distrustful of me.

Yes, that is true. I have displayed and still display particular timidity on this question; I only ask that you understand why, and what kind. It is not a matter of the degree of punishment, which I cannot escape in any case, but of something else.

Indeed, I am very afraid — afraid before history — of finding myself in the company of the degenerates and fascist murderers of S. M. Kirov, of finding myself in the position of a man who all but incited terrorism against the leaders of the Party and Soviet power.

That is why, from the first interrogation, I so passionately protested: how could I be confused with the scoundrels who went as far as murdering S. M. Kirov?

But facts are stubborn things.

And, having learned all the facts from the indictment against the “Leningrad center” (published in the newspapers), I had to acknowledge the moral and political responsibility of the former “Leningrad opposition,” and my own personal responsibility, for the crime committed, in the sense set out in my previous testimony.

However, this is only part of the question.

What G. E. Evdokimov said on this subject yesterday during the confrontation with me — I say this frankly — makes the deepest impression on me and more than anything else forces me to think this through to the very end as well.

How could an organization of “Zinovievists” have existed in Leningrad in recent years without my knowing about it?

Yet it is a fact that I did not know this before my arrest and before my interrogations in the DPZ. I am now telling you the pure truth: I did not know and thought that it had not existed in recent years. I fully understand that it is late and pointless for me to evade responsibility. But if I told you now that I knew of the existence of an organization of former “Zinovievists” in Leningrad in 1933-1934, I would be telling a lie.

When I spoke with V. Levin in the summer of 1932, not a single word was said about the organization in general or the Leningrad organization in particular. But, of course, I knew that I was authoritative for him (that is, Levin), that my anti-Party opinions strengthened him in his anti-Party opinions.

I could not fail to understand that V. Levin did not live in Leningrad in a vacuum. I knew that in 1926-1928 V. Levin played the role of one of the major organizers of the anti-Party forces of former “Zinovievists” in Leningrad. I should have understood that he probably also had friends for whom he in turn was authoritative, and that he would probably share with some of them what he had heard from me.

In substance, this meant reviving anti-Party sentiments in Leningrad in 1932. The dividing line between this and reviving the anti-Party organization of former “Zinovievists” in Leningrad is, of course, very narrow.

Of course, in the summer of 1932 I spoke “only” of ideological disagreements; of course, I said that we did not want an organizational struggle (because we considered it hopeless), that we would “only” wait, and so on. But the investigation is right that in practice this encouraged organizational ties as well.

Returning from exile in 1933 (where I had undergone much inwardly and tried to break the old anti-Party sentiments within myself), with criminal thoughtlessness I failed to reveal to the Party all the individuals and all the attempts at anti-Party agreements.

At that time two things had to be done: first, make a series of public speeches not of a diplomatic kind, but genuinely exposing our previous anti-Party line and genuinely expressing solidarity with the Party and its leadership; second, reveal to the Party all the former anti-Party ties, with all the specific names and details. I had enough strength for the first. Not for the second. And that was my chief misfortune and my chief guilt.

Recalling the psychology of that time now, I see that the mood was somehow egocentric: after all, I had almost completely broken myself, inwardly as well; after all, I was now speaking and writing so as to leave no doubts that the entire past was over and I was submitting to the Party seriously and finally; Kamenev was doing the same, so — I told myself — everything would be over for those who listened to us too. More than once Kamenev and I said then: well, now this is the last chance to remain in the Party, to overcome the period of apostasy and estrangement; let us now behave at least in such a way that if, say, in a year the OGPU were asked about us and our behavior, the OGPU would reply: we can say nothing bad about them.

At first even Evdokimov looked askance at this mood of mine (and Kamenev's). And I remember perfectly the feeling that it was even physically difficult for me to speak with him on this subject after returning from Kustanai, because I read in his eyes: what is this, are you serious?

I genuinely made no organizational attempts to revive the old. But I “only” gradually began, nevertheless, to collect all the “news” again. Then I “only” began again to give that same Evdokimov negative characterizations of much that the Party leadership was doing. Then Kamenev and I again began exchanging in detail everything we had heard, with the appropriate comments, and so on.

I truly knew nothing about Leningrad in 1933-1934 except something incidental and fragmentary. But, of course, I should have remembered that people remained in Leningrad who had followed us, who had not abandoned anti-Party positions, who probably met each other, and for whom the last thing they had heard about me was the sentiments of 1932.

In those years, 1933-1934, I thought least of all about the former “leaderless” people (the youth). There had been no intimate connection with them. But I knew and could not fail to know that, even excluding them, there were people in Leningrad formerly connected with us who retained anti-Party sentiments, and that they probably met each other.

I really did not hear about their visits to Moscow in 1933-1934, nor about trips to Leningrad for anti-Party affairs in those years.

When Evdokimov told me that he had been in Leningrad (I do not remember whether in 1933 or 1934), he certainly did not speak about meetings with Levin or anyone of that sort, but related whom he had seen at Smolny, how the official leaders had spoken to him, how the late S. M. Kirov had asked him: “What do you think, can Zinoviev be trusted?” and so forth.

But, I repeat, had I wished to think at all seriously about the past, I could not fail to understand that a certain number of former “Zinovievists” with anti-Party sentiments remained in Leningrad.

This could only have been ended by handing over to the Party all the anti-Party-minded individuals with whom we had previously been connected.

For this we lacked Party spirit and a sense of responsibility. We rejoiced (Kamenev and I) that this was not directly demanded of us (that is, the Party and state authorities did not demand it), and counted on its “somehow” “dissolving” by itself.

In reality, however, something quite different was happening in Leningrad at that time. From the indictment in the case of Kirov's murderers I learned that it was precisely in 1933-1934 that these people's work became particularly active. The results are known.

Thus, in recent years I genuinely did not know of the existence of an organization of former “Zinovievists” in Leningrad. But I knew and could not fail to know that anti-Party-minded former “Zinovievists” remained in Leningrad, and that they probably met.

I knew and kept silent. I knew and concealed it from the Party.

And objectively this has greater significance than the fact that I did not know about the organization of recent years. And I must acknowledge before the investigation that the crux of the question lies in the latter.

In 1933-1934 the situation was, of course, not that today I gave a speech or wrote an article or statement fully recognizing the Party's general line and leadership, and tomorrow whispered in someone's ear that in fact I opposed all this.

But the previous years of deception and double-dealing had produced the opinion: well, all this must be forced; in fact they probably think something else.

And in time isolated assessments, remarks, and words reached the former “Zinovievists,” from which they could conclude that the hidden stone really remained.

And the conclusions followed of themselves.

I am filled with repentance — the most ardent repentance. To end my days accused of one or another involvement in terror against the Party's leaders, in such a vile fascist murder as Kirov's murder, is tragic enough. And of course I had never dreamed of anything like it.

I am ready to do everything, everything, everything to help the investigation uncover all that took place in my anti-Party struggle and that of my former associates, as well as of those with whom I happened to come into contact in the anti-Party (essentially counterrevolutionary) struggle against the Party.

I am naming all those whom I remember and recall as former participants in the anti-Party struggle. And I shall do so to the end, remembering that this is my duty.

A separate and, of course, the most painful chapter is my attitude toward the Party's leaders, and especially Comrade Stalin.

This is neither the time nor the place for me to evade the matter with banalities.

Yes, what in 1925-1927 could be considered “negative personal characterizations in political struggle” turns into oceans of slander in the light of events as they subsequently developed and as matters stand in 1935.

The younger members of the Politburo (relatively younger, but in fact among the oldest workers of the Bolshevik Party), whom we tried to treat so contemptuously, fulfilled a tremendous historical task in an era when I and others who considered ourselves “irreplaceable” proved to be mouthpieces of anti-proletarian tendencies and worse. That is a fact.

As for Stalin, there is nothing to say.

Many of the slanderous statements against him, many of the repulsive declarations and characterizations, are attributed to me personally without cause. They did not exist at all or were the fruit of “collective creativity.” But what there was is enough, more than enough.

I can now say only one thing. If I had the opportunity to repent before the entire people, it would be a great relief to me, and I would say: here is yet another example of how great men, great fighters of the world proletariat, must pass through a period of slander and insults, even if only from an embittered handful, yet one capable of putting many logs in the path of this great leader of the proletarians.

I do not want to say words here that would seem flattering. Nobody needs this, and Stalin himself least of all. If I am not destined to see and hear any more of how he, Lenin's true and worthy successor, continues to lead the USSR, leads the columns of the world proletariat from victory to victory, I ardently wish him happiness and success on that path.

Of myself personally I shall permit myself to observe only this: despite what has happened to me in recent years, throughout my conscious life I have been, and until my last breath shall remain, wholeheartedly devoted to the world proletariat.

Let others learn from my painful example; let them see what it means to stray from the Party path and where this can lead.

If I ever again have any opportunity to work, I shall give everything to make at least some small amends for my great guilt.

G. Zinoviev

January 13, 1935.
