INTERROGATION RECORD

Conducted on December 30, 1934, by the head of Section IV of the SPO of the GUGB, KORKIN.

SAKHOV, Boris Naumovich, born in 1900 (in his documents, born in 1897), in the small town of Kakhovka; his father was an employee of the Anatra brothers' steamship company. Jewish, Soviet citizen. Resides in Arkhangelsk, procurator of the Northern Territory. Wife: SAKHOVA Sariya Samoylovna, works as a physician. Two sons, aged 10 and 11. Without property. Secondary education. Member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) since 1919, never expelled from the Party; received a severe reprimand with a warning for factional activity. In 1919, around August, was arrested in Ekaterinoslav by Shkuro's counterintelligence service. Held by counterintelligence for 5–6 days and in prison for about three weeks; subsequently escaped from custody while being transferred from prison. Moved to Aleksandrovsk, established contact with the Party organization there, was admitted to the Party, and soon afterward, still in 1919, remained chairman of the underground Party committee until the arrival of the Red Army, that is, approximately until early 1920.

QUESTION: Describe your connections with former participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc.

ANSWER: After the Fifteenth Congress, in January 1928, at the summons of the Samara Party organization, from which I had departed without authorization, I returned from Leningrad to Samara. In Samara, I maintained close comradely relations with A. S. KUKLIN until approximately August 1928, when he left for Moscow. Around the time of KUKLIN's departure, EVDOKIMOV arrived in Samara; I also had a close personal relationship with him. I was transferred from Samara to Moscow in July 1929, and, since I had no apartment, I lived with KUKLIN until November 1929.

In 1929–1930 and possibly in early 1931, I visited GERTIK's apartment (more frequently in 1929, less often in subsequent years), where I met KHARITONOV, GORSHENIN, KOZHURO \[1\], BAKAEV, GESSEN, BRAVO, NAUMOV, and BRAUDE. EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, FEDOROV, and ZALUTSKY went there less often. Around 1931, I stopped visiting GERTIK's apartment, since people unknown to me came there and, on some occasions, conversations were allowed that amounted, in essence, to entirely inadmissible criticism of the actions of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) on particular issues. I recall that LEPESHINSKAYA told NAUMOV and me that someone from among the Trotskyists visited GERTIK's apartment. In addition, people arriving in Moscow were often there; of these, I remember M. NATANSON, ANISHEV, and ZILBERMAN.

I visited GORSHENIN about four times during my entire stay in Moscow, and EVDOKIMOV considerably more frequently. At their apartments I met GESSEN, BAKAEV, and, rarely, KUKLIN; at EVDOKIMOV's, almost always BRAVO, less often SHAROV, and once GERTIK. NAUMOV and KUKLIN frequently visited my apartment; EVDOKIMOV came on isolated occasions, ZALUTSKY twice, and BAKAEV once; possibly others also called. Visits by all these persons, apart from KUKLIN and NAUMOV, took place chiefly before 1931.

Around 1931–1932, I. I. TARASOV, who was then doing practical training at Trekhgorka, stayed overnight at my apartment for two or three nights.

I believe it was in 1930 that, having telephoned beforehand, I went to ZINOVIEV's apartment (Kaloshin Lane \[2\]); when leaving, I saw KAMENEV.

On one of my official trips to Rostov in 1931, I met Comrade DMITRIEV there. At my request, KUKLIN had given me his address on the eve of the trip.

Whenever I came to Leningrad, I visited SHEINBERG (he died in the autumn of 1934), my wife's brother.

QUESTION: Describe the nature of the conversations you had at these meetings.

ANSWER: Political conversations, especially in the presence of several people, took place only at GERTIK's apartment, which, as I testified, I visited during 1929–1930.

The dissatisfaction of everyone who gathered at GERTIK's, including myself, was connected at that time chiefly with the failure to bring the former opposition leaders, ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and others, into leading work in the Party.

In the conversations, dissatisfaction was expressed with the state of the Comintern's work; people said that “the Comintern's policy is not active enough, there is no one to lead the Comintern, a number of Communist parties are in disarray, nothing works without ZINOVIEV.”

In connection with both these issues, Comrade STALIN was slandered, the exclusion of ZINOVIEV and others from leading work being attributed to his supposedly personal motives.

There was talk of the difficult food situation in the country, which was explained as a consequence of the country's economic mismanagement.

Comrade STALIN's letter “Dizzy with Success” was regarded as the result of an inability to ensure the full implementation of a correct policy — as a retreat.

In conversations about the struggle against the kulak, it was considered that the Party's Central Committee was then carrying out what the opposition had proposed.

Approximately the same sentiments were expressed in meetings with EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and others in 1931–1932 as well; in subsequent years, however, I do not remember political conversations deserving the investigation's attention. At meetings during these years, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, and others displayed noticeable caution and reserve.

In 1929 and 1930, I remember that a number of conversations discussed the line of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) toward the Right. Some Zinovievists regarded the Central Committee's line as a continuation of the destruction of the Party's leading cadres: “today ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV are being removed, and tomorrow BUKHARIN”; others, including myself, considered all the Central Committee's actions on this question correct.

QUESTION: Do you acknowledge these conversations to be anti-Party and anti-Soviet?

ANSWER: Yes, I acknowledge these conversations to be unquestionably anti-Party and anti-Soviet. The thrust of all these conversations was directed, in essence, against the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), and especially against Comrade STALIN.

QUESTION: Do you know of the existence of the Zinovievist organization, and what exactly do you know?

ANSWER: The preservation of contacts among former participants in the Zinovievist organization, the preservation of group cliquishness and mutual shielding, and the continuation of anti-Party and anti-Soviet conversations in fact meant and mean the preservation of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization after the Fifteenth Party Congress as well, and its transition to an underground existence.

The former leaders — ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, FEDOROV, ZALUTSKY, KUKLIN, KHARITONOV, BAKAEV, and others — essentially preserved their old opposition center and undoubtedly gathered as members of that center and discussed what was happening in the Party, the Comintern, and the country from anti-Soviet positions.

QUESTION: The investigation knows of your membership in the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization. What can you testify about this?

ANSWER: Yes, I belonged to the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization and, in the first years, approximately until ZINOVIEV's exile, that is, until the end of 1932, I took part in its activities. After ZINOVIEV established a connection with RYUTIN, I politically withdrew from the counterrevolutionary organization, but I did not have the strength to break off my contacts with EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and other active members of the organization.

B. SAKHOV.

Interrogated by: head of Section VI of the SPO of the GUGB, KORKIN.
