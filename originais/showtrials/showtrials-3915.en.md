December 28, 1934.

DPZ

TO THE CENTRAL COMMITTEE OF THE ALL-UNION COMMUNIST PARTY (BOLSHEVIKS)

In the DPZ, I learned from the investigator who had committed the murder of S. M. KIROV. Horror and numbness seized me. I remain gripped by horror and numbness to this very moment. And the more details I learn from the indictment about how and in what circumstances this villainous crime was prepared and committed, the greater the horror that seizes me.

The anti-Party group associated with my name, as everyone knows, never contemplated terror even in its worst days. And nevertheless the Party is right when it says that political responsibility for the crime committed rests on the former “Zinovievist” group, on me in particular. I curse, hate, and despise the fascist murderers who raised their hand against a great proletarian fighter, just as the entire working class curses, hates, and despises them. At the same time, I see that moral and political responsibility for the crime committed rests on us, on me in particular. The overwhelming majority of those who, according to the indictment, belonged to the “Leningrad center” came from the group of “Leftists” (or “leaderless”) who broke with the “Zinovievist” group before the Fifteenth Congress, which left its mark on all subsequent relations with them. For years I did not see these people, heard nothing about them, and could never have suspected that they were degenerating into a group of fascist murderers. And nevertheless moral and political responsibility for what happened rests on us, on me in particular.

In the indictment, I read that the murderers were connected with one of the consuls, gave him information, took money from him, spoke with him about contact with Trotsky, and were preparing to flee abroad and serve the interventionists there. Some of them were probably directly guided by the hand of an experienced agent of the Russian or foreign White Guards. Possibly some of them sold themselves for money. But it is a fact that the scoundrels who reached this point were once connected with the “Leningrad opposition” and with me personally. It is a fact that even now they invoke the platform of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc. From this it follows that political responsibility for what happened falls on the former “Zinovievist” anti-Party group and, consequently, above all on me.

Yesterday in prison I had to sign a document stating that I had read the resolution expelling me from the Party “as a counterrevolutionary.” This was the hardest thing I remember in my life. After this I feel as though I were already dead. I know that my position is not such that any statement or letter of mine could change anything in my fate. It is useless for me to resort to tricks, and useless to deny things as well.

My guilt is immeasurable. It consists in the fact that, not having fully understood the anti-Party character of my bankrupt “platform,” even after the Party had defeated it, I continued to get in the way, flirted with other anti-Party little groups, failed to break sharply with the entire past, failed to tell those close to me clearly, without reservations and honestly, that we had gone bankrupt, failed fully and genuinely to acknowledge inwardly STALIN's rightness and greatness, failed to draw all the conclusions from all this, failed to go away somewhere for several years to do practical work; in a word, failed honestly and completely to draw the conclusions from my political bankruptcy. The Central Committee gave me the opportunity to remain in the ranks of the Bolsheviks. My heart bleeds at the thought that I failed to take advantage of this opportunity.

My position now is terrible in the full sense of the word. All the time I think: at least now, at the very last moment, do what you must do to the end!

What exactly must I do in this situation, so tragic for me?

1\. Tell everything I know.

2\. Take upon myself all responsibility for what happened arising from the situation.

3\. Acknowledge in advance that everything the Party now says about me and now does to me will be correct.

I implore you to believe me in the following:

1\. I did not know, knew and heard absolutely nothing, and could not have heard of the existence in recent years of any anti-Party organization or group in Leningrad. For a number of years I had no contacts with Leningrad, did not seek them, and could not seek them.

2\. In the investigation I testify and will testify to everything I know and remember, concealing nothing, sparing neither myself nor others.

3\. A number of statements, at various times, attributed to me by other arrested persons during the investigation are either errors of memory, especially concerning dates, or attempts to get back at me as the principal political accused.

In my testimony, I set out in detail what I remembered about the long period of anti-Party struggle and anti-Party sentiments. My greatest vacillations in the recent period related to 1932. To that same time, and to the preceding period, belong attempts at “contact” and, in essence, collusion among several anti-Party groups.

We, myself in particular, were sincerely opposed to the policy of the Right, and yet we had a certain “contact” with BUKHARIN, TOMSKY, and RYKOV. How could this be? The logic of the situation operated: the discontented, even for different reasons, seek one another out. At first, when BUKHARIN, RYKOV, and TOMSKY were still members of the Politburo, we were also motivated by the desire to keep abreast of affairs and obtain political information through them.

Concerning the mutual soundings of “various anti-Party little groups” in 1932, I must say the following. Politically, these were, of course, attempts at collusion against the Central Committee's line. It looked “innocent” at first. But if the Central Committee had not constantly warned the Party that the tendency toward a union of the Right and the “Left” was inherent in the very nature of things, if the Central Committee had not constantly struck at both, some form of collusion among anti-Party-minded groups in 1932 would have taken place one way or another. The Central Committee's correct policy was breaking up, and did break up in 1932 as well, this anti-Party collusion in its infancy.

The investigation points out to me that my references to the fact that supposedly no Party representative had previously asked me to name names and relate the practical details of the anti-Party struggle suggest that I wish to try to shift responsibility from myself to someone else. I understand that this may indeed suggest such a thought. To clarify this passage in my testimony, I shall say the following:

It is impossible for me to shift responsibility to anyone else. I understand this well. I pointed to this circumstance only so that the investigation might better understand my psychology at the time. I then considered naming names too “petty” for me, that only political solidarity was required of me. And I interpreted the fact that no one demanded names from me as meaning that others viewed matters the same way.

Of course, if I had known that an anti-Party organization continued to exist in Leningrad, and still more if I had known anything about the existence of a group with terrorist sentiments, I would have been obliged immediately to report everything I knew. But I knew nothing of the kind and could not have known. For Leningrad, the matter could only concern very old things that I considered long dead, and people whose role in the anti-Party struggle up to 1929 had long been more or less known. As for the whisperings in Moscow with BUKHARIN, TOMSKY, RYKOV, SHATSKIN, STEN, LOMINADZE, and SAFAROV, I am indeed guilty of not having spoken about them openly to the Party in 1932, or at least later. Here it was precisely the psychology I speak of above that operated. I fully acknowledge this guilt of mine.

In 1933–1934, I was sincerely filled with the desire to work honestly under the leadership of the Central Committee and Comrade STALIN. I was in complete agreement with the Party's domestic and foreign policy. Trying to follow the Comintern's work, I fully agreed with its united-front tactics. Their practical implementation in France caused me doubts: careless statements about a “united popular front,” about “organic unity,” and so on.

But my greatest misfortune was that, having suffered political bankruptcy in 1925–1927 and lost the Party's political trust, I nevertheless persistently gravitated toward questions of high politics, particularly Comintern policy, instead of taking up some modest practical work in the cultural and educational sphere or something of that kind. I tried to turn to other work; I took up literary criticism, began working on Shchedrin, Pushkin, and so forth. But I could not immerse myself in it completely.

My lesson must serve as a stern warning to all who are in a more or less analogous position. Honesty with the Party to the end, without evasions, without whisperings, or leave altogether and stop getting in the way! It is no accident that precisely now, when the country has finally embarked on the road of great growth and great construction, the renegades have finally reached their dead end, the abyss.

If, on the strength of past years of honest work, I can still ask the Central Committee for anything, I ask and implore: open to me at least a small hope of forgiveness from the Party, give me at least a small opportunity to work wherever it may be. Until my last breath, I shall think only of this and hope for it.

G. ZINOVIEV

Handed over by me on December 30, 1934.
