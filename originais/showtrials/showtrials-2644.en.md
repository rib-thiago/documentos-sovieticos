## INDICTMENT \[2\]

IN THE CASE AGAINST ZINOVIEV G. E., EVDOKIMOV G. E., HERTIK A. M., BAKAEV I. P., KUKLIN A. S., KAMENEV L. B., SHAROV Y. V., FEDOROV G. F., GORSHENIN I. S., PERIMOV A. V., TARASOV I. I., FAIVILOVICH L. Y., HERTSBERG A. V., GESSEN S. M., SAKHOV B. N., BASHKIROV A. F., TSARKOV N. A., BRAVO B. L., ANISHEV A. I., CHARGED WITH CRIMES

UNDER ARTICLES 17, 58-8 AND 58-11 OF THE CRIMINAL CODE OF THE RSFSR

Evidence from the preliminary and judicial investigations into the clandestine counterrevolutionary terrorist group which, under the leadership of the so-called “Leningrad Center,” prepared and carried out in Leningrad on December 1, 1934 the murder of Comrade S. M. Kirov, member of the Presidium of the USSR Central Executive Committee, member of the Politburo of the Central Committee of the AUCP(b), and secretary of the Central Committee and the Leningrad Committee of the AUCP(b), established that a so-called “Moscow Center” was simultaneously engaged in clandestine counterrevolutionary activity in Moscow. It was formed by leaders of the former anti-Soviet Zinovievite group, which, notwithstanding its capitulation, had not ceased its underground work aimed at counterrevolutionary objectives in the spirit of the so-called Zinovievite-Trotskyist platform.

The ensuing preliminary investigation fully confirmed this information and established that the so-called “Moscow Center” included Zinoviev G. E., Evdokimov G. E., Hertik A. M., Bakaev I. P., Kuklin A. S., Kamenev L. B., Sharov Ya. V., Fedorov G. F., and Gorshenin I. S. These persons rallied several of the most active members of the former anti-Soviet Zinovievite grouping around themselves and established systematic ties with members of the Leningrad group, convicted by the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court of the USSR on December 28–29, 1934 in the above-mentioned case.

The preliminary investigation established that the so-called “Moscow Center” did not confine itself to maintaining ties with the underground Leningrad group of former Zinovievites and with individual sympathizers in other cities, but acted, in the words of defend\<ant\> Fedorov, as a political center that systematically directed the clandestine counterrevolutionary activities of both the Moscow and Leningrad groups over a number of years.

Despite the denials of the main leaders of the “Moscow Center”—the accused Zinoviev, Hertik, and Kamenev—the investigation considers this circumstance fully established not only by the testimony of persons convicted in the “Leningrad Center” case, especially Levin, who admitted that he was an authorized representative of the “Moscow Center,” Rumyantsev, Myasnikov, and oth\<ers\>, but also by the testimony of members of the “Moscow Center” itself—Bakaev, Evdokimov, Gorshenin, Fedorov, and Sharov—and members of the underground Moscow counterrevolutionary Zinovievite group—Safarov, Bravo, Anishev, and Gessen.

One of the chief tasks of this underground counterrevolutionary group, headed by the “Moscow Center,” was to preserve its cadres, accumulate strength, and intensify among its members feelings of bitterness and open hatred toward the leaders of the party and Soviet power.

Defend\<ant\> Fedorov, describing the activities of the underground counterrevolutionary Zinovievite group, confirmed that:

“The basic task facing the Zinovievite organization at that time was to go underground in an organized way and reorganize the methods of its underground work, while retaining the main opposition cadres inside the party. These cadres were retained by issuing instructions to submit false, duplicitous statements to the party expressing agreement with and submission to the congress’s decisions…”

These duplicitous and treacherous methods of counterrevolutionary activity were characteristic of the tactics of struggle adopted and widely practiced by the members of the underground counterrevolutionary Zinovievite group, who stopped at nothing to achieve their aims.

In his testimony of January 4 of the cur\<rent\> ye\<ar\>, speaking of how “the Moscow Zinovievite counterrevolutionary center crystallized, whose voice was heeded by participants in the Zinovievite groups,” defend\<ant\> Bakaev stated:

“… Our center had no positive program that it could set against the party’s Central Committee. There was only malicious, hostile criticism of the party’s most important measures, criticism worthy of the White Guard degenerates of ‘Poslednie Novosti.’

Therein lies the tragedy of our position: in our struggle against the party, and in our malicious attacks on it, we sank straight into the White Guard swamp, the counterrevolutionary swamp. Lenin was a thousand times right when he said that the logic of struggle leads every opposition to direct, undisguised counterrevolution. Such is the outcome we must acknowledge…”

Describing the counterrevolutionary methods used by the Zinovievite underground in its struggle against Soviet power, Safarov G. I., a member of the group whose case was separated and referred for further investigation, testified:

“After the especially frequent and lively meetings of 1932, when the counterrevolutionary plotters against the party of Lenin and Stalin expected to turn the known temporary difficulties of the transition from the First to the Second Five-Year Plan to their advantage, all the underground circles, frightened by arrests and the crushing of the Ryutinists, returned to their usual way of life—to clandestine undermining, creeping counterrevolution, and poisoning the wells…”

The same purpose was served by the widespread circulation among the members of this underground group of information about various domestic and foreign political events, given an openly counterrevolutionary interpretation with deliberate distortion of reality.

The same defend\<ant\> Bakaev states in this connection that:

“The information was invariably counterrevolutionary in substance; it covered every issue of domestic and foreign policy…

Thus, little by little, the threads of political connections extended from Zinoviev’s apartment through our apartments to the Moscow comrades, directly to Leningrad and other places; the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite underground was emerging and growing stronger…”

This counterrevolutionary “information” was transmitted through several group members closest to Zinoviev and Evdokimov, who exploited their official positions for criminal purposes.

The investigation established that this was precisely how members of the “Moscow Center”—Kuklin, Hertik, Evdokimov, and Sharov—acted, regularly traveling to Leningrad and maintaining counterrevolutionary ties with members of the “Leningrad Center.”

In its struggle against Soviet power, as defend\<ant\> Fedorov testified, this clandestine counterrevolutionary group did not disdain any means:

“… Members of the organization were breeding grounds for the vilest slander, rumors, and gossip about the party leadership…

… Throughout the entire activity of the Zinovievite organization, malice and hatred for the party leadership were cultivated in its ranks, and the organization did not hesitate to resort to slander, lies, deception, and distortion of facts—that is, it employed all the vilest means borrowed from the arsenal of fascism…”

Safarov G. I. also speaks of the counterrevolutionary degeneration of the underground Zinovievite group.

Safarov testified that:

“We increasingly stewed in our own juices, deepening our degeneration and dragging into the swamp all those who, like ourselves, were cast by the magnificent force of the proletarian advance against all the filth of the past into the ghostly counterrevolutionary little world of internal emigration…”

“All thoughts and ideas amounted to giving ideological form to bourgeois-restorationist reaction against socialism…

Politically, all counterrevolutionary, anti-party groups in the anti-party underground merged into a single reactionary mass. We became condensers of the counterrevolutionary malice and hatred of the remnants of the exploiting classes…”

It was no accident that genuinely fascist methods of struggle and open terrorist sentiments grew and strengthened on this soil, poisoned by the Zinovievite underground, leading to the vile murder of Comrade S. M. Kirov.

A number of defendants, both those charged in the present case and those whose cases were separated and referred for further investigation, speak of the inevitability of such sentiments, logically prepared by the entire nature and substance of the Zinovievites’ clandestine activities.

This is asserted by defend\<ant\> Tsarkov, who directly connected Nikolaev’s shot with “extremist sentiments,” as he put it, arising from the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary positions, and by defendant Bashkirov, who stated:

“That Nikolaev’s shot was the result of his (Nikolaev’s) membership and training in the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievite organization” and that “the entire counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievite organization bears political responsibility for this act…”

The investigation did not establish facts providing grounds to charge members of the “Moscow Center” directly with having consented to, or given any instructions for, the organization of the terrorist act against Comrade Kirov. Nevertheless, the entire situation and the whole character of the clandestine counterrevolutionary “Moscow Center” demonstrate that they knew of the terrorist sentiments of the group’s members and inflamed those sentiments.

The members of the “Moscow Center,” and above all the accused Zinoviev, Evdokimov, Hertik, and Kamenev, must therefore bear not only moral and political responsibility but also responsibility under Soviet law for the consequences of their underground counterrevolutionary activities, which pushed their Leningrad group onto the path of terrorist acts.

The accused themselves cannot fail to accept this responsibility, at least in moral and political terms.

Thus, speaking of his responsibility for the atrocity committed on December 1, 1934, defend\<ant\> Zinoviev testified:

“… The objective course of events is such that, with bowed head, I must say: the anti-party struggle, which in earlier years took especially acute forms in Leningrad, could not but contribute to the degeneration of these scoundrels. This vile murder cast such a sinister light on the entire preceding anti-party struggle that I acknowledge: the party is entirely right in what it says concerning the political responsibility of the former anti-party ‘Zinovievite’ group for the murder committed…”

EXAMINATION

## OF EVDOKIMOV Grigory Yeremeyevich.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Defendant EVDOKIMOV, I have several basic questions: can you confirm that the Zinovievites’ return to the party after the Fifteenth Congress was essentially duplicitous; that, at the same time as they returned to the party, an underground Zinovievite organization was formed which fought the party leadership over a number of years?

EVDOKIMOV: I confirm it.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Do you acknowledge that this entire organization and its activities were essentially counterrevolutionary?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I acknowledge it.

CHAIRMAN: Do you acknowledge that this organization was led by the so-called “Moscow Center”?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I acknowledge it.

CHAIRMAN: Do you confirm that this center existed until very recently?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I acknowledge it.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Do you confirm that this “Moscow Center” maintained connections with and issued certain directives to its supporters both in Leningrad and in several other cities?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I confirm it.

CHAIRMAN: Do you deny the fact that over a number of years, until very recently, the leaders of the “Moscow Center” systematically displayed intensely hostile attitudes toward the leaders of our party and government, and inculcated those attitudes in their supporters both in Moscow and in certain other cities, particularly Leningrad?

EVDOKIMOV: No, I do not deny it.

CHAIRMAN: Do you confirm that intensely hostile attitudes toward the representatives of our party and government, cultivated for a number of years, engendered terrorist sentiments among members of the Leningrad group which subsequently led to the murder of Comrade KIROV?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I acknowledge it.

CHAIRMAN: Do you confirm that one of the main tasks of the underground organization was to preserve its cadres, accumulate forces, and strengthen hostility toward certain leaders of our party?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I acknowledge it.

CHAIRMAN: Do you wish to give additional testimony?

EVDOKIMOV: Yes, I do.

CHAIRMAN: Please proceed…

EVDOKIMOV: When I signed the interrogation record stating that I pleaded guilty to the charges within the limits of my testimony, that admission did not encompass everything I had done up to the present moment.

I acknowledge this dry formula of the accusation brought against me in the investigation, but its political substance must be recalled to a greater extent than was done during my testimony.

We returned to the party after the decision of the Fifteenth Congress, but not as proletarian revolutionaries who had genuinely renounced their political positions, which the Fifteenth Congress had declared counterrevolutionary and incompatib\<le\> with our remaining in the party’s ranks. No. We returned to the party having deceived it on this most important question, for our readmission was conditional upon fully and finally acknowledging the counterrevolutionary nature of our struggle against the party in 1925–1927. We acknowledged this in words. In practice, throughout those years we remained in those counterrevolutionary positions. Moreover, because through our own fault we were cut off from everything that was happening in the Soviet country, our counterrevolutionary sentiments stewed, so to speak, in their own juices and grew ever more acute.

I have thought a great deal about all this, as have my fellow-thinkers, but to my deepest regret everything became fully clear to me only when I found myself imprisoned. During all these years, I repeat, we stewed in our own juices and set ourselves the goal of changing the party leadership so as to implement our counterrevolutionary positions, which we advanced as fundamentally different from the general line pursued by the party and its Central Committee. Blinded \[5\] by our counterrevolutionary positions, blinded \[6\] by malicious hostility toward the party leadership, we did not see, sense, or feel what was happening in the country, among the working masses. I shall not speak to the court about the enormous, decisive historical importance of what happened inside our country during these years. Those achievements contributed to the revolutionizing \[7\] of the entire international workers’ movement. It is not for me to tell the court about that. I only wish to say that we did not perceive what was happening in the country as proletarian revolutionaries, but judged it from our counterrevolutionary positions. From reality, from what was happening in the countryside and cities among the broad masses of millions, we took only what we thought supported those positions—that is, the difficulties encountered in advancing toward the victory of socialism under the Communist Party’s leadership. Yet we assessed these difficulties quite differently from the party, the working class, and the working people of our country. We did not regard them as difficulties arising from the very nature of the tremendous tasks facing the working class and working masses. No. We regarded them as difficulties arising because the party, under its existing leadership, was following the wrong course. During the investigation, I and, as far as I know, some of my fellow-thinkers in this counterrevolutionary struggle tried to distinguish different stages: one when our anti-party, counterrevolutionary sentiments had supposedly given way to entirely loyal attitudes, and others when the anti-party sentiments grew stronger, increased, and deepened. This was a mistake both on my part and on the part of those fellow-thinkers who tried to take this position. Throughout all the years of the existence of our clandestine counterrevolutionary organization, we remained in the same counterrevolutionary positions; that is the essence of this underground organization.

Not one development in the life of our country passed without our assessing it from our counterrevolutionary positions. I shall try to be brief here, but in order to characterize the essentials of our assessments and attitudes I consider it necessary to take up a decision of immense historical importance: collectivization. On this question too we held anti-party counterrevolutionary positions and, as with other questions, paid attention first of all to the enormous obstacles that naturally stood in the way of the party, the working class, and the peasant masses carrying out this historic undertaking. Our basic assessment was as follows: we regarded it as madness, an adventure, to believe it possible within a few years—two or three years—to transform a small proprietor into a socialist farmer, to make conscious socialists in only a few years out of small proprietors steeped for centuries in individualistic attitudes. You can see that in posing the question this way we did not differ at all from the Mensheviks or other enemies of the working class, who placed great hopes on this issue.

How did we differ in this matter from all the enemies of the working class? What label of the enemy detachments did we wear? Like the international counterrevolutionary bourgeoisie and international Menshevism, we calculated that in implementing collectivization the party would encounter such insurmountable obstacles that our effort to change the party leadership, our preparatory work to turn the party away from the path it was following, would succeed thanks to the difficulties that arose during collectivization.

We watched what was happening in the countryside undergoing collectivization not as proletarian revolutionaries ready to devote everything they had—all their strength, knowledge, and energy—to the victorious completion of that immense historical process. No\! We watched as enemies of the party, as enemies of the working class.

Let the comrades who share my views and are sitting here before the court recall how carefully we selected facts, even little facts, which in our opinion supported our counterrevolutionary positions on collectivization. How did we assess the difficulties of the first collective-farm spring, naturally attended by great obstacles? I recall the assessment I myself gave, because that spring I was on an assignment in the North Caucasus and traveled among collective farms. I said that what saved us to some degree that time was solely the fact that weather conditions extended the possible sowing period by a whole month or even more (it had been a very early spring). I said that without this circumstance, given the immense struggle between the kulaks and those who followed our party in order to complete that first collective-farm spring by fulfilling the tasks of the sowing campaign, without the extension of the collective-farm sowing period, the party would have failed in that first sowing.

And this was repeated at every spring sowing and every harvest campaign.

The party itself did not conceal from the workers of our country or the world the difficulties connected with collectivization, nor did it conceal the enormous losses caused by shortcomings during the harvest campaigns. Very large figures were cited for these losses.

But one thing was for the party, acknowledging those material losses arising from shortcomings in the collective-farm movement, to summon and mobilize workers and collective farmers and organize them to overcome the shortcomings, strengthen labor discipline, and transform the mentality of yesterday’s small proprietor. We, however, judged the same phenomenon quite differently—as confirmation of our own position: we regarded it as madness to collectivize the countryside at the pace adopted by the party.

The same was true of industrialization. On that issue too we selected all the facts and, when there were none, made them up, using rumors, hearsay, and gossip to find in the surrounding circumstances everything that, in our opinion, could substantiate our assessment of the reality and progress of industrialization, rather than the party’s assessment, which was correct.

We did not merely gather these isolated facts to show that, owing to the supposedly incorrect leadership exercised by the Central Committee under Comrade STALIN, industrialization incurred enormous unnecessary additional costs, both economic and political. No: we had our own generally negative assessment of everything taking place in this field. We said, for example, that public information about the progress of industrialization did not correspond to reality. We said that the Central Committee was deceiving the party and the working class in this matter by presenting a state of affairs radically different from the actual situation.

Our attitude toward the difficulties naturally encountered in the initial steps of giants such as the tractor factories did not differ from the attitude of the international bourgeoisie toward those difficulties.

We said that industrialization was being carried out without an accurate accounting of the country’s resources, without due concentration and reinforcement at the truly decisive links and facilities, and that the plans themselves were unrealistic and overstrained, leading to enormous imbalances among decisive elements of the national economy. For example, we took the difficulties of rail\<way\> trans\<port\> and concluded that, if industrialization were pursued not as it was under the current leadership but as we proposed, achievements would be greater and there would be no such glaring contradiction between those achievements and the material circumstances of the working class as existed in the present situation.

We again asserted, slanderously, that the material condition of the working class was not improving but instead deteriorating. I must explain further what unnecessary overhead costs meant in economic and political terms. I have already addressed the economic aspect. Politically, in our language, it meant that the pace of collectivization in the countryside was arousing hostility toward the dictatorship of the proletariat among millions of urban workers as well as among the peasants.

It is quite natural that I am agitated; perhaps in places my revolutionary point of view will be imprecise, and I ask in advance that this be pointed out or not associated with the overall nature of my speech.

As to the working class, we said that it was bearing tremendous hardships, that dissatisfaction with the party and Soviet power was therefore growing ever greater among the workers. That was what we meant when we spoke of excessive overhead costs, and hence came our malicious counterrevolutionary assertions and slanders that “the present party leadership will cost the country dearly” or, as we said, “STALIN will cost the country dearly.”

Regarding internal party leadership, we gave the harshest, most malicious and slanderous descriptions of the party regime. In repeating our counterrevolutionary positions against the party leadership, we failed to see what every rank-and-file party member and every ordinary worker sees: the party’s powerful growth, unprecedented unity in its ranks, the unprecedented development of inner-party democracy, and the involvement of the whole mass of party workers in building the state.

We did not see or perceive any of those processes, which were of enormous worldwide importance, not only for the Soviet country.

I told the personnel of the People’s Commissariat of Internal Affairs who were conducting the investigation that, as I sat there, I was reading our contemporary fiction—the book by KATAEV \[8\], “Forward” \[9\]. That work struck my conscience painfully, for it reflected an unprecedented rise and enthusiasm among the masses.

We said that the party’s internal regime was one of enormous, unprecedented domination by a bureaucratic apparatus, and we went so far as to assert that the party as such did not exist, only a Central Committee headed by STALIN. We said that STALIN stood at the center of everything, that this was a regime of personal dictatorship, and that on the most important matters of principle determining correct class policy, not only the party but even the Central Committee was often confronted with accomplished facts and decisions already made.

On questions of leadership of the international labor movement, we continued making our slanderous accusations that an incorrect understanding of the idea of the victory of socialism in one country led, at decisive moments for the international workers’ movement as a whole, to the leadership of the Comintern and the Central Committee of our party, the Politburo and Central Committee, failing to take all necessary steps to activate that movement in keeping with the possibilities presented in each country.

All this amounted to the slanderous assertion that the Central Committee and our party were doing enormous harm to the international workers’ movement and hindering the development of the international proletarian movement.

As an example, I shall cite my last conversation with ZINOVIEV. It not only characterizes our relations on this question but is valuable because it took place toward the end of 1934.

I visited ZINOVIEV, probably on November 12 or 18, one of those days off. We discussed Comintern affairs, and he made an accusation against the Central Committee to the effect that efforts to establish an international united front were being conducted fundamentally incorrectly; at that time, in France for example, the initiative and leadership in establishing that front had in practice been handed over to the Second International. These are crucial questions determining party policy, upon which the development of socialist construction in our country and the destiny of the entire international workers’ movement depend. The court can see from what I have said that on all these decisive questions we held counterrevolutionary positions and went so far as to make malicious, slanderous accusations against the party and Central Committee. In all these matters we essentially differed in no way from the entire international counterrevolutionary rabble, which, like us, like me, pinned its hopes of crushing Soviet power on failure in collectivization. We made similar assessments of what was happening in industrialization. Broadly speaking, such were our attitudes; we lived in our own circles, stewing in our counterrevolutionary juices. Everything was poisoned by this venom, as was the consciousness of our fellow-thinkers. There was not a scrap of gossip or the foulest ordinary rumor that we did not seize upon and discuss in our midst. As time passed, we paid ever greater attention to these things, eagerly and joyfully picking up every rumor of disagreements \<in\> the Politburo or Central Committee. During the investigation I said that I had always been skeptical of such rumors. But what matters to the court and the case is not whether I was skeptical but how each of us treated those rumors. We pinned tremendous hopes on these ordinary rumors, which we invented ourselves, rumors about disagreements in the Politburo and Central Committee: now STALIN was dissatisfied with VOROSHILOV, now VOROSHILOV with STALIN, now STALIN with KAGANOVICH. We seized on anything we thought could be used as proof that the party was harming the country by following an incorrect course and that elements of discontent had developed in the country. At one point, I cannot now recall the exact circumstances, we had lively discussions about growing dissatisfaction among the commanders of the Red Army. We did not merely live with these attitudes ourselves: we poisoned everyone connected with us with them. Thus when we are charged with responsibility for the terrorist sentiments that grew, strengthened, and so tragically manifested themselves for the party in Leningrad through the clandestine counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization, I state firmly: yes, it is precisely for this that we must bear responsibility. This was the poison with which we corrupted the consciousness of our followers, inculcated these sentiments, and drove some of them to such extreme counterrevolutionary degeneration that they became White Guard bandits, the murderers of S. M. KIROV.

However hard it is, this must be acknowledged. This admission did not come easily to me. I read ZINOVIEV’s statement, about which he has just spoken to the court. He says he is terrified by the thought of being lumped together with those White Guard bandits, those scoundrels. Yes, that terrifies me too\!

But ZINOVIEV and I must not forget that those scoundrels, those White Guard bandits, became such because for many years their consciousness was poisoned by the counterrevolutionary venom I have described.

We should have foreseen this, and only our counterrevolutionary blindness and bitterness toward the party leadership prevented us from doing so.

I return to where I began, to what I said at the outset, because it is related to my testimony during the investigation. For a time I myself adopted the same position as some of my fellow-thinkers when explaining to the investigation the different stages through which we passed. Some comrades initially attached great importance to the fact that the intensification of the activities of the Leningrad center of the underground counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization coincided with the period of least activity of our Moscow center.

And what does that change? To what extent does it lessen our responsibility for our appearance before this court? Not in the slightest\! Very well, our activity declined, but did those here in Leningrad not consider us to remain in our old positions? (And we did remain in those positions.)

I have already said in my testimony that even when the course of events drove us into a corner and we found no way or grounds to criticize the line of the Central Committee and party, what did we do? In such cases we declared: it is our line that is being implemented, the line we advanced in our united Zinovievite-Trotskyist platform of 1925–1927. Even at such moments we always remained in that position: not for one instant did we abandon our anti-party past of 1925–1927. In my statement and testimony I said, among other things, that ZINOVIEV’s attitude before the Seventeenth Congress and in the first period after it was more loyal than at any other time during the existence of our underground counterrevolutionary center. I said something untrue—not because I wanted to lie, but because I was mistaken.

My mistake arises from the fact that we had become so accustomed to the system of deceiving the party that even in prison it is difficult to eradicate that system from our psychology.

What do I mean here? What did the speeches by KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV at the Seventeenth Party Congress amount to? ZINOVIEV said that he was sincere in that speech. It is not for me to judge the sincerity or insincerity of that speech; I consider only the political aspect. I note at once that what I say about KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV applies equally to me and, to a somewhat lesser degree, to the fellow-thinkers present here.

ZINOVIEV went to the congress rostrum and again analyzed, rather successfully, the counterrevolutionary nature of the positions we had held in 1925–1927. But what he and KAMENEV told the congress was only half the truth, and half the truth under certain circumstances can be worse than the most harmful lie, just as there are times when a lie is told for salvation.

He should have told the party congress: these are our present views, but the congress must know that we, KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV, are renouncing those views, while within the party there are persons known to us both who continue counterrevolutionary activity; there are numerous enemies in the party’s ranks who retain their party standing solely to fight the party more effectively.

By failing to say this, their speeches merely lowered the party’s revolutionary vigilance. Many at the congress believed ZINOVIEV, many applauded him; STALIN himself applauded ZINOVIEV.

By withholding that second half of the truth, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV further reinforced in the minds of our fellow-thinkers the supposed legitimacy of deceiving the party.

Previously it had been a matter of declarations to the Central Control Commission, statements to the party’s Central Committee, or other maneuvers. Here, however, it took place at the Seventeenth Party Congress, a congress whose voice was heard with unprecedented attention throughout the world, by all our friends and all our class enemies.

At that very Congress of Victors, as the party rightly called it, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV found it possible to withhold the second half of the truth, turning their speeches into statements that reinforced the ideas of our enemy.

If it is possible to deceive the Seventeenth Party Congress, the Congress of Victors, where in our arsenal of crimes could we find greater proof of the supposed legitimacy of deception?

I repeat that responsibility for these speeches rests not only with ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. I too was at the congress and had a guest pass; who prevented me from speaking there, at the congress, to tell the second half of the truth?

Awareness of this guilt of mine before the party is one of the most painful admissions. I repeat that it is not only I who am responsible, nor only ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. So \[10\] strong were our counterrevolutionary attitudes, so \[11\] hard did we strain our brains to the limit, that not one of us came forward at the congress to declare that we would not deceive it again.

In the same statement ZINOVIEV makes a mistake often made by people in a position similar to ours.

At the end of that statement he says that he was always devoted to the interests of the working class. I shall now speak not of ZINOVIEV but of all of us. No, subjectively we were not fully devoted to the interests of the working class. If we had been, what could have prevented us from perceiving the great things happening in our country? What could have prevented a person genuinely devoted to the working class from perceiving them when even the fiercest enemies of our class recognize our achievements, when the power of the Soviet country has risen to an unprecedented degree, when that country has become a decisive factor not only in the eyes of our working masses but also in those of our class enemies? What could have prevented us at such a moment from properly understanding what was happening, as someone devoted to the country’s interests should have understood it?

What prevented us was our counterrevolutionary bitterness and blindness, our malice toward the party leadership. This is not a matter of subjective disposition.

I am finishing. Perhaps I have omitted something, perhaps something important and serious. My speech was not written down, and speaking is very difficult.

I conclude by asking the court to believe that I have truly and sincerely, without reservation, acknowledged the full gravity of the criminal acts I committed over the years since 1925, acts which caused enormous harm to the party, the working class, all the working people of our Soviet country, and the entire international workers’ movement, acts that led to the tragic event in Leningrad, the tragic White Guard bandit murder of Comrade KIROV.

I shall accept any decision of the court as fully deserved by me and, believe me, I speak sincerely, only two circumstances will console me or soften my grief. The first is that I have truly broken with that disgraceful past.

I shall return for a moment to what happened at the Seventeenth Party Congress.

This manifested hostility not merely to the party leadership but the highest degree of hostility toward it: a situation in which a small handful of counterrevolutionaries sets itself against the entire party.

My grief will be somewhat eased by the knowledge that I have withdrawn from that hostile web. The second circumstance that will somewhat ease my grief, and again believe in my sincerity, will be the knowledge that the cause of the final victory of communism is in reliable hands: those of our party, its Central Committee, and its brilliant leader Comrade STALIN.

EXAMINATION

## OF ANISHEV, Anatoly Isayevich.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant ANISHEV\! Do you plead guilty to having been an active member of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite group?

ANISHEV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: Tell us what the structure of this group was, what ties it had with the “Moscow Center,” and whether there were links with organizations in other cities.

ANISHEV: The organizational structure of our underground counterrevolutionary organization corresponded to the tactics adopted; that is, it did not have any definite, rigidly fixed form. It was based chiefly on personal ties, ties passed along a chain from the center to each member, thus encompassing all those who after the Fifteenth Congress had not withdrawn or abandoned the counterrevolutionary positions we held.

During the preliminary investigation I myself raised the question and testified that what we tried to portray as merely preserving personal ties was certainly not simply that, but genuine organizational ties, albeit in the form corresponding to our tactics.

I pointed out that when I settled in Tashkent, old personal ties were not at all necessary for establishing contact with all the new members of the organization who arrived later, even though I had never met them before. I established contact with them very quickly. The same was true when I came to Leningrad: I had no need of old personal ties, only of knowing that they held the same counterrevolutionary positions as I did.

As to the nature of the connections: personally, when I moved to Leningrad, I considered it my duty to visit one of the members of the center and obtain appropriate information. In the first period after my return from Central Asia, I mostly visited HERTIK; more recently, BAKAEV. Whenever I went to Moscow, I always visited them. Once I visited EVDOKIMOV, but lately I always went to BAKAEV. I also visited SAFAROV.

The connection between Moscow and Leningrad was maintained through visits by members of the center from Moscow. I know, in particular, of two visits by HERTIK, who, although he was not a Muscovite, came precisely as a representative of the center concerning attitudes toward the ZINOVIEV–RYUTIN affair, and so on. That was how connections between Moscow and Leningrad were maintained.

No one authorized me to maintain such ties, but it was understood that when I traveled to Moscow I would meet one of the center’s members. Likewise, whenever someone came to Leningrad, nobody doubted that whatever he conveyed to us would be passed on to those we met in Leningrad. My contacts in Leningrad were less regular than in Moscow, but I had ties there with RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV, and other members of the Leningrad organization.

CHAIRMAN: Did you meet LEVIN?

ANISHEV: I met LEVIN by chance.

CHAIRMAN: Which of them belonged to the Leningrad Center?

ANISHEV: I do not know.

ANISHEV: KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, and HERTIK. As for FEDOROV, I was uncertain.

It seems to me that this conclusion predetermined everything that followed, our subsequent degeneration, and the counterrevolutionary path we then took.

It seems characteristic that the renewed activity of our underground organization coincided with periods of difficulty in socialist construction. It was precisely those difficulties that we emphasized and pinned our hopes on. The joy with which we picked up every rumor or report about difficulties on the socialist-construction front made this clear. Nothing remained of the Leftist phrases with which we had once covered ourselves. I think that even then it was perfectly clear to me that the Leftist phrases abundant in our \<plat\>forms by the Fifteenth Congress were a cover for those whom the party called a handful of capitalist restorers. SAFAROV then said that ZINOVIEV had approved the draft. SMILGA put the question clearly: he considered that capitalism had not yet exhausted its potential in the countryside and that therefore

any attempt to restrain development in the countryside was reactionary; our task was to put forward a version of the platform based on that position. \{He ???? ???? of two ????, but\} this slogan of \{????\} free\{\}\*m\* capitalist\{\}\*m\* development\{i\}\*a\* in the countryside found its place in the counter-theses. As to industrialization, all the conversations I had or heard, and every assessment of the policy pursued by the party, amounted to saying that it was demagogy and the projected pace was unrealistic. I remember, for example, a conversation at BAKAEV’s apartment when we discussed the program.

CHAIRMAN: Did HERTSBERG speak this way as a member of the “Moscow organization”?

ANISHEV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: With which representatives or members of the Moscow Center did you have such conversations?

ANISHEV: With BAKAEV, KUKLIN, and EVDOKIMOV.

CHAIRMAN: In particular, what conversations did KUKLIN have with you?

ANISHEV: I cannot recall in detail who said what at the gathering I testified about, at BAKAEV’s apartment early in 1933. BAKAEV and KUKLIN were there. Among other things, I relayed information from the Agricultural Academy about what was happening on the collective farms—slanderous, counterrevolutionary information. There was also discussion of the pig-iron production program, again from counterrevolutionary positions. BAKAEV and KUKLIN were present, but I cannot remember who said what exactly.

CHAIRMAN: When was that?

ANISHEV: Early in 1933.

CHAIRMAN: Perhaps you could touch somewhat on the question of fomenting hatred against the party leadership and Soviet government. To what could this lead, and to what did it lead?

ANISHEV: I have already said that whatever topic we began discussing—industrialization, international politics, collectivization, inner-party questions, or Comintern affairs—we always drew a direct line to the leadership and, in particular, the leaders of the party.

It seems to me extremely important for understanding what happened to realize that the hatred cultivated and nurtured in our organization was directed not merely at the leadership in general but personally at the party leaders. To understand the relationship between the expression of this hatred and the terrorist act that took place, it is important that this hatred was always aimed at a particular person, above all, of course, Comrade STALIN; Comrade KIROV occupied a special place in the cultivation of hatred among us.

I can cite all the conversations I have mentioned. I do not know whether it is necessary to repeat them all.

CHAIRMAN: No. We want to know who displayed particular malice toward the leadership. In what form was it expressed, and toward whom? Please clarify. Why did KIROV’s name provoke so much hatred, on whose part exactly, when, and in connection with what circumstances?

ANISHEV: The hatred of KIROV and the slanders directed against him began at the very moment he was appointed to Leningrad. From that moment, KIROV’s appointment was interpreted as the rise of Thermidorian reaction. Numerous slanderous rumors about KIROV appeared, and leaflets circulated containing all sorts of allegations against him…

CHAIRMAN: Who wrote those leaflets?

ANISHEV: GESSEN. First of all, KIROV’s biography was distorted: his advancement was portrayed not as the rise of a proletarian revolutionary but as the rise of Thermidorian reaction. KIROV was represented in this counterrevolutionary light in our circles. Likewise, in recent years, whenever Leningrad came up in conversation, just as any assessment of the general situation led to personal attacks on STALIN, assessments of the situation in Leningrad led to personal attacks on KIROV.

I recall, for example, a conversation with HERTIK to the effect that Smolny had become a completely dead and alien institution where no workers could be seen; from this we drew conclusions about the nature of KIROV’s leadership. I also recall a conversation which, if I am not mistaken, involved myself, TARASOV, TSEITLIN, and LUKIN \[16\], concerning a demonstration in Leningrad. I do not remember what holiday it marked, but we assessed it by saying that the previous link between the reviewing stand and the demonstrators no longer existed, that Leningrad’s leaders had become detached from the city’s working masses, neither knowing the workers nor being known by them.

Thus the fomenting of hatred, transmitted with every piece of information and every conversation, undoubtedly created the atmosphere that gave rise to what happened, namely KIROV’s murder.

Therefore I believe that fostering this hatred should essentially be considered incitement to terrorist acts.

CHAIRMAN: With which defendants in this case did you discuss KIROV?

ANISHEV: I mentioned HERTIK and GESSEN. I do not remember any other conversations about KIROV.

CHAIRMAN: Did you speak with RUMYANTSEV?

ANISHEV: The conversation with RUMYANTSEV took place in 1932. I have already described that conversation in which attacks were made on the leaders.

CHAIRMAN: In particular, who expressed special hatred toward STALIN?

ANISHEV: Among the defendants, it is hard to say. This savage hatred was perceptible in all the conversations. It was especially evident in conversations with HERTIK, in the conversations at BAKAEV’s apartment which I have described, and with EVDOKIMOV, who visited us and whom I last encountered on a street in Moscow.

CHAIRMAN: Did this fomenting of hatred manifest itself in a terrorist act?

ANISHEV: Yes, I believe that was the result of those conversations.

CHAIRMAN: What do you wish to add to your testimony?

ANISHEV: I have already said that the positions and policies we held were openly counterrevolutionary, positions of capitalist restoration. I have spoken of the forms and double-dealing we adopted as our tactics when we entered the party, when we believed we had not been defeated ideologically and that the leadership lacked ideas and, at any rate, had not defeated us ideologically. It seems to me that all this produced the counterrevolutionary crime that occurred. If by the Fifteenth Congress we already had behind us the November 7 demonstration and a number of other actions plainly beyond the bounds of inner-party struggle, lately, of course, there can no longer be any talk of inner-party struggle.

While inside the party, we were in fact waging a directly anti-Soviet and counterrevolutionary struggle aimed at the defeat of Soviet power.

I shall not speak in detail, since I have already given written testimony on this. I would only like to say what I have recently become convinced of. I worked on a collective farm in Tajikistan and reached the following conclusion: there is no difference between the policy pursued by the kulaks in collective farms and the policy we pursued in the party—the same double-dealing and duplicity. The same desire to seize on every failure and exaggerate it here and there.

CHAIRMAN: What do you mean by “here and there”?

ANISHEV: This tactic of the kulaks resembles the tactic we pursued inside the party like two drops of water, and both have the same aims. That is how far we sank.

CHAIRMAN: Did you have a program?

ANISHEV: Lately it was not customary among us to talk about a platform. We never discussed the old platform. I asked HERTIK what positive program we could implement, and he was unable to answer. He said the party had crushed us and that we could not devise anything else. In essence there was only one program. We could not dispute the victories of socialist construction achieved by the party.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant ANISHEV, do you wish to add anything?

ANISHEV: No.

SAKHOV: I have a question for ANISHEV. Let him say in which year we last met and exactly where.

ANISHEV: I cannot remember now.

SAKHOV: Well, roughly speaking, did we meet three, four, or five years ago?

ANISHEV: It was a long time ago.

SAKHOV: You do not even remember the meetings?

ANISHEV: No.

SAKHOV: I have a question for EVDOKIMOV. May I ask it?

CHAIRMAN: We are examining ANISHEV now; when it is your turn, you will have the opportunity to ask questions.

HERTIK: I have a question for ANISHEV. Let him say when and where we last saw each other.

ANISHEV: I remember well the last meeting during HERTIK’s visit to Leningrad in 1932. I cannot recall exactly now, but there was another visit by HERTIK to Leningrad. I remember two visits to Leningrad in 1933.

CHAIRMAN: Was that at the beginning or end of 1933?

ANISHEV: It was in mid-1933 and early 1932. I remember two meetings. The first took place during my visit, when I was at BASHKIROV’s; the second when NATANSON \[17\] was there. One meeting took place at the State Publishing House on that same visit, while the other was at another time. In 1933 I did not meet HERTIK; nor did I meet him in 1934.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant ANISHEV, sit down.

EXAMINATION

## OF BAKAEV, IVAN PETROVICH.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant BAKAEV, please tell us who belonged to the Moscow Center.

BAKAEV: It is difficult for me to say who belonged to the Moscow Center up to the most recent period, but I know everyone who belonged to the group up to 1932, namely ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, FEDOROV, HERTIK, KUKLIN, GORSHENIN, and myself.

CHAIRMAN: And was SHAROV a member of the Center?

BAKAEV: Yes, SHAROV and KHARITONOV.

CHAIRMAN: KHARITONOV belonged to it until 1932?

BAKAEV: Yes, until 1932. After that it is hard to say, since this central group usually met at ZINOVIEV’s home, and I stopped visiting ZINOVIEV in 1932.

CHAIRMAN: At the face-to-face confrontation on January 9, you testified that you knew of a Zinovievite center in Moscow comprising ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, KAMENEV, BAKAEV, SHAROV, FEDOROV, KUKLIN, and HERTIK, and until 1932 also KHARITONOV?

BAKAEV: I had previously told the investigators that in fact I was a member of this central group, because although I stopped visiting ZINOVIEV, I continued to meet several comrades whom I knew still visited him, and not only did I continue to meet them, but I also continued discussions on anti-party topics with individual members of the group. That was how things stood.

CHAIRMAN: At the same confrontation you were asked, “Were you a member of the Moscow Center?”

BAKAEV: They all considered me one of their own.

CHAIRMAN: Being regarded as one of their own is one thing; being regarded as a member of the Center is another. Do you deny belonging to the Center until very recently? There was a leading center directing the work of the whole Zinovievite organization not only in Moscow but in Leningrad as well. Were you a member of that center up to the most recent period?

BAKAEV: This center effectively existed until 1929. There were several meetings during that period, which I attended. I know there were regular meetings, though I was not at them all because until May 1931 I did not live in Moscow. After 1929 I know of no meetings of that kind, apart from brief, accidental gatherings at ZINOVIEV’s apartment involving ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. I do not recall KHARITONOV, but EVDOKIMOV was there and we discussed TROTSKY’s exile. That was a more or less organized meeting; ordinarily, however, two or three people met ZINOVIEV by chance without prior arrangement. As I had not visited ZINOVIEV since 1931, it is difficult for me to say whether or not I actually remained a member of the Center. I met individual comrades who belonged to it and discussed political matters with them. The discussions were not merely anti-party but essentially counterrevolutionary.

CHAIRMAN: I do not quite understand your testimony of January 9. It was recorded there: “I acknowledge that until recently I, together with EVDOKIMOV, was a member of the Moscow Center.” And when EVDOKIMOV was asked, “Do you confirm BAKAEV’s testimony?” he replied, “I confirm BAKAEV’s testimony.”

BAKAEV: I did not tell any of my comrades that I was withdrawing and, in practice, did not withdraw; therefore I was a member of the Center.

CHAIRMAN: So you are saying that the Center has not met in full lately?

BAKAEV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: But all nine people belonged to the Moscow Center at one time or another?

BAKAEV: Yes, and until 1932 KHARITONOV as well.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Then why did you single out KHARITONOV in this case? For what reasons?

BAKAEV: For quite a long time I did not encounter KHARITONOV at ZINOVIEV’s,

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Did he himself drift away from you?

BAKAEV: Apparently, yes.

CHAIRMAN: KHARITONOV is not a defendant in this case. I ask about him only because you single him out in your testimony: you say that KHARITONOV belonged to the Center until 1932. Thus it is clear that the other nine remained members until recently.

BAKAEV: In 1932 I did not meet him at ZINOVIEV’s or at the homes of the comrades with whom I maintained friendly relations.

CHAIRMAN: Now tell us: do you consider that all the activities of your organization were essentially counterrevolutionary and anti-Soviet?

BAKAEV: Yes, essentially they were.

CHAIRMAN: What tasks and objectives did your organization, and the Moscow Center in particular, pursue? I mean the recent period, not 1928–1929, but 1934–1933 and even 1932.

BAKAEV: Unfortunately I cannot answer the question as phrased—what objective our organization pursued in the past year. I know of only one objective: the exchange of political information.

CHAIRMAN: With a view to replacing the party leadership and changing the government?

BAKAEV: There were discussions about changing the party leadership in 1928–1929. Afterward there were no such discussions.

CHAIRMAN: But you gathered together, talked, told various anti-Soviet jokes and political gossip. Did you have some goal in mind?

BAKAEV: I hesitate to say that there was such a goal. We simply had nowhere else to go.

CHAIRMAN: But your aim was to discredit our party leadership and replace it with other people. Apparently there were discussions about this throughout those years. Your fellow-thinkers seated here say so.

BAKAEV: We did not set ourselves that task, but ordinarily the conversations among us were directed toward discrediting the existing leadership.

CHAIRMAN: Then what objective did you pursue by engaging in this discrediting activity?

BAKAEV: Perhaps merely to obtain personal satisfaction. We did not set ourselves political objectives of that kind. At least I know of none.

ANISHEV said that we counted on the failure of Soviet power and the overthrow of the leadership. There was no such orientation\! We never set ourselves such tasks\! He also spoke of special hatred for KIROV on the part of the Moscow Center and the Leningrad Zinovievites. Well, hand on heart, I must say I never saw such personal hatred of KIROV among the comrades with whom I discussed him. What is more, in 1932 I met Vladimir LEVIN, who called KIROV nothing but “Our Mironych.” His opinion of KIROV, who had managed to win enormous authority in Leningrad, was highly favorable.

Speaking honestly, I cannot say that KIROV occupied a special place as an object of hatred among the people I knew. I know that KIROV was particularly tolerant toward former oppositionists.

CHAIRMAN: Then I shall put the question differently: “What line did members of the Moscow Center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievite underground disseminate among your fellow-thinkers and participants?” That question was put to you during the investigation.

BAKAEV: The line was essentially anti-Soviet and counterrevolutionary. It held that the existing leadership was incapable of coping with the difficulties facing our country and the international workers’ movement. I fully confirm all the statements I made in describing our attitude to the building of collective farms; in fact we saw only the negative sides. Under ZINOVIEV’s bad influence, we said that collectivization was being carried out by force, that it would ruin the countryside and agriculture, that it entailed consequences we could not yet foresee; we said that if collectivization continued in the forms pursued until then—and we were speaking of 1929–1930—we would face the danger of a rupture between the working class and the peasantry.

We had a very negative assessment of the situation in the leadership of the Comintern.

CHAIRMAN: Do you confirm EVDOKIMOV’s testimony concerning collectivization and industrialization?

BAKAEV: In general, I agree.

CHAIRMAN: In what respects do you disagree?

BAKAEV: On certain particular positions.

CHAIRMAN: What other positions did you disseminate?

BAKAEV: We spread malicious slander, telling our supporters that the Central Committee did not fight the leaders of the Right deviation energetically enough, that it was insincere in its decisions to struggle against the Right, and that it itself violated inner-party democracy. But EVDOKIMOV has already spoken on this, and I shall confine myself to expressing my agreement with him.

Even after I stopped visiting ZINOVIEV, I knew from GORSHENIN that both ZINOVIEV and the others assessed the results of the First Five-Year Plan negatively. We regarded the figures describing its fulfillment as inflated, claimed that the plan had not been fulfilled, that it had failed especially as regards quality indicators and above all labor productivity, and attributed this to weak party leadership, saying that the Central Committee could not cope with the fundamental tasks of economic construction.

GORSHENIN conveyed to me, quoting ZINOVIEV, similarly anti-party and essentially counterrevolutionary discussions concerning the Seventeenth Party Conference resolution on improving workers’ material conditions two- or threefold.

The Ivanovo events \[18\] were regarded by us, again according to ZINOVIEV, as an expression of the dissatisfaction of a considerable part of the working class with the general policy of the party and Soviet power.

It is difficult to recall all the specific cases we discussed in groups of two or three to illustrate the shortcomings of the party leadership.

CHAIRMAN: You not only pointed out shortcomings of the party leadership but also argued that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV could replace it?

BAKAEV: In speaking of these shortcomings, we often remarked that the existing difficulties could be reduced if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were brought into the party leadership.

CHAIRMAN: Was the question of replacing the leadership raised?

BAKAEV: The question of changing the leadership arose only once, in 1929, when the Rights, represented by TOMSKY, proposed to us through ZINOVIEV and FEDOROV that we form a bloc against the Central Committee majority. In making this proposal, TOMSKY said, “If you still want to preserve yourselves for political life, you must support us; we Rights have decided to begin an open struggle against STALIN and the Central Committee, since we cannot go on living this way.” ZINOVIEV and FEDOROV did not answer TOMSKY then; they said they would convey the proposal to their comrades. In 1929 this question was indeed discussed at a meeting of the central group.

CHAIRMAN: And later, if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were in power, things would be different?

BAKAEV: It was not said that everything would be completely different, but that existing difficulties would be greatly reduced.

CHAIRMAN: We must clarify how you brought up your fellow-thinkers in a spirit of malice and hostility toward Comrade STALIN. You repeatedly stated that everything stemmed from ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV having been removed from leadership and that “we want to put the leaders of our group in charge.” “If ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were in power, things would be different.”

BAKAEV: There was no force whatsoever.

CHAIRMAN: That is a different matter?

BAKAEV: There were such conversations.

CHAIRMAN: Were terrorist sentiments being cultivated or not?

BAKAEV: There were anti-party and essentially counterrevolutionary conversations intended to discredit the existing party leadership. Perhaps it was said that things would be different if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were brought into the party leadership. That was said too, but nothing was done to implement that last proposition, because we did not conduct factional work.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: You maintained contact with all your fellow-thinkers in Moscow and Leningrad; you met, spoke in a spirit of hatred, and brought up others in the same spirit.

BAKAEV: Not with all, but with a number of our fellow-thinkers.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Did you seek to preserve the cadres among your supporters?

BAKAEV: The policy of preserving cadres, and the preservation of cadres itself, relates to 1928–1929. If permitted, I would like to dwell on that question in some detail.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Until 1934, you and some of your fellow-thinkers were concerned with maintaining contacts among former members of the Zinovievite opposition, wherever they happened to be—Moscow, Sverdlovsk, Tashkent, and so on. You spread rumors, gossip, and provocative information not merely in 1928–1929 but right up to 1934.

Your testimony contains the reply, “We fed our fellow-thinkers with anti-party, counterrevolutionary information about the Comintern and the country.” You do not deny this?

BAKAEV: No.

CHAIRMAN: “We systematically exerted influence on our cadres, educating them in a spirit of malice and hostility toward the leadership, particularly toward Comrade STALIN. We repeatedly discussed how the worsening of difficulties in the party and Comintern largely resulted from ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV having been removed from leadership, that everything depended on the return of our leaders and consequently ourselves to party leadership.” You gave this testimony quite clearly, logically, and coherently. Do you confirm that formulation?

BAKAEV: I confirm that formulation.

CHAIRMAN: Further, your testimony says that you educated your fellow-thinkers to expect the failure of the party leadership and the subsequent strengthening of your organization, keeping track of where your supporters were located. Do you confirm this?

BAKAEV: That refers to the earlier period.

CHAIRMAN: Your testimony states, “We showed great interest in what was happening inside the Leningrad par\<ty\> organization and in Leningrad’s working-class districts.” Do you deny this too?

BAKAEV: No, I do not deny it.

CHAIRMAN: In that connection, explain in detail how contacts between Moscow, Leningrad, and other cities were maintained. Who maintained them?

BAKAEV: I know that among former oppositionists working or formerly employed in Leningrad, the following retained ties with the Zinovievite group until recently: Vladimir LEVIN, ANISHEV, SOLOVIEV, Mitya SHIRYAEV, and Vladimir RUMYANTSEV. I believe that is everyone.

CHAIRMAN: And MANDELSTAM?

BAKAEV: He rarely came to Moscow.

CHAIRMAN: He rarely came, but you traveled there from Moscow?

BAKAEV: I would not say that. I know from LEVIN that he sometimes met MANDELSTAM, whom I myself met once at HERTIK’s during all the years I lived in Moscow. That was several years ago.

CHAIRMAN: Can one say that the ties between your fellow-thinkers \<in\> Leningrad and Moscow were maintained through trips by your people?

BAKAEV: There were LEVIN, ANISHEV, SOLOVIEV, SHIRYAEV, and until 1932 Vladimir RUMYANTSEV.

CHAIRMAN: Who from the Moscow Center came to Leningrad to convey information or instructions? Did you come several times?

BAKAEV: I came several times.

CHAIRMAN: Did KUKLIN come?

BAKAEV: I went there, but I had no meetings.

CHAIRMAN: Who else came from the Moscow Center?

BAKAEV: KUKLIN came, Volodya SOLOVIEV; HERTIK came for the last time in 1933–1934 and met LEVIN. I know this from HERTIK.

CHAIRMAN: Did GESSEN come?

BAKAEV: Recently. EVDOKIMOV, however, usually came to Leningrad for the May Day celebrations every year.

CHAIRMAN: So the ties ran in both directions. But did the Moscow Center maintain connections with fellow-thinkers \<in\> other cities besides Leningrad?

BAKAEV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: Through whom were these ties maintained? Who were your representatives?

BAKAEV: People arriving in Moscow from other cities usually visited us. Three former oppositionists lived in Rostov: Pyotr ROTSKAN \[19\], Nikolai GORDON, and Timofei DMITRIEV. Each visited Moscow, seeing ZINOVIEV and other comrades. ROTSKAN visited me; GORDON regularly visited HERTIK, as did Timofei DMITRIEV.

CHAIRMAN: You know three names from Rostov?

BAKAEV: Yes, all old Petersburg men.

CHAIRMAN: Who do you know in Sverdlovsk?

BAKAEV: In Sverdlovsk I know ELKOVICH, also a Petersburg man, who lived there for several years and traveled to Moscow. He usually stayed with GORSHENIN and almost always visited ZINOVIEV.

CHAIRMAN: Through GORSHENIN?

BAKAEV: No, ZINOVIEV and ELKOVICH were old acquaintances; ELKOVICH could visit ZINOVIEV on his own.

CHAIRMAN: Who else do you know in other cities among your fellow-thinkers who maintained contacts until recently?

BAKAEV: Olga RAVICH lived in Voronezh. She and ZINOVIEV were old friends. When she came to Moscow, she usually visited him. I hesitate to say anything else about other cities now.

CHAIRMAN: You also began to mention Arkhangelsk.

BAKAEV: SAKHOV was there. He went there but had not yet returned; at least I have not seen him since.

CHAIRMAN: Who else was in Arkhangelsk besides SAKHOV?

BAKAEV: Besides SAKHOV, ROZNER was there. Until 1929 or 1930, that is, until his departure, he firmly shared our common attitudes. I have not seen him in several years and do not know how he stands now.

CHAIRMAN: Do you recall no one else from other cities?

BAKAEV: In Novosibirsk, in 1929–1930, there was PEKAR-ORLOV \[20\], and several others, including ZELIKSON. In Smolensk, Sergey GESSEN.

CHAIRMAN: Can one conclude from this that all these people systematically traveled to Moscow, received information of one kind or another, and, in other words, maintained systematic ties with the Moscow Center?

BAKAEV: One cannot say that the connection was systematic, but it did exist; it existed throughout.

CHAIRMAN: In Moscow, apart from the leaders who belonged to the Center, who else do you know? People who had not broken with you up to the most recent period?

BAKAEV: My wife Anna KOSTINA; Boris BRAVO; Aleksei PERIMOV; Aleksandr HERTSBERG and his wife POZDEEVA. I believe those were all who maintained regular contacts until recently.

CHAIRMAN: Tell us how you assess that systematic campaign, or rather persecution, which you and your comrades conducted against our party leadership. Could all those malicious attacks, provocative rumors, and gossip, taken together, have contributed to the emergence of terrorist sentiments? Do you consider the two connected?

BAKAEV: I do not know all the circumstances of KIROV’s murder, but I think that everything that was done in our circles and spread like an infection to the periphery could not fail to affect the attitudes of unstable elements in our organization; it could not fail to push them toward the most extreme, wholly inadmissible terrorist forms of struggle against the party leadership.

CHAIRMAN: But do you consider that your entire group, the whole organization, must bear some responsibility for this tragic affair?

BAKAEV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: In your testimony of January 12, you expressed this idea much more clearly. The investigator asked, “The murder of KIROV committed by NIKOLAEV, a former opposition member: what do you know about it?”

You replied, “I have no connection with this vile act, but I believe that our entire organization bears grave moral and political responsibility for it.”

That is how you expressed yourself on January 12, just two days ago.

BAKAEV: I have never disclaimed that responsibility.

CHAIRMAN: You do not disclaim it?

BAKAEV: No.

CHAIRMAN: What can you add to your testimony?

BAKAEV: Nothing for the moment.

CHAIRMAN: Tell us, BAKAEV, did you listen to EVDOKIMOV’s testimony, take it in, understand it?

BAKAEV: Yes, yes.

CHAIRMAN: What is your opinion: do you regard EVDOKIMOV’s characterization of the activities of your counterrevolutionary organization as correct?

BAKAEV: Objectively, it is so.

CHAIRMAN: What do you mean “objectively”? Tell us, is it correct in substance?

BAKAEV: In substance, I do not think that any of those present set themselves the task of aggravating…

CHAIRMAN: Be brief. Do you consider EVDOKIMOV’s assessment correct?

BAKAEV: Objectively, it is correct.

CHAIRMAN: Why, then, does your testimony lack such clarity? You are trying to evade the question. You say you belonged to the Center only until 1932. EVDOKIMOV says in his testimony that you too belonged to the Center that existed until recently. Now you tried to say that you were a member only until 1932.

BAKAEV: I did not manage to get it across.

CHAIRMAN: What did you not manage?

BAKAEV: I seriously intended to withdraw from the circle of people with whom I had lived for many years.

CHAIRMAN: But you did not withdraw and continued to share the same attitudes as before?

BAKAEV: I think only the degree was different; the essence did not change.

CHAIRMAN: Can it be considered that you belonged to the “Moscow Center” until recently?

BAKAEV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: Do you confirm that although you did not meet at ZINOVIEV’s home, you met and spoke with ZINOVIEV?

BAKAEV: In 1933 I met ZINOVIEV in Kislovodsk at the “10th \<Anniversary\> of October” sanatorium. He was taking narzan mineral-water baths there, but we had no conversations except about resort matters. Later, in 1933, I met him in Moscow, but again we had no conversations.

CHAIRMAN: Did you meet KUKLIN and the others?

BAKAEV: I met chiefly with GORSHENIN and also with others, but political conversations were very rare.

CHAIRMAN: The Moscow Center \{existed\} \*comprised\* not just ZINOVIEV but ten people?

BAKAEV: When I visited ZINOVIEV, I could tell you everything that was happening in the Moscow Center. Now I cannot.

CHAIRMAN: Did you travel to Leningrad in 1933–1934?

BAKAEV: I was there in 1934.

CHAIRMAN: Whom did you visit among the Leningrad people in 1934?

BAKAEV: I came to Leningrad on business.

CHAIRMAN: And in between business, whom did you visit?

BAKAEV: I saw SOLOVIEV.

CHAIRMAN: Who visited you in Moscow in 1934?

BAKAEV: I cannot remember for 1933–1934.

CHAIRMAN: What was ZINOVIEV’s attitude toward particular questions of foreign and domestic policy?

BAKAEV: I observed especially strong dissatisfaction on ZINOVIEV\<A\>’s part with Central Committee policy in connection with the major excesses and difficulties of collectivization. Then, during complications in the Donbass several years ago, there were problems in living conditions and miners’ productivity fell considerably; at that time ZINOVIEV was especially bitter toward the party leadership. He interpreted declining output and labor productivity as one of the strongest forms of protest by a considerable part of the working class against the overall leadership. But ZINOVIEV also expressed different attitudes when some of the difficulties of collectivization were overcome.

ZINOVIEV said that without STALIN we would not have overcome those difficulties. Those excesses and missteps had cost the party and our country dearly. I must say quite frankly that ZINOVIEV’s prevailing attitude toward the overall party leadership was negative.

CHAIRMAN: What do you wish to add to EVDOKIMOV’s testimony?

BAKAEV: One could conclude from EVDOKIMOV’s speech that we were betting on difficulties. I disagree on that point. I cannot see how these difficulties could have secured the return or inclusion of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and others in the party leadership. I agree with the rest.

MATULEVICH: We have testimony that ZINOVIEV sowed doubts about the material condition of the working people as a result of implementation of the Five-Year Plan.

BAKAEV: I reported that at the outset, quoting GORSHENIN, who had had a particular conversation with ZINOVIEV. I knew that ZINOVIEV did not believe in the Five-Year Plan and considered it impossible to fulfill.

MATULEVICH: Did he say this to his closest friends and comrades?

BAKAEV: He told GORSHENIN, GORSHENIN told me, and I passed it on.

CHAIRMAN: Do you wish to add anything?

BAKAEV: No.

EXAMINATION

## OF THE WITNESS SAFAROV, GEORGY IVANOVICH .

CHAIRMAN: The court decides to summon Georgy SAFAROV as a witness in this case and to question him immediately. ( SAFAROV enters )

CHAIRMAN: You are a witness in this case, in which ZINOVIEV, BAKAEV, KAMENEV and others, nineteen persons in all, stand accused. Answer one fundamental question: what do you know about the counterrevolutionary activity of the organization led by the Moscow Center, consisting of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, BAKAEV and others, and what part did ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV and others play in that activity?

SAFAROV: In recent years?

CHAIRMAN: Yes, in 1932–33 and particularly in 1934.

SAFAROV: In connection with the temporary difficulties of 1932, anti-Party and anti-Soviet groupings attempted to work out and reconcile certain views so as to forge a counterrevolutionary bloc. Having found myself at the bottom of this pit of internal emigration, I took part in those negotiations, meetings, and preparations to forge the bloc. Already in 1931, when MADYAR visited me in the Comintern together with RUMYANTSEV, RUMYANTSEV raised the question of why I had not restored my relations with ZINOVIEV. RUMYANTSEV was a friend of former Komsomol officials who subsequently became fascists, and belonged to that leaderless group to which I too belonged after rejoining the Party in mid-1928. RUMYANTSEV and this group in Leningrad began drawing increasingly close to the Zinovievites; connections arose first of all among Vladimir LEVIN, MANDELSTAM, RUMYANTSEV and others.

In 1932, around spring or summer, MRACHKOVSKY, a Trotskyist with whom I had maintained personal relations for many years, arrived in Moscow. Our meeting, and a subsequent meeting with MRACHKOVSKY and another Trotskyist, Ivan Nikitich SMIRNOV, were devoted to discussing the situation in the Party and the country.

In my conversations with MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV, I advanced the notion that we faced the threat of a rupture between the working class and the peasantry, and that the Party leadership had no line. I defended these counterrevolutionary views, suggesting to MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV—and later discussing it with ZINOVIEV as well—that we prepare a platform-like appeal to the Central Committee signed by some twenty longstanding Party members.

MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV were skeptical of that proposal. They were also skeptical when I said it was necessary to acknowledge that the counterrevolutionary Trotskyist-Zinovievite bloc had been mistaken regarding the building of socialism in one country and the question of Thermidor. Nevertheless, they seized on this conversation with me: MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV categorically insisted that I meet ZINOVIEV as soon as possible, saying even then that, whatever the form of the initiative, we had to assemble a bloc of Zinovievites with the Trotskyist group—SMIRNOV, MRACHKOVSKY, LOMINADZE.

As for the Rightists, their outward attitude was somewhat negative. In any event, these were the three groups they named first. Since I met MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV more than once, on every occasion the conversation turned on the point that “you must renew your relations with them”; MRACHKOVSKY said explicitly that matters could not be handled that way.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: What matter?

SAFAROV: Forging the counterrevolutionary bloc and the question of the platform’s content. I then met SMILGA at about the same time in his apartment. SMILGA advanced views of interest to the court, since they directly resembled those later expressed by ZINOVIEV in July 1932. SMILGA said: “Clearly there is inflation in the system of government measures; regulation of the national economy will soon break down; a critical moment is approaching.” In response to my proposal about returning, SMILGA said: “You must take account of the situation that exists—on one hand in the working class and Party, on the other in the Central Committee.” He posed the question by saying that, as a result of implementing the Five-Year Plan, enormous masses of peasants—“muzhiks,” as he called them—people of petty-bourgeois origin, had entered the factories and affected the Party; therefore anti-Party and anti-Soviet groups had no foothold either among the workers or in the Party. He expounded the theory of the degeneration of the working class, which I later heard from ZINOVIEV when we met at Ilyinskoye in 1932.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: From ZINOVIEV? How much later?

SAFAROV: About a month or a month and a half later... SMILGA said: “I know you have a very uneven temperament; do not rush.” He said that there were two groups in the Central Committee, and that hostility prevailed between them: “Wait until they fall upon one another.”

In the same connection he stated: “I see KAMENEV. A man like him, now that the economic situation borders on catastrophe, does not lead” \[21\], and again insisted most emphatically that I reestablish my relations with ZINOVIEV. To continue: just before the meeting, MADYAR, ZINOVIEV’s agent in the Comintern, came to me and said: “ZINOVIEV wishes to speak with you.” He gave the exact time and place: half past three in the afternoon, at the dacha in Ilyinskoye. This was approximately between July 10 and 15. I categorically maintain it was in July, around those dates, because I was leaving on vacation on July 15 and had previously spent a month and a half in military retraining; I therefore arrived at ZINOVIEV’s place in military uniform.

When I arrived, we immediately started talking. We went into the garden. First a few words about the Comintern situation, which I tried to portray in an anti-Party light, but he said: “I already know that from MADYAR.”

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Did he receive information?

SAFAROV: Yes. MADYAR told ZINOVIEV about Comintern affairs; there were dozens of such reports from MADYAR.

Taking the individual points of this conversation, it went as follows: ZINOVIEV interrupted me when I was speaking about Comintern affairs and moved on to the position of the Leningrad workers. He said that the situation was appalling, the food situation appalling, and conditions generally appalling; that workers could not reach the leading representatives of the Leningrad organizations; he uttered malicious slander about KIROV, citing KUKLIN’s impressions and later reports from the EMELYANOVS that veteran Leningrad workers had been driven into a corner, no longer felt at home in the factories, and that any “snot-nosed Komsomol member,” as he put it, or appointed secretary could dress down a veteran worker who could not open his mouth. He then depicted the old proletarian cadres as pushed away from leadership and influence not only within Party life but in the broader life of the working class. He developed at length SMILGA’s same theory: the peasantization of the working class. Masses had flooded construction sites, peasant masses had entered the factories; the old cadres were suppressed by bureaucratic management, and this petty-bourgeois mass made it possible to do anything without heed to the views of workers and ordinary Party members.

ZINOVIEV developed this theory very seriously. It was only natural, he argued, that we and our groups had no influence in the working class, because the working class had degenerated. This theory of the degeneration of the working class was promoted quite strongly and widely by the Zinovievite organization, and not only among the Zinovievites.

In the same connection ZINOVIEV spoke of the role of young people in the factories. He said that alongside the peasants there came young people and adolescents—boys and girls—who were bought off with promises of all sorts of opportunities for rapid advancement in life, and so on.

On relations between the working class and the peasantry, ZINOVIEV said the following: nothing remained of Lenin’s cooperative plan. The leadership had abandoned that plan. Remember, LENIN spoke of ten to twenty years. What LENIN wanted to do in ten or twenty years, the leadership headed by STALIN was doing in two. Hence the rupture between the working class and the peasantry, hence all the difficulties, hence the widening crisis in the country.

If we sum up their assessment of the working class, it is perfectly clear that among the Trotskyists—MRACHKOVSKY \[22\], SMIRNOV—as well as among ZINOVIEV’s followers, even at that time no positive aspect was emphasized; essentially everything came down to saying that the Central Committee had upset something here, was rushing too fast there, had suffered a breakdown elsewhere, and so forth.

In reality, the counterrevolutionary bloc being forged in 1932 expressed the sentiments and tendencies of bourgeois reaction against socialism. I say this to stress how this utter lack of ideas, this absence of any independent thought among these people, directed all their thinking against the Party leadership and the Central Committee. Of course ZINOVIEV and the Zinovievite organization discussed these matters most of all, year after year, not only in 1932 but earlier, fabricating reports of disagreements with the Central Committee and predictions that everything would collapse any day now, that a few more difficulties would bring their own time.

In particular, that was chiefly what the work consisted of; this was the basis upon which the counterrevolutionary activity developed.

When I outlined my proposal for an appeal to the Central Committee to ZINOVIEV, he replied that these were times when “everything” was in the air. He said I was in a hurry, as always. He said that we were now in the trenches, and used this vivid phrase—we were sitting in the garden on a felled tree stump: “SAFAROV, we who have known one another for many years have had to sit on a cut stump in this garden, yet you are in a hurry.”

Next, the question of the platform. ZINOVIEV said: “You know, SAFAROV, a great deal in the old platform is wrong. Much of what was written there can now be abandoned.”

I said that the old platform had been wrong about building socialism in one country; he replied literally: “Well, building socialism in one country—but who are ‘they’?”

On the question of Thermidor: “Whom does this bureaucracy represent? It stands outside the classes.”

ZINOVIEV then developed the central idea of the Zinovievite counterrevolutionary group: that this leadership would not withstand any serious complication, domestic or international, and would fall apart. He repeated what SMILGA, who was in touch with KAMENEV, had told me earlier. In this sphere, all sorts of gossip circulated about struggles within the Central Committee and the existence of various factions. These rumors were spread chiefly by the Zinovievite organization, passed along from person to person, with an agent as skilled and characteristic as MADYAR, who truly broke all records, and these conversations could be relayed at once.

People said that the Central Committee would collapse any day now; yet if a Party member appeared ten minutes—or seconds—later, the very same person would start singing praises to STALIN’s leadership. MADYAR was one of ZINOVIEV’s principal informants. Information flowed quite regularly, because every Zinovievite from outside Moscow considered it his duty to visit EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, or someone less important to obtain every detail and report about the leadership. All this so-called information, all this counterrevolutionary gossip, had a malicious character, one might say an incitement against the Party Central Committee—against STALIN, KIROV and others. This malicious, systematic incitement amounted to the claim that the leadership had to be removed. We had no forces at present, because the organization had no authority either in the Party or in the working class. It was a form of internal counterrevolutionary emigration that believed everything was headed downhill because it was not in power and that all socialist construction consisted of excesses; thus sentiments were gathered and condensed. In my confrontation with ZINOVIEV I already quoted a thoroughly fascist phrase he told me, first uttered by KAMENEV in 1926. ZINOVIEV repeated the phrase at a meeting of the Zinovievite bloc’s center as an apt expression; I had heard it repeatedly in 1926–27 and again in 1932: “STALIN can pour poison into your ear without your even noticing it.” He went on: “But who is in charge? You speak of appealing to the Central Committee—to whom? Who leads Leningrad? KIROV and CHUDOV. Workers cannot reach KIROV,” adding: “KIROV spends more of his time hunting.” He continued: “Lev Borisovich met TOMSKY recently, who goes to the Central Committee, and said it was a pitiful sight, sheer poverty.”

At this point he mentioned the merits of Pyotr ZALUTSKY. Even ZALUTSKY could not hold back and produced a counterrevolutionary pamphlet against the late KIROV. ZALUTSKY also reprinted it. It was a system of inculcating sharply fascistic hostility toward the leadership and its individual representatives. This went on constantly in ZINOVIEV’s circle, and everyone sitting here knew perfectly well that we could always compose a Central Committee and have it approved. ZINOVIEV went on to say—and I categorically insist that this was in July 1934, not September—that I ought to meet Lev Borisovich and acquaint myself with the draft of STEN’s platform, which he had, and give my views about it.

I did not meet KAMENEV because I left shortly afterwards, but I categorically maintain that this was how things happened and that I had this conversation with ZINOVIEV. I began telling him, in connection with his remark that a platform was in the air, about the writing of such a platform.

I began describing the situation and attitudes of various figures in the anti-Party groups. I told him of MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV: that they were prepared to fight, willing to draw closer to the Zinovievites, and regarded such a combination, that is, a rapprochement between Trotskyists and Zinovievites, as likely and desirable. MRACHKOVSKY had proposed that I reestablish relations with the Zinovievites, and I conveyed this to ZINOVIEV.

ZINOVIEV said: “That is very good; these are people we should deal with, we must come to an agreement, and I think there will be no great difficulties.” I then told him of SMILGA and my conversation with him, but this made little impression since SMILGA met KAMENEV more often than he met me. I conveyed the sentiments of LOMINADZE’s group. Again, the discussion arose over the question of a platform. MADYAR also served as a link between ZINOVIEV and LOMINADZE’s group. I said LOMINADZE had mentioned they were sketching something like a platform. Someone reported this to LOMINADZE, who came rushing to me in alarm and declared there had been no platforms. But regarding the grouping he expressed perfectly clear views.

This finest Stalinist youth, it was said, was fiercely opposed to the leadership. SHLYAPNIKOV was discussed; when asked “what are your platform views?” he replied: “Our platform is the old one.” I passed this on to ZINOVIEV. He said: “Well, that is nonsense, of course, but he is an old, thoroughgoing Bolshevik,” and so on.... Generally, regarding the forging of the bloc, ZINOVIEV said: “With LOMINADZE—certainly yes; with the Trotskyists—certainly yes; agreement is necessary and possible.” He gave me very definite instructions: “You must see EVDOKIMOV, MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV; it is very important to keep in contact. You have Leningrad contacts” (meaning SHATSKY’s group and others). “We shall meet more often.”

... And then, sitting in a railway carriage, I came across a very small company consisting of VAGANYAN\<A\> \[23\], SMIRNOV and MRACHKOVSKY. Since MRACHKOVSKY had met me many times without mentioning it, and had pretended that he was not directly linked with ZINOVIEV, I asked in surprise; he tried to smooth the matter over. But it was perfectly clear that he had a direct and immediate connection with the Zinovievite group \[24\].

If we examine the nature of these links and relations, first and foremost \<th\>ey rested on personal ties, on the transfer of everyday social relations, often fostered by drinking bouts and other indecencies. There was a chain of trusted personal friends, secured by mutual responsibility. This chain and these contacts worked without fail. For instance, the day after my conversation with ZINOVIEV (there were only the two of us, and nobody else heard it), I visited HERTZBERG, who immediately greeted me: “Have you made peace with Grigory?\! Congratulations\!”

Indeed one could cite many examples of this chain’s unfailing operation. I have already mentioned the anti-Party views on Comintern affairs. I said: “... They will be smashed and then rush in the opposite direction and float back toward us.” A few days later in 1932, when I visited KOTOLYNOV, I heard precisely the same formulation.

Another example. After the arrest of Ryutin’s group, KNORIN briefed a restricted circle of Party acti\<vists\>. I passed this to EVDOKIMOV. The next day I went somewhere and there they repeated to me exactly what I had said. I could cite many examples of this kind showing that the organization was kept informed and was systematically incited against the Comintern leadership as well. Under the conditions of clandestine work, the organization operated on a fairly extensive basis and on a considerable scale. Naturally this information and the entire system of work were based on the same principle as preparations for the Fourteenth Congress (the comparison is, of course, very relative): at first people spoke in quarter-voices, then in half-voices, and finally aloud.

The existence of this incitement against the Party leadership, the suggestion that the proletariat had degenerated, that there was bureaucracy and other thi\<ngs\>, inevitably nourished terrorist sentiments; they were the direct prerequisite and, one might say, the casing of the bullet that struck KIROV. In this respect one can cite facts and dozens of cases in which people systematically repeated the same accounts and ideas by word of mouth. This went on continually. For example, I met Nikolai DMITRIEV in 1932; I was coming from Artyom’s \[25\], was going to EVDOKIMOV’s and then to Sverdlovsk. I met him at NAUMOV’s. SAKHOV described his trips to Leningrad; LEONOV likewise. My comparison with what had happened at the Fourteenth Congress was not accidental: ZINOVIEV and the Zinovievites were so steeped in duplicity that they often formulated matters so they could be understood one way or the opposite way. When it came to forging the bloc, however, they spoke more openly. This was the form that culminated in the murder of Comrade KIROV. All the activity up to the last moment prepared this outcome. It was prepared by the Zinovievite organization and the various anti-Party groups I have mentioned. The system spread everywhere, and so as not to prolong my testimony, I ask to be questioned.

CHAIRMAN: Tell us about the \{Oc\}\*Sep\*tember meeting with ZINOVIEV.

SAFAROV: The \{Oc\}\*Sep\*tember meeting with ZINOVIEV took place after I returned from Kazakhstan, where I had stayed until September 1. I suppose ZINOVIEV had already received complete information about Kazakhstan from SAKHOV.

CHAIRMAN: What was ZINOVIEV particularly interested in?

SAFAROV: Difficulties, famine. As I have already testified, he put it this way: well, industrialization! They think agriculture, livestock raising, and several branches of industry have risen; they call that industrialization.

Then he turned to the Comintern. On this subject he spoke at greater length about united-front tactics. “Now, when a rapid offensive is taking place with seizures of power, the Party, its leadership, the Central Committee of the AUCP(b), do nothing to organize this struggle; on the contrary, they do everything to paralyze it. The Party is not even prepared to go underground; in Germany, as he said, we face the inevitable defeat of the Party as a result of the existing policy.”

CHAIRMAN: In your testimony of January 11 there is this phrase: “ZINOVIEV asked me whether I knew or had in mind anyone in foreign parties who had an ‘independent opinion,’ of course in the sense of opposing the line of the AUCP(b) and the Comintern.”

SAFAROV: The question was whether there were people who could independently speak out against the Central Committee of the AUCP(b). I told him I knew of no such people. He said MADYAR should be consulted.

CHAIRMAN: During the September meeting did ZINOVIEV speak with MRACHKOVSKY and SMILGA?

SAFAROV: Since a certain secrecy surrounded relations with MRACHKOVSKY and SMILGA, he insisted that the talks continue.

CHAIRMAN: Talks through your mediation?

SAFAROV: Yes. He emphasized that the situation was acute as regards the threat of war. In that connection he repeated his old view that this leadership would not withstand a serious international complication. It was therefore difficult to assess the situation then. He said that, were it not for the existing circumstances, agreement would be easier, but now nothing was working out. I must also address what prevented this bloc or platform from being finally formalized. I think the answer is not difficult: it was the smashing of Sten’s and Ryutin’s group. This immediately induced the counterrevolutionary groups that had reached an understanding to decide to part company.

After that arrest, as I have said, I visited EVDOKIMOV and asked: “So once again it all amounted to a squeak?” referring to an intervention before the Central Control Commission.

EVDOKIMOV said: “Well, what do you expect? He (ZINOVIEV) said he had been pushed aside”; I am sure this must be understood as an opposition hint. I said: again, it was only a squeak; are we going to settle on Ryutin’s kulak platform? He replied that the matter was postponed. I saw EVDOKIMOV at the Twenty-seventh House of Soviets. Earlier I had met SMIRNOV (MRACHKOVSKY was absent); I categorically insist that this was before the failure of the Sten–Ryutin platform. I visited SMIRNOV and, when I repeated my remark about the kulak platform and so on, he replied: “Well, times are now more or less clear: each collective farm must decide for itself.”

CHAIRMAN: By the word “collective farm,” did you mean your own group?

SAFAROV: Yes, our organization: each collective farm was to decide for itself. I said that, after all, we had held a general discussion and seemed to have agreed upon common views, from which conclusions ought to follow. But I asked: if anyone asks me how things stand, should I say RYUTIN and the others are victims of the present regime, or is everyone to answer as he wishes?

CHAIRMAN: When did you last meet ZINOVIEV?

SAFAROV: In September 1932, but I knew ZINOVIEV’s views.

CHAIRMAN: You did not meet again?

SAFAROV: No.

CHAIRMAN: Nor with KAMENEV?

SAFAROV: No.

CHAIRMAN: You met your associates who are now in the dock. Perhaps you could describe the defendants? For instance, what do you know about GERTIK and his functions?

SAFAROV: It is very late now; I do not consider him my associate. But as regards GERTIK, the question seems perfectly clear to me. GERTIK was one of the central organizers of that group. From around the time of the Fifteenth Congress, GERTIK’s apartment constantly served as the headquarters for all kinds of meetings of Zinovievites in Moscow, and so on. This was so obvious that, to avoid the slightest suspicion that I had adopted a counterrevolutionary position, I avoided meeting GERTIK, knowing how far this radio network reached and knowing that the day after my conversation with ZINOVIEV the entire Zinovievite organization had learned every detail of it.

CHAIRMAN: In short, communications were well organized?

SAFAROV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: How would you describe SHAROV’s participation in the organization, his functions, and his personal qualities?

SAFAROV: I met SHAROV only rarely at the Kremlin hospital, but apart from those meetings I knew that SHAROV was one of those people who see the entire world through their leader’s eyes. Secondly, SHAROV was a close friend and associate of people occupying central positions—particularly BAKAEV. Their friendship went back many years. He had contacts in Leningrad from the time he was a leader there; he maintained those contacts and traveled to Leningrad fairly often.

CHAIRMAN: Did he travel there himself?

SAFAROV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: What do you know about SAKHOV?

SAFAROV: I met SAKHOV in 1932 on Vozdvizhenka Street. He told me he had defended, before the Oblast\<level\> Control Commission, ABRAMSON, a member of the Trotskyist-Zinovievite bloc involved in a sordid affair who had seized an apartment through administrative pressure \[26\]. He said they did not know SAKHOV had acted as a former oppositionist \[27\] and did not suspect his connection with the organization. He added: “How could one fail to help one of our own?” At the Oblast\<level\> Control Commission he helped not only ABRAMSON. He asked me “which of the boys I had been seeing.”

CHAIRMAN: The “boys” among your associates?

SAFAROV: We shared the same circle of acquaintances, so from SAKHOV’s words I could tell who belonged to the organization. Others also told me of SAKHOV’s trips to Leningrad.

CHAIRMAN: Do the defendants have any questions?

SAKHOV: I do. ( Addressing SAFAROV ) You say that I was in Kazakhstan. Are you assuming I informed ZINOVIEV solely because I was in Kazakhstan, or do you have further evidence?

SAFAROV: The fact is that your organization suffered above all from the use of stock formulas. You conveyed certain things in such a stereotyped form that one could not mistake them. Naturally, knowing all this and bearing in mind that in this matter in Kazakhstan there was...

SAKHOV: In other words, since SAKHOV was in Kazakhstan, the information must have come from him?

SAFAROV: No. Since it involved judicial and prosecutorial matters, the information must have come from you.

SAKHOV: I wish to make a statement about my journey to Kazakhstan and the conclusions SAFAROV has just drawn. He said he learned of this in 1932 at a meeting with ZINOVIEV. I inform the court that I was in Kazakhstan in March 1933 and had never been there before. If there is any doubt, I request that the relevant records be obtained. That is my first point. Therefore SAFAROV is not telling the truth.

Second, SAFAROV stated here that in ABRAMSON\<A\>’s case I acted so that no one knew who I was. In this connection I ask the court to call as a witness Comrade KODATSKY, a member of the Party Central Committee, whom I visited and with whom I discussed this case.

This statement of SAFAROV’s is not merely unfounded; it is false. I believe KODATSKY will confirm that.

CHAIRMAN: The file contains testimony from ABRAMSON.

SAKHOV: I do not know ABRAMSON’s testimony, but for me KODATSKY’s testimony would carry much greater authority, since he is a member of the Central Committee.

SAFAROV: But since the matter involved the Procuracy, I was certain that judicial information of that kind (I have known the composition of the Zinovievite organization for many years) could only have come from SAKHOV.

As for ABRAMSON, things happened as follows: GRIBOV handled the case in the Oblast\<level\> Control Commission ( addresses SAKHOV ); you said that “those present at the meeting of the Oblast\<level\> Control Commission did not know I was an oppositionist”; you yourself boasted of that.

SAKHOV: I want the court to know that even this statement by SAFAROV plainly contradicts his earlier ones. He says contradictory things, since GRIBOV knew perfectly well that I was an oppositionist.

CHAIRMAN: Who else has questions?

EVDOKIMOV: Allow me\! The question I want to ask SAFAROV is not very important to the factual side of the case, since the investigation knows that in 1932 the Moscow Zinovievite Center took steps to establish connections with various organized anti-Party groupings. But it is important to me, because what I told the investigators differs from what SAFAROV is saying now. During the preliminary investigation I stated that at my meeting with MRACHKOVSKY and SMIRNOV we held no anti-Party political conversations (apart from their crit\<ical\> attitude toward the railway project), and I explained to the investigators that there had been no such talks. There is a discrepancy here: SAFAROV says this occurred after he had already met ZINOVIEV, whereas he met ZINOVIEV only after seeing me. At SAFAROV’s request I asked ZINOVIEV whether he wanted to meet SAFAROV, so this was later. There is some confusion, SAFAROV; you have mixed things up.

CHAIRMAN: When did the meeting in the railway carriage take place?

EVDOKIMOV: In my opinion, in 1932. In any event, before my meeting with SAFAROV in the summer of 1932. Otherwise it is impossible to explain why we did not have that conversation.

This question is not fundamental to the character of the activity of our underground counterrevolutionary organization in Moscow. The investigation knows all our attempts to establish ties with anti-Party groups, but it matters to me.

One small detail: SAFAROV says that when he got there he found a certain group; as I remember it, I found SAFAROV there. Otherwise it would be impossible to explain why we had no anti-Party discussions; I attribute it to SAFAROV’s presence, since I did not know his views.

CHAIRMAN: Who was there?

EVDOKIMOV: SAFAROV, MRACHKOVSKY, SMIRNOV, and a woman who apparently was close to MRACHKOVSKY and well known to SAFAROV and SMIRNOV. I do not know her. Toward the end of my visit, TER\<-VAGANYAN\> arrived.

SAFAROV: When MRACHKOVSKY left by train—that was my last meeting with him, after all our conversations—he simply said: “Shura SAFONOVA is going to travel; if you need to send anything, she can carry it.” This very fact shows that EVDOKIMOV is completely mistaken. He says Shura SAFONOVA said she would travel some weeks later.

CHAIRMAN: That is immaterial.

SAFAROV: I recall a detail that might refresh EVDOKIMOV’s memory. EVDOKIMOV arrived with Ivan Nikitich SMIRNOV, and I was very surprised that neither Ivan Nikitich nor EVDOKIMOV had told me this.

EVDOKIMOV: I know that this detail bears significantly on my testimony. In fact, there were no political discussions there. I passed the statement to Ivan Nikitich and said that SAFAROV’s presence prevented the talks. How could that be, if I had already met SAFAROV?

SAFAROV: The information was of such a kind that someone was leaving in half an hour, and they refrained from discussing it in the presence of Shura SAFONOVA and oth\<ers\>, considering such talks in their presence unnecessary.

CHAIRMAN: Any questions?

EVDOKIMOV: I testified to this during the investigation, and I have testified to the same here.

SAFAROV: I assure you he has forgotten that the first meeting was arranged by MADYAR.

CHAIRMAN: Who else has questions?

SHAROV: I should like him to recall one of the trips to Leningrad.

SAFAROV: It is hard to say exactly, but there were trips. I was told that RUMYANTSEV traveled in 1931 and KOTOLYNOV in 1932.

SHAROV: I have not seen RUMYANTSEV or KOTOLYNOV since the Fifteenth Congress.

CHAIRMAN: But you did travel to Leningrad?

SHAROV: Yes, I did.

CHAIRMAN: Who else has questions?

KAMENEV: He said SMILGA told him that he had met me in 1932 and drew a political conclusion from it. I ask him to say whether I had political discussions with SMILGA at that time and whether he knows their content.

SAFAROV: SMILGA expressed his views on the economic situation: that everything would go to hell, that the entire system of state regulation was bad; this coincided completely with KAMENEV’s views.

CHAIRMAN: You are being asked about political discussions directly between KAMENEV and SMILGA.

SAFAROV: There were meetings; SMILGA himself told me.

KAMENEV: I deny that. I had business meetings with SMILGA, but there were no political conversations before 1932.

You mentioned that ZINOVIEV had said you were to get a draft program from me, STEN’s sketches. Do you know anything else about this? Did anything appear in Party circles?

SAFAROV: When STEN’s group was broken up, I concluded that this was Ryutin’s group.

KAMENEV: For some reason you insisted that your meeting with ZINOVIEV took place between July 10 and 15, that you had concentrated the contacts in your own hands, and that these negotiations came to nothing. ## OF IVAN STEPANOVICH GORSHENIN

CHAIRMAN: The hearing resumes. Defendant GORSHENIN\!

CHAIRMAN: For many years you were connected with the Moscow Center and recently belonged to it. Tell us what methods of struggle against the leadership of our Party and government the Moscow Center used in recent years.

GORSHENIN: Regarding the methods of struggle employed by our counterrevolutionary organization against the Party line and leadership, I can say the following: first of all, our basic policy was to preserve the cadres.

CHAIRMAN: Which cadres?

GORSHENIN: To preserve our cadres—the former Zinovievites making up our counterrevolutionary organization—inside the Party. That was our principal tactical reliance, and we made every effort to keep them in the Party.

As for tactics and the organization’s aims, the main aim was the return of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV to the leadership. This remained the central goal throughout our activity.

We envisaged this as the restoration of the old, so-called Leninist Bureau...

CHAIRMAN: You intended to introduce ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV in place of whom?

GORSHENIN: There was a general formulation about restoring the Leninist Politburo. Details about precisely whom to replace were not discussed. The matter concerned ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV and their return to the Politburo.

CHAIRMAN: Do you understand me? Introduce ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, but in place of whom?

GORSHENIN: In place of KAGANOVICH, KIROV, and others.

As for methods and tactics, these must be divided into two periods: before 1932 and afterwards. In the first period we relied upon the difficulties inside the country, which became temporarily acute and worsened in 1932, to generate disagreements in the Party and the Central Committee. We assumed this would inevitably bring down the leadership, that opposition elements would emerge within the Central Committee, and that we should act together with them, in a bloc, against the existing leadership.

After the exile of ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV I think the essence of our organization’s work did not change, but its tactics did. The difficulties had been overcome—it was already 1933—and this was even clearer in 1934; the counterrevolutionary anti-Party groups on whose intervention we had counted, and with which we could have acted jointly, had been crushed. No prospect remained for our organization or center in that respect. To penetrate the leadership and force our way back into it, there was only one method left: maneuver, feign sincerity, and try to win the confidence of the existing leadership. Hence KAMENEV’s and ZINOVIEV’s speeches at the Seventeenth Congress, which I regard as a maneuver.

CHAIRMAN: You regard both ZINOVIEV’s and KAMENEV’s speeches as a maneuver?

GORSHENIN: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: And the tactic of constantly discrediting the Party and its leaders was also your tactic?

GORSHENIN: Yes, that too was part of our tactics, part of our methods. At our gatherings and meetings we criticized Party policy, constantly attacked the leadership, and maintained hostile attitudes toward the existing leaders, especially Comrade STALIN. That was another of our methods.

CHAIRMAN: What consequences followed from cultivating these attitudes?

GORSHENIN: The consequences are plain to see.

CHAIRMAN: Speak more clearly.

GORSHENIN: I consider that our cultivation over many years of a hostile, embittered attitude toward the Party leadership, particularly Comrade STALIN, could not fail to prepare the ground for the emergence of a fascist-type organization such as the Leningrad group that carried out the terrorist act. CHAIRMAN: Who personally directed this campaign to discredit the leaders and create hostility toward them?

GORSHENIN: The leaders of our entire organization were ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV...

GORSHENIN: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: Up to very recently?

GORSHENIN: In the last year there was much more caution and restraint, but sometimes it broke through. I became firmly convinced that even during 1934, regardless of how it was expressed, the hostile attitude toward the leadership, particularly STALIN, remained essentially unchanged.

CHAIRMAN: How were the Moscow Center’s links with other cities—Leningrad, Sverdlovsk, and others—manifested?

GORSHENIN: As for Leningrad, I cannot say anything, for I am not from Leningrad, know little of the local organization’s members, and know nothing of its connections; I have little to say on th\<at\> subject. CHAIRMAN: Whom in particular?

GORSHENIN: Before 1932 those who came visited ZINOVIEV, EVDOKIMOV, GERTIK, and me. In recent years, EVDOKIMOV.

GORSHENIN: No, apparently there were none. I have named those I know personally and with whom I met.

CHAIRMAN: You worked at Gosplan of the RSFSR as Hea\<d\> of the Consolidated Planning Department?

GORSHENIN: Yes.

GORSHENIN: Mainly on the condition of our country, the results of the First Five-Year Plan, livestock raising; above all, information not released to the press, as well as information that was published but was presented by u\<s\> in an anti-Party and anti-Soviet light.

CHAIRMAN: Was there an occasion when you had to leave Gosplan and KAMENEV told you not to leave?

MATULEVICH: Did you know of the organization’s “Leningrad Hungarian”?

It has already been said that our organization had no positive political or economic program. Yet for seven years we existed underground and pursued our counterrevolutionary activity. What held us together for those seven years? It cannot be explained simply by everyday habits, by our liking for tea and the pelmeni that EVDOKIMOV served us.

There was something deeper, some root that bound and united us for seven years. What was this guilt?

If we analyze all our sentiments and our assessments of the Party’s main decisions and general line, we must state unequivocally that the ideological-political basis of the existence and counterrevolutionary activity of our organization was disbelief in building socialism in one country. This was the ideological-political foundation upon which our organization rested.

EVDOKIMOV has already spoken in part about this. I am repeating myself somewhat...

CHAIRMAN: Why repeat it? You have already said that you agree entirely with all the propositions...

GORSHENIN: I ask permission to finish my thought: disbelief in building socialism in one country was the central, decisive point.

We denied the socialist character of the collective-farm movement; we believed there was no socialism there and that collective farms would collapse. We also denied the socialist character of the great construction projects, such as Magnitostroi, the Kuznetsk plant, and so on, on the grounds that workers lived in dugouts, conditions were unsanitary, work conditions were not socialist, and other similar slanderous arguments. We said that nothing being built was essentially socialist.

I have already said that we examined international policy and Comintern policy from that same standpoint, from \<disbelief\> in the possibility of building socialism in one country, and held that STALIN consciously refrained from stimulating the Comintern’s activity, subordinating everything to his idea—the idea, wrong in our view, of building socialism in one country.

Someone said that our basis was the old platform.

That is not entirely accurate, since much has changed; but the main idea of the old platform, disbelief in the possibility of building socialism in one country, remained essentially intact. Neither I nor the other members of our organization with whom I had contact, whose sentiments I could ascertain, had overcome these old ideas. Hence the conclusion must be that after the Fifteenth Congress we disarmed neither organizationally nor ideologically and continued to fight the Party line.

Which classes’ views and sentiments did our organization reflect when it denied the possibility of building socialism in one country? I think there is little need to say more, since it is perfectly clear: the sentiments of classes alien to the proletariat and the dictatorship of the proletariat, of the remnants of the capitalist exploiting classes that had not lost hope of socialism’s failure.

CHAIRMAN: Have you discovered this only now, or did you know throughout those eight years and consciously act against the Party?

The second element uniting our organization was hostility toward the Party leadership, particularly Comrade STALIN; I regard it as the second factor cementing our organization together.

I have already said that this hostility was sustained and inflamed, and its source was our leaders, the heads of our organization: ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV.

I confirm that our criticism was almost always accompanied by attacks and the claim that if we were in power, if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV led the Party, everything would be far better than it was.

I would like to address one further matter: KAMENEV’s position. The indictment refers to his statement that he belonged to and led the organization until 1930, but had essentially withdrawn from it afterwards. Lev Borisovich’s way of putting this surprises me somewhat.

First, I know with absolute certainty that meetings of members of the center and their meetings with KAMENEV were held in his apartment not only until 1930 but up to 1932, and quite often.

SHAROV, I, KHARITONOV, and other members of our Moscow Center and the organization visited him. Up to 1932.

CHAIRMAN: And in 1933–34?

GORSHENIN: In late 1932 a meeting was held at my apartment on Lev Borisovich’s initiative, attended by about twelve people, including KHARITONOV, I think BAKAEV, KOSTINA, and GERTIK. There was much discussion of stagnation on the ideological and cultural front, and so on.

This coincides precisely with the revival of the activity of the whole counterrevolutionary organization in autumn 1932, as I recall.

During 1933 and 1934, out of caution, KAMENEV met nobody, so far as I know, but remained in close and constant contact with ZINOVIEV, not only at the dacha but in the city as well. From conversations with ZINOVIEV I know very well that

there was not a single politically important issue placed before our organization on which ZINOVIEV did not consult KAMENEV.

I say that KAMENEV, out of caution, did not meet anyone.

CHAIRMAN: Are you speaking of 1934 onward?

GORSHENIN: In August 1934 at ZINOVIEV’s and KAMENEV’s dacha, in KAMENEV’s study, a meeting was attended\<by\> KAMENEV, ZINOVIEV, and EVDOKIMOV concerning his removal from the editorial board of “Bolshevik.”

CHAIRMAN: Did ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV not speak with visitors from the provinces in 1934?

GORSHENIN: In practice they did. I met ZINOVIEV about five times and told him everything.

CHAIRMAN: Do you fully confirm your testimony of December 14?

GORSHENIN: I do.

## OF ALEKSANDR VLADIMIROVICH HERTZBERG .

Our relations were not of a kind in which he would give me information. When SAFAROV visited me I asked whether he had seen BA\<D\>DING, recently arrived from Germany and working for the Comintern. That was the simple question I put to SAFAROV, but he may have understood it as meaning that BADDING had then informed me about the German Party situation.

That is the actual state of affairs.

My actual activity in the underground Zinovievite organization consisted (and this does not lessen my guilt) of keeping contact with individual Zinovievites. I visited ZINOVIEV in summer 1932 and later met EVDOKIMOV several times by chance, without political discussions; these were wholly accidental encounters. I believe that this one fact is the best proof that I had not severed my ties with the Zinovievites, with their political system, or with Zinovievism. By this fact alone I must be regarded as a member of the Moscow organization.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Your earlier testimony about this episode was expressed even more strongly. You wrote: “During the Seventeenth Congress I met KUKLIN. This was the talk of a traitor who hated the Party, the Soviet Union, and its leaders.”

HERTZBERG: Yes, I consider that completely correct.

CHAIR\<MAN\>: Second question: when you talked with ZINOVIEV in summer 1932, you testified that ZINOVIEV said: “The Comintern, through its policies, assists HITLER’s coming to power...”

Do you confirm that testimony?

HERTZBERG: Yes, I confirm it. Permit me to add something SAFAROV recalled and I had forgotten. I testified that ZINOVIEV assessed the Comintern’s work and complained that the Central Committee was pushing the old cadres out of leadership; KRZHIZHANOVSKY’s removal was discussed. SAFAROV correctly recalled that ZINOVIEV said to me: “You know, SAFAROV was here,” without adding more, except that SAFAROV had come to work out some kind of platform. When SAFAROV visited me in summer 1932, he said something about my having just heard of this, as an example of the speed of communications. But that cannot be stated in that way, because it is entirely possible that I put such a question to SAFAROV. I must state categorically that when he was at my home SAFAROV did not tell me that he had visited ZINOVIEV to develop a platform, or that MRACHKOVSKY and others figured in the matter. Moreover, I did not talk to SAFAROV alone: my wife POZDEEVA was present and could be questioned. I know she knows nothing of it either, and I myself learned of it only today in court.

After GERTIK returned from exile, he visited my apartment. I asked: “Why were you exiled?” He said there had been some misunderstanding. “ZINOVIEV was exiled and they considered me his agent. They arrested and exiled me for no reason.” The impression was that “they” were one thing and he was entirely innocent. At that time I was not disposed to break with GERTIK. Asking myself whom I trusted more—him or the fact of his exile—I must honestly say that I thought some mistake had occurred. Yes, I must admit I was politically blind, that I did not understand, and that I regarded ZINOVIEV’s and KAMENEV’s statements at the Congress as sincere. I did not understand. Today I would say: “I did not understand that it was half the truth”; I believed it was sincere. I knew people met and visited one another, but until then I interpreted hostility to the leadership as arising from their having no posts, no work. This does not absolve me, of course, and I will not speak in my own favor. On the contrary, when I was receiving treatment at the Kremlin hospital, I met certain members of the organization but did not discuss anything with them. Perhaps if the meetings had been more frequent I would have understood matters better.

I must also say the following: I never heard any Zinovievite say: “We have done so many foolish things; now we must correct them.” Nor did I ever tell anyone that I had abandoned my old attitudes; on the contrary, I took part in the conversations. Therefore I say that I bear full responsibility for the aims pursued by the Zinovievite organization and all its acts. The organization’s acts resulted in a situation in which it, including me among its members, must bear full responsibility for Comrade KIROV’s murder.

I think everyone must now acknowledge one simple thing. It is very bad that it is so late, but I must declare that I accept fully the following: the entire system of Zinovievism is an unbroken chain of deception and betrayal of the Party and working class. This chain descended to the very bottom of counterrevolution, linking itself to fascism. The Zinovievites have fallen into the abyss, and between that abyss and us now lies the blood of one of the leaders of Soviet power. This is a reality from which there is no escape. It is perfectly clear that the entire organization and each individual member bear full responsibility for the murder, whether they met once in three years or more often: the connection existed.

BRAVO: After passing the document on I did not see ZINOVIEV again, but I had told him about it in autumn 1932 when I visited his dacha with EVDOKIMOV.

CHAIRMAN: One of your statements records that you admit guilt for pursuing a two-faced policy while a Party member and, after joining the Zinovievite organization in 1930, carrying out counterrevolutionary work to undermine the Party’s authority.

Do you confirm that?

BRAVO: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: You committed crimes by using your official position and giving secret documents to your organization.

Do you confirm that?

BRAVO: Yes, I confirm it.

CHAIRMAN: You spread bitterness and hatred toward the Party leadership among the oppositionists you met.

Do you confirm that?

BRAVO: Yes, I confirm it.

CHAIRMAN: What do you wish to add?

BRAVO: I found myself in roughly the same circumstances as my associate HERTZBERG, and the functions I performed consisted of gathering information. My associate HERTZBERG said here that although he might not have known much, he bore political and moral responsibility for what the Zinovievite organization did and intended to do.

I must apply what he said to myself as well.

But there was very much I did not know or understand until today. Matters were not so simple as SAFAROV suggested: there was a chain through which all kinds of rumors and slander passed, but that was not everything. Not everything passed along that chain, since although I was close to EVDOKIMOV, I did not know many facts. I did not know that SAFAROV had embarked on the path of counterrevolutionary struggle, even though he spoke with EVDOKIMOV. Despite my closeness to EVDOKIMOV, I did not know that some bloc was being prepared or that negotiations were taking place. Despite our closeness, I never heard from him such a developed counterrevolutionary program as he described here before the court as the organization's program. We discussed isolated questions. He spoke about his conversations but did not tell me many things, and I therefore saw matters differently. When we talked about the collective-farm movement, I understood that enormous overhead costs were being discussed; I did not know this meant collectivization ought not to continue. Rather, I imagined that if our former\< opposition\> leaders were in power, it could all have been accomplished at less cost.

When he spoke of the working class's difficult food situation, I understood the problem not to be that industrialization had been pushed too far — industrialization itself was a good thing — but that it was being carried out very badly, and that was why workers were hungry. It turns out there was much I did not know, much that the chain did not pass on to me despite my closeness to EVDOKIMOV.

Nevertheless, despite all this, I am obliged to share not merely political and moral responsibility for everything the opposition did but also responsibility under Soviet law.

And there is a second issue: responsibility for the blood shed by Comrade KIROV.

My main activity was gathering information, but the chief and essential thing I did was to scatter the seeds of bitterness and hatred toward the leadership that flourished in the counterrevolutionary organization. During the investigation I gave some examples.

How did we act? I would come, say, to Leningrad, meet LEVIN and BASHKIROV, and our conversations mainly revolved around the former opposition leaders. We had Grigory ZINOVIEV, Grigory EVDOKIMOV, and Grigory FEDOROV — three leaders who “suffered endlessly.” We made martyrs of them, surrounded them with a halo of martyrdom: they were not given work, they were oppressed, every possible way was sought to remove them not only from the leadership but from the Party. Everyone who met me would invariably ask, “How is EVDOKIMOV doing?” and I would answer that “poor EVDOKIMOV” was feeling this way or that.

I recall that when I was at ZINOVIEV's dacha (this was the second time; the first was in 1930), in response to news that YAROSLAVSKY had been removed from Pravda's editorial board, ZINOVIEV said something to the effect that this was understandable: of course STALIN would not work with anyone who had known LENIN and worked with him. Naturally I repeated that remark, in different forms, when I was in Leningrad. To whom and under what circumstances, I cannot say.

Every pretext was used to turn people against STALIN. If the subject was the death of Dina GERTIK... there were any number of cases and examples where everything was used to turn people against the leaders, against STALIN.

And whereas one part of the counterrevolutionary organization understood these feelings as meaning that they had to struggle to advance their own leaders, another part understood them differently, in its own way, as “strike him down\!” That is why I must state directly that I must bear not only political and moral responsibility but also responsibility under Soviet law for a leader's shed blood.

INTERROGATION

## OF YAKOV VASILIEVICH SHAROV

CHAIRMAN: Defendant SHAROV, there were three periods during your detention. In the first you admitted nothing; in the next, approximately from January 5, you admitted belonging to the anti-Party Zinovievite group. On the eighth you expanded that admission. Do you confirm what EVDOKIMOV said?

SHAROV: First of all I must say that however harsh the assessment that has been made, I share it fully and completely.

I took the path of struggle against the Party at the Fourteenth Congress. Between the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Congresses I waged a conscious, open struggle against the Party. I belonged to the center of the Trotskyist-Zinovievite organization and was an active member. The theses for the Fifteenth Congress and the Congress's decisions momentarily lifted my spirits and my hopes that I should adopt the Party's position. But the old ties were not broken, nor were opposition sentiments wholly abandoned. Thus through one door I stood on the Party's general line, and through another I went to ZINOVIEV's apartment, where immediately after the Fifteenth Congress there was a meeting that in substance continued the old platform, only under new conditions.

The meeting at ZINOVIEV's apartment was formally convened to liquidate the organization that had existed between the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Congresses. In fact this was no liquidation but organization in a new form — or more accurately a new direction, new methods of struggle against the Party.

Almost everyone who had signed the “Platform of the 23” attended this first large meeting at ZINOVIEV's apartment. In fact it was precisely a gathering of those signatories, and so I ask to be excused from listing those present, since it was a continuation of what had been done before the Fifteenth Congress.

The basic orientations at that meeting appeared in KAMENEV's report on the country's economic condition, with certain changes from before the Fifteenth Congress. Perhaps the wording concerning the leadership was merely softened. This was the essence of ZINOVIEV's report. He raised the issue that the previous form of struggle was no longer suitable. We therefore had to build a new organizational form that would preserve our essential cadres. That form consisted of personal reports on conditions. You know and remember that, by decision of the Fifteenth Congress, we were required to disperse across the different parts of our Union. According to the instructions we received, we were all to gather information and report to ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV; conversely, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV would gather information in the center and report to us. Thus a new connection, new forms of struggle, were established. One could say we were no longer fighting as we had on the eve of the Fourteenth Congress, gathering and transmitting information to the Leningrad commission. Instead we adopted the tactic of a snake's hiss, intending at the first opportunity to release poison and poison others; and, on the other hand, to collect everything that could be found against Soviet power, inform the center, and pass all lies and slanders on to the whole group and individual people. Such was that first meeting immediately after the Fifteenth Congress.

After that meeting I left for Ulyanovsk, spent six months there, and can report no interesting episodes because I did nothing there. I can only say that while staying in Ulyanovsk I went on a day off to Kaluga, where ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were. I remember ZINOVIEV going through a great mass of books in his study; he said he was writing a book on instructions from the Politburo and was in fairly good spirits.

After Ulyanovsk I returned to Moscow in August.

I found ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV already there; probably hardly any of our old oppositionists were present. I therefore often visited both ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. At these meetings I gave them more information than they gave me. I was then working at the Moscow Council of the National Economy, was a member of the Regional Executive Committee, took part in the activists' meetings, and whenever possible informed them about what was happening in the Moscow Committee and at those meetings. I was acquainted with the work and kept them informed.

CHAIRMAN: This is becoming too detailed; make it shorter.

SHAROV: In 1928–29 more and more people gathered in Moscow. The work, contacts, conversations, and meetings revived, and continued throughout the various stages and under differing assessments. Therefore I wish to dwell on my last meeting with ZINOVIEV, in order to give a correct assessment of my conduct and that of the counterrevolutionary organization.

In 1932, approximately in late April or early March, when I was at ZINOVIEV's dacha in Ilyinskaya, he talked with me, reviewing the entire situation of the collective-farm movement at that time. On the condition of the working class he put it approximately like this: “Now all the stones,” or, as he said, “soon even the stones will speak, and we, Comrade SHAROV, are all keeping silent.”

That was his general assessment at the time.

That was the assessment KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV gave.

If before the Fifteenth Congress, when it was a question of the struggle and the leadership, one said “I will vote for STALIN against RYKOV,” then in 1932 one had to formulate it as “I will join everyone against STALIN.” That, it seems to me, would be the correct assessment, \*which ZINOVIEV gave. Thus I can say\* that up to 1932 our assessment, that of our counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization, had changed in economic\< matters\> not at all, apart from the snake's hiss and slander over isolated episodes in the Party's struggle to build our socialist society. The question of struggle against the leadership was raised ever more forcefully. That is approximately how all my meetings and ties with ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV up to midsummer 1932 may be assessed; after that I did not see them. I was more closely connected with, and met, EVDOKIMOV. I have already said, and today I am especially struck by the fact, that EVDOKIMOV and I are old friends, bosom friends, as the saying goes. Nevertheless, I was greatly surprised by what SAFAROV said today about EVDOKIMOV meeting Trotskyists, forming a bloc, and so on, and about his recent assessment of the Comintern when speaking with ZINOVIEV.

I must say EVDOKIMOV never discussed these issues with me. At the same time various questions arose for me and I put them to EVDOKIMOV. For example, when SOSNOVSKY's article appeared in Izvestia \[32\], I was leaving the canteen with EVDOKIMOV and said to him: it looks as though SOSNOVSKY has been readmitted to the Party and will probably live in Moscow; apparently the man has come back.

He did not answer my question, but formulated something like this: “Oh, at some convenient opportunity the boss will deal with us so harshly that we won't know what hit us.” This had to be understood as meaning that many of us were gathering in Moscow and the boss, meaning STALIN, would therefore deal with us. That was his answer to the question I had asked.

CHAIRMAN: What has SOSNOVSKY got to do with it?

SHAROV: I said SOSNOVSKY had probably returned to Moscow; he did not answer the question itself but said things would soon go badly for us. I could not understand what that had to do with my question about SOSNOVSKY's return to the Party. Today, however, it is more or less clear to me.

CHAIRMAN: What seems clear to you?

SHAROV: When I heard that in 1932 he negotiated with the Trotskyists and continued making that assessment of the international movement, it all showed that EVDOKIMOV continued close work and established links for the struggle against the Party.

The second incident was when, in 1934, during one of the reports at the People's Commissar's office, I heard: “Your ZINOVIEV has landed in trouble again; there is a Central Committee resolution removing him from his work at Bolshevik.” At my first meeting with EVDOKIMOV I asked what had happened. He said it was not worth talking much about. Again he evaded my question.

Such was EVDOKIMOV's attitude toward me lately.

I must tell the court about one conversation I had with EVDOKIMOV.

Once we assessed the “Ivanovo affair” by saying it could be compared with Kronstadt. STALIN, of course, would draw no conclusion from that story of the Ivanovo revolt other than making an example of some individuals through reprisals. That was approximately our assessment of the Ivanovo events \[33\].

That is what I can say about my conversations and meetings with EVDOKIMOV. I also had various meetings with other participants in our counterrevolutionary Zinovievite organization; I met them, or rather they visited me, from the first half of 1933 until recently. REM \[34\] came; he lived in Leningrad, worked at the Oktyabrsky factory as chairman of the ORS \[35\], and frequently came to Moscow on business and often visited me. But I must say there was nothing so interesting or valuable in those meetings as to present it to the court as material illustrating one or another of our attitudes.

There were no such conversations with REM. I told him how the deputy\< authorized representative\> drove me away; I told him how well Comrade EZHOV treated me. This is not of much value to the court...

CHAIRMAN: You yourself say it is insignificant, so why are you telling us?

SHAROV: Because it was discussed during the investigation, I am telling you about it.

... And one more meeting, with TARTAKOVSKAYA in November, which likewise contains nothing of interest.

CHAIRMAN: Tell us, please: until recently you belonged to the Moscow Center together with GORSHENIN and GERTIK, correct?

SHAROV: Yes, I am a historic member.

CHAIRMAN: Since the Fourteenth Congress?

SHAROV: Since the Fourteenth Congress.

CHAIRMAN: Who belonged to the Center? According to your testimony: ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, GORSHENIN, GERTIK, SHAROV, KHARITONOV...

SHAROV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: Did you often travel to Moscow on organizational business?

SHAROV: I must tell the court honestly that my last actual trip on counterrevolutionary organizational business was on the eve of the Fifteenth Congress or during the Congress itself. That was when I had a conversation at LEVIN's apartment. I think it was during the Fifteenth Congress itself.

CHAIRMAN: According to MYASNIKOV's testimony, it was in summer 1929.

SHAROV: I think it was before the Fifteenth Congress, before my departure for Ulyanovsk.

CHAIRMAN: You have already testified in detail about that meeting; there is no need to repeat it. Do you confirm your testimony?

SHAROV: Yes.

CHAIRMAN: And did you travel during the last year?

SHAROV: During the last year I traveled because my family is there.

CHAIRMAN: When you went to Leningrad, did you meet your associates?

SHAROV: I must say that on all my trips to Leningrad I never met the Leningrad comrades. The trips went like this: I would go on the eve of a day off and return on the day off itself. Those trips were exclusively to see my children.

CHAIRMAN: At the face-to-face confrontation with EVDOKIMOV, to the question “Name the members of the Leningrad Zinovievite organization known to you,” you answered: “The Leningrad Zinovievite organization included Vladimir LEVIN, RUMYANTSEV, MANDELSTAM, KOTOLYNOV, ALEKSANDROV \[36\], KOSTRITSKY, KORSHUNOV, and REM.”

SHAROV: Yes, I named them, but I did not meet them. I believed a center existed in Leningrad just as in Moscow, but I met none of them except LEVIN; I met him accidentally in 1933.

CHAIRMAN: But you testified: “In Leningrad I knew of the existence of a Zinovievite organization led by the ‘Leningrad Center,’ whose members included Vladimir LEVIN, RUMYANTSEV, and MANDELSTAM.” This is your testimony of January 8.

SHAROV: Yes, allow me a few words: from the Fourteenth to the Fifteenth Congress, the Leningrad members always considered themselves connected with the Moscow center only through ZINOVIEV, or at most through EVDOKIMOV. After the Fifteenth Congress, I think they did not even speak with EVDOKIMOV. They considered it a revolutionary merit to speak only with the leaders.

CHAIRMAN: And did they consider it beneath them to speak with you?

SHAROV: They were all leftists and made no attempt to meet with me. Indeed, since the Fifteenth Congress (apart from one meeting with LEVIN) I had not seen them. Therefore, when SAFAROV says I went to Leningrad, I asked him to name a single instance when I went and spoke with someone from that group with whom I broke at the Fifteenth Congress. So, strangely enough, although I worked for three decades in Leningrad, I had no connection with Leningrad.

During the Fourteenth Congress I was an active participant, was in Moscow, and worked for Leningrad; afterwards I maintained no connection and carried out no organizational work.

CHAIRMAN: Who in your organization especially inflamed bitterness and hatred?

SHAROV: ZINOVIEV nevertheless remained the principal leader and inspirer.

INTERROGATION

## OF GRIGORY EVSEYEVICH ZINOVIEV

CHAIRMAN: Defendant ZINOVIEV, what can you say regarding the charge brought against you?

ZINOVIEV: I am fully conscious of and acknowledge that I am the principal culprit in this case.

I recognize and acknowledge many features of this affair connected chiefly with my personal political mistakes, shortcomings and crimes, with my own rather large, enormous personal shortcomings. There are, of course, general questions as well.

There are many of us in the dock, more than fifteen people with different biographies; among them are many who have taken part in the workers' movement for many years. Much of what they did was out of trust in me, and for that, naturally, I must condemn myself. Much else exists independently of that. So this is not only a matter concerning me personally or other leaders of this organization, initially factional and then grown into a counterrevolutionary one; there is something more general as well.

At this stage I see my task as sincerely and wholeheartedly repenting before the court of the working class, to the very end, for what I have come to understand as mistakes and crimes, and describing all this in such a way that it will not only put an end to this group once and for all but will also bring some benefit in connection with this case, for instance by serving as a personal lesson to others.

There are still factions in the country that have become, or are becoming, such organizations, and the same fate awaits them. Abroad, in fraternal communist parties, in other countries and circumstances, there are germs of similar phenomena that may serve as examples of what happens and of the conclusions to which it leads. I too want to draw a final balance.

The very course of the investigation and inquiry in this case also bears the marks, all the marks, of the work carried on for many years.

We lacked spirit and strength, lacked the resolve to raise the question clearly, frankly, and fully from the beginning.

How often people in our circles said in the sharpest terms “what have things come to,” that the organs of internal power, the OGPU, ought to intervene. And did this case not turn out to be the sharp point of that very question put before us?

And so, in this instance, it is only with the aid of the organs of state power that these deeply embedded splinters are finally being pulled out.

Of course it is not the case that we are all simply villains, certainly not. In this sense I wanted to reply to EVDOKIMOV's testimony concerning the difference between subjective and objective things.

It is true that the Party judges us according to objective characteristics.

This is what makes our case so painful, even though I do not know the overwhelming majority of those sitting in the dock. Subjectively they were devoted to the working class and objectively did not want counterrevolutionary sabotage of Soviet power, yet the opposite occurred. That is the sense in which I spoke of the subjective element in my statement. Of course one cannot imagine that a person was either wholly honest or absolutely dishonest, or that all we thought of was how to deceive. It was not so. There were hesitations and doubts; many were overcome, many returned in worse forms. I am speaking of myself and think this applies to others too. The result was the picture now unfolding before us. If we set aside my own misfortunes, grievances and crimes, now that we stand trial, we may say that the various groups which united had something in common. This common element was factionalism on a mistaken political platform, which later developed into counterrevolution and could not fail to do so. That is the point.

At the Seventeenth Party Congress I happened to use a certain phrase. I remember that once in conversation the leader, Comrade STALIN, told me: “A mistake can be forgiven you as it can anyone else, but hypocrisy and double-dealing toward the Party cannot and will not be forgiven.” The Congress noted this correctly. I answered at the time that this was of course true, but where did the hypocrisy come from? Not only from personality, but from factionalism, from hypocrisy. This was correct in general terms, but only in general terms. I was dragging this double-dealing and hypocrisy out of myself with tweezers and pincers — rightly described by a very harsh word, duplicity. I was dragging it out of myself, of course, under arrest, in the light of Comrade KIROV's murder, and did not get it all out. Only now is state power intervening in the matter, rightly, and it will drag out this double-dealing to the end. I shall not rehearse elementary points about factionalism being inadmissible in the Party under the dictatorship of the proletariat and its inevitably leading to counterrevolution.

Now I must speak much more concretely. If you have at first found yourself merely in opposition to the working class, if you have not understood or accepted this idea, you must either submit or withdraw, and submission must above all include revealing your organizational connections to the organs of state power. What seemed to me and my comrades most intolerable and irreconcilable proves to be the most elementary and necessary thing. It is not a matter of speaking of laws but of understanding to the very end. It is not enough to make a declaration or a statement: it is an elementary demand that you disclose your organizational links to the Party. And the word “disclose” has none of the bad connotations to which we became accustomed before the revolution. This means ending factionalism, and the Party itself will know how to sort out those whose names you have revealed and do what is necessary in the interests of the working class.

If you cannot submit, if you feel you cannot do so, then step aside, take up some purely practical work and do not try to prepare any kind of double-dealing. This poison will destroy you and your friends and inflict great damage on the cause you wish, or once wished, to serve.

Neither the investigation nor the court demanded an account from me going further back than the Fifteenth Congress. First I must say that when I saw Uritsky Square today, after so many years, I involuntarily recalled November 7, 1927 \[37\] and thought: had we been arrested then, it would have been better for the republic, for the Party and a little for ourselves. Had the question been put squarely then — I do not say this because I am guilty now and wish to shift blame onto others — it is now clear that the firmer the approach to this matter, the better it would have been.

I did not attend the Fifteenth Party Congress because I had been expelled. But when it was said that voices had been raised almost calling for the opposition's arrest, I was of course climbing the walls; at that time there was no abuse I did not utter over this, and, naturally, against STALIN. Is it not clear that it was right? It was right because objectively no other guilt remains.

Our example can give concrete form to the general laws concerning the criminal nature of factional struggle against the Bolshevik Party in the era of the dictatorship of the proletariat on an incorrect political platform developing into counterrevolution. We can now specify the forms. They are not new; perhaps they are new to me, perhaps for the first time they have taken such clear shape for me, but one must state quite concretely that there are only these ways out. One is to go directly underground, establish a new party, as Trotsky did, for example. We did not choose that road; I shall not now explain in detail why. Another way, if you feel unable to submit, is to withdraw and find some small piece of work for the working class outside the Party, naturally under its supervision.

But there was a third road, which proved the worst and most terrible: the Party demands something, I declare that I submit, but mentally reserve certain qualifications, and then at every turn of events I seek justifications for myself.

This third road that we chose was the most terrible, a road that led honest people (I do not mean myself; there were undoubtedly honest people in that movement) onto a path that can only be called counterrevolutionary and ultimately brought them to the court in which we now sit.

This path is best characterized by the word duplicity. It was very difficult to accept that word, having lived through it, to apply it to oneself... But it is correct... there is no escape from it; no other word will do.

Not because the people concerned were personally villains, but because that road inevitably leads there. Everything follows from that.

In a proletarian party, of course, disagreements and intense struggle can occur, and open demands for a change of leadership are possible. But the road we took, the road of double-dealing, then led to concealing things from the Party and partly from oneself... double-dealing with oneself, because tonight you convince yourself of something and think one way, and tomorrow new events arise — in Ivanovo-Voznesensk, or involving collective farms and food supplies — and all that stirs up something new, turns everything upside down in your head, and you start pulling out the same arguments once again, thus deceiving yourself. You keep trying to pull out that splinter, and cannot.

For me personally another factor played an enormous role (I cannot and must not conceal it): I had grown used to feeling myself a leader. For twenty years I had been a member of the Central Committee; I took it for granted that I ought to know everything, that if I was removed from the leadership it was either an injustice, a misunderstanding, or something lasting only a few months. It was terribly difficult for me to step down from that position.

I understand this is no justification. I understand it is no mitigating circumstance. But it relieves me to express everything I really think and as it really happened. I am pulling out of myself the last splinter of those crimes, that shameful counterrevolutionary road now being laid bare here.

This played an enormous part, for I failed to understand that once, after Lenin's death, you first came forward claiming to show another road; once that road was shown to be wrong, to put it mildly; once you \{came together\} \*linked yourself\* with Trotsky, against whom you had fought for so many years; once you devised a platform against the Party line after Lenin's death; once events had proved all this — you should find the courage to step into the background, start again, become an ordinary worker, make no claims, and so forth. But I could not imagine things otherwise: how could I be without my own circle, without knowing everything, without being in the thick of politics? Countless connections remained, through the Comintern and other kinds of work, above all in Leningrad.

And for me personally this played an especially great and fatal role on the road that brought me here. It follows from this that these were not especially evil or corrupt people who indulged in mockery, gossip and repeating every kind of rumor... it was the absolutely inevitable chemical result of the fact that, politically bankrupt, we took the road of double-dealing and concealment, and I personally was unable to relinquish the claim to a leading role.

The Party still treated us very carefully. This continued for many years, and the more the originally principled character, although mistaken, was hollowed out, the more elements of gossip, personal struggle, unhealthy reactions to events in the Central Committee, rumor-mongering and the like gained the upper hand.

That is the general character. If I am not mistaken, in my final statement I shall have an opportunity to supplement anything I have left unsaid in this part.

Now I must turn to the factual side.

\[Everything I have said follows from the entire conduct here in the pretrial detention facility \[38\], follows because when talking with the central Party organs it was necessary to address counterrevolutionary groupings and the question of the Moscow Center.\] \[39\]

Was it a center? Of course it was. Up to 1929 it existed like any organization, with all the appropriate attributes.

I did not select it, but on the shoulders of the Fourteenth Party Congress that group remained, awaiting new events and the appearance of the Rightists.

If one examines the situation, everyone can see this, for instance in the question of the Rightists.

Others and I considered ourselves opposed to the Rightists. We claimed to have opposed them before, when the Rightists truly emerged from their shell, when they openly appeared, when STALIN unleashed a genuine hurricane of fire against them. But it turned out that at that very moment the meetings between BUKHARIN, myself and KAMENEV, the way we regarded matters, gave rise to a philosophy that we had to inform ourselves, had to know everything, while in practice sympathizing with another line. We truly did sympathize. All this had to be woven into double-dealing, keeping double books with the Party. And this brought matters to the point of a real defeat, when the issue of liquidating the kulaks as a class was raised and the Party turned upon them. I personally, with TOMSKY and, I recall, partly with FEDOROV, said all manner of outrageous things against the leadership and the Party's general line.

The real defeat, when the Party had prepared itself and become powerful.

The Central Committee under STALIN struck along the whole line. This was the organic defect in our entire existence.

Until 1929, of course, we had a center organized according to all the rules, negotiating with other centers and so on.

In 1929, of course, much could be said of honor as fear, of another's gangrene.

The Party raised the issue ever more decisively. Naturally there were fears of being caught, and new forms were sought while remaining within the general bounds of the same double-dealing.

But it was not only that: life itself, like a hammer, shattered the very last remnants of the political ideology that had existed.

There was none of that lull between 1932 and 1933, for the same reasons, \<as\> a counterweight to fear, and then of course because of the immense events which increasingly, piece by piece, finally shattered the old ideology to fragments.

In 1933 came the so-called revival of our Center and of all the other anti-Party bureaus.

I described that year in some detail, partly on my own initiative \[40\], partly under pressure from the investigation's face-to-face confrontations.

I think that now, taken together, my statements have truly shed light on 1932 as it actually happened. I shall not discuss it in detail now.

It was when the greatest operation conceived by the Party, the liquidation of the kulaks as a class, was approaching its end and its successful conclusion still required some time; when the illness could still be felt in many places and resistance by hostile elements was increasingly heated, that these difficulties began. Then all the bad elements unable to understand the historical significance began to revive, everything essentially non-Bolshevik and, in fact, double-dealing, among us in particular.

The gigantic operation under way and already nearing a successful conclusion, everything I had not believed in, became proletarian and brought a surge of success, a surge of hope, a surge of bitterness against the leadership.

It was just then that all the groupings began to look for leaders for themselves, either me or KAMENEV. My candidacy was being put forward, so to speak, from every side; that is beyond dispute.

This, for me, is of course, in historical terms, nothing but a disgrace. That is how it was.

The right-wing elements, for instance, that same kulak Ryutin platform, keep making a great noise; they criticize KAMENEV and me not for what ought to be criticized, but because people are not sufficiently steadfast.

Let us say that these people have exhausted themselves. The party apparatus is not bringing genuine fighters to the fore. They place their hopes on young people, on those prepared to grant an amnesty to KAMENEV and me.

TOMSKY, BUKHARIN, RYKOV — where do they stand? With them, generally speaking, the platform is embodied in the so-called Leninist Politburo, which is sufficiently well known to you.

ZINOVIEV: (continues) LOMINADZE, SHATSKIN \[41\], those groups moving away from the Central Committee's position are essentially seeking us out. SHLIAPNIKOV, who claims that he was right against LENIN, also partly gravitates toward us and seeks us out. SAFAROV likewise. In a word, all or almost all of them. During the investigation I was asked about EISMONT and TOLMACHEV \[42\], but I did not know them. In general, all anti-party groups at that time were trying to raise me, KAMENEV, and the group present here as their banner. Was that accidental? Of course not. Many distrusted us because \<we\> had not opposed the Bolshevik Central Committee decisively enough. We did not establish a practical organization with them. Why? For several reasons. Of course, one of the most important was that STEN was caught and KAMENEV and I were exiled. The main reason was political: the Central Committee opposed both the right and left deviations of the time, saying that sooner or later they would converge somewhere, and events themselves quickly became matters of plain fact. Another reason why this did not come about in practice was our cowardice: we still occupied the position of double-dealers. We peered and watched, but there were no actions ready to be carried out. At that time my feelings, too, were as anti-party as could be; my remarks, expressions, and so forth against the party leadership, especially against Comrade STALIN, were most venomous. That followed from the situation, and I cannot deny it. It really was so, and in this respect 1932 was one of my most inglorious years. Then came exile. It would be wrong to imagine that I regarded it merely as an episode after which I would come back and resume my work. How did a person regard exile before the 1917 revolution? I thought: they will send me into exile; I will return and take up the old work again. It was painful and made me think very seriously, for really grave matters were at stake: one more step and the connection would be severed forever. The events taking place were of enormous importance. In the spring of 1933 there was a certain turning point. In exile, Comrade STALIN's report on the results of the First Five-Year Plan played an enormous role for me. I do not mean to say that it brought me to my senses, that I was reborn, that all I had been missing was STALIN's report and that everything vanished as if by magic; but I must say that the report made an immense impression on me. It compelled me to reconsider and rethink a great many things in circumstances particularly conducive to doing so. The second decisive event for me was what happened in Germany. More than once I had said that the Comintern was to blame for failing to see what was coming, and that I would have done better. All that is true; I made such statements. But beyond that, HITLER came to power, creating a new situation for the world working class as well. It created new and immense dangers and, naturally, beyond double-dealing, wretched gossip, and the constant thought that I had been a leader and should be one again, there had been other thoughts over the course of ten years; now a danger approached that forced me to reconsider my thinking in another direction. These two facts — the end of the First Five-Year Plan and the spring of 1933, together with events in Germany — had, I believe, a politically salutary effect on me; not that I was cured, but they did exert a certain healing influence. Besides, there was no other path.

I wrote a letter that opened the way back into the party for me \[43\].

I wrote it in such a manner that it came out like a popular pamphlet any worker could understand. Of course, it contained a great deal of ambiguity and double-dealing. Part of what I wrote was dictated by necessity: I submitted, but did not bring my beliefs into submission. Much of it was true, and much made me reconsider things and gave me a sense that I had pulled that thorn halfway out. I returned to Moscow genuinely intending to put an end to all factional and duplicitous work, to all cliques, all attacks, and so forth, but that did not last long.

KAMENEV returned in the same frame of mind, under the influence of the same events. We told one another that we did not share that course of action, and this was entirely sincere. Here the subjective, psychological sphere also begins. I am speaking before the Military Session of the Supreme Court and have been called upon to explain, not to excuse, that the psychology of those whom you wish to understand consisted precisely in the thought that now it was all over. But what was to be done about the network of one's contacts? This is what I am now finishing saying, after suffering greatly and realizing that it was inevitable: if you wish to end factional work in a proletarian country, you must understand that you will emerge as a comrade, a representative of the working class.

Now, so to speak, not of my own free will but under the impact of all those blows, I understood everything. Understanding was the hardest, most agonizing part. Two things had to be done. A statement had to be made proving to everyone — to LOMINADZE, to the SAFAROVS, to all anti-party elements in the country and abroad — that this man was no longer theirs. Such statements had to be made.

I made them, not sincerely enough, but I made them. And because they lacked sufficient sincerity, everyone immediately sensed it. The main misfortune was that I could not bring myself to take the second step I ought to have taken: to hand over the whole network of my contacts to the organs of the working class. I will not unravel the thread of the psychology of why this was so, and so \<forth\>. It is not hard for you to guess, and it is not worth a broken penny. But that is how it was.

I went round and round the subject, thinking myself fortunate to have exposed this person and that one without their even requiring it. I went to YAGODA himself; I approached him with an intellectual sort of conversation, with a touch of Dostoevskian soul-searching: you know what kind of person I am, you know that people will come to see me — what would you advise me to do?... In short, I beat about the bush.

He is a practical man, with other business to attend to, and politely said, “Yes, if they should come...”.

I imagined that by reporting this I was making things easier in some way. I did not go and say that in Moscow there was, in fact, a group that met and maintained contact with me... and so on and so forth. Instead of saying or writing that, I had a conversation that accomplished nothing.

I remember (I think this is how it happened) that KAMENEV and I decided he too would approach Comrade AGRANOV on the same matter. That was the maximum, the pinnacle of party-mindedness we reached: going to people and trying, as it were, to take off a skin without getting it wet.

And so, if you want to draw an instructive lesson from all this, independently of everything else... For one must tell other people who still imagine themselves politicians rather than defendants and prisoners that, if they wish to save themselves and remain in the camp of the working class, if they do not want the party to have to crush them, they must take this elementary step. Do not say that the GPU must not interfere in party affairs: it too is a party organ.

Take this elementary step — it is a thousand times more important than the others.

...I remember very well the phrase I uttered after Hitler: “God grant that we build socialism in one country.” It seemed to me that there had been such an enormous change in the balance of popular forces that no other words could express it. KAMENEV reproached me in a friendly way for that phrase. And when he looked at me as if to say, “Do you really think that?”, I had no genuine answer. The genuine answer should have been, “Comrade EVDOKIMOV, let us go together to YAGODA and tell him the whole truth.” But that good intention began to evaporate rapidly in the Moscow atmosphere. Once again came the thirst to know everything: how could I not know everything going on in the Comintern, how could I not know everything happening in matters of world politics, and so \<forth\>?

I did not understand that I had been politically bankrupt since 1928, that I had to atone for my sins...

And so it gradually began again, merely in a different form, but essentially the same thing. With a subjective colouring in which personal considerations predominated...

My personal psychology naturally played a part in this.

It seemed to me that I was speaking with complete sincerity when I addressed the Seventeenth Party Congress. “Nowadays it is customary (or so I thought) to praise Stalin too much. Well, I shall do it too,” as though adapting myself in matters of style, without realizing that this was not a matter of style, without realizing that it was necessary, as necessary as daily bread; that this was no kind of cult, but that the situation now required explaining to the masses wherein his greatness lay. Yet I considered it a concession to the times. Essentially, however, I believed that what I was saying corresponded to the party's requirements, that I was doing it sincerely, that it was the right thing to do and useful to the party. Certainly it was not the case that I finished my speech and contradicted it the following morning.

This is no longer that same party, but the Bolshevik party of the dictatorship of the proletariat. For once one has arrived at this final and irreversible understanding, this inner recognition of STALIN's greatness that now exists among all the people and throughout the country, how could one go back to reviling him? Such is my psychology, as it stands.

When you first asked about the center, I initially thought: what center, what part could I have played in it? Asking what center you meant, I could not picture it: it did not fit either the years or the history as I knew it. What kind of center could it be? Had we been discussing this before December 1934, had Comrade KIROV's portrait not been draped in mourning, one might still have imagined that things would somehow have dissolved of their own accord, and so forth. Clearly one must count such a mistake as one's fault. No faction ever simply dissolves that way; before our eyes, in history, no faction or grouping has completely disappeared in one way or another, and that is how one can end up in the counterrevolutionary camp.

At first I thought: what sort of center is this? It is not the center that existed in 1925–27, because circumstances are different. If there was a center, there was no platform. There was the idea that contacts and cadres had to be preserved, and so on. For a time they would say, “You will lose your cadres,” and we would say, “No, you will lose yours.” It was a question of the expediency of retaining one or another counterrevolutionary group. There was a wish to preserve the so-called cadres. Of course we picked up every supposed piece of news — in ordinary human language, counterrevolutionary rumors and gossip — and passed them on. What is this called in Bolshevik language, in political language? It is called counterrevolution.

That center did exist, and existed until the very last moment. A decision was made in 1929 to disperse. It grew thinner in numbers, and many went through the same psychological process, but in practice things turned out differently. There can be no doubt that, in any new period of difficulties, all party and anti-party groups would seek links with counterrevolutionary groups, particularly with me, in order to repeat what happened in 1932 on a larger scale.

Now as to its composition. Up to 1929 it was clearly defined: KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, SHAROV, KHARITONOV, LASHEVICH, FEDOROV; after that it became somewhat smaller. They were not regarded as members of a center in the sense of an organizing force, a center that viewed itself politically as a center; nevertheless, in actual fact it performed the functions of one.

Among the newer, or relatively newer, people others drew closer — GESSEN was also close to us and was considered a young force; he was close not because he was a leader, but because he was among the more acceptable people.

That was the composition, its basic nucleus. You know all of them. Many are sitting before you, plainly exposed. They themselves will confirm what I have described, and many will go down in history. It could only have been a center that played an inglorious role. This group carried out a counterrevolutionary function for a certain period.

That is what I wanted to say. The second and most important question concerns the Leningrad organization. I consider it important to emphasize the history of the “young” leftists, the “leaderless” people. They had a certain significance, although they were not widely known in the party. This is very important to note and to outline briefly. It was a group made up chiefly of Komsomol members, who generally played a major role in the whole intervention at the Fourteenth Party Congress. Although I bore the greatest blame, precisely for my wavering during that congress, I was pushed forward more than I advanced of my own accord. On the eve of the opening of the Fourteenth Congress, when, at the initiative of the late Comrade DZERZHINSKY, an enlarged Politburo met and KAMENEV and I were summoned, we were told: “The congress opens tomorrow. We have an overwhelming majority; you will be beaten. We propose that you do not make a counter-report, do not start a fight at the congress or shake the party, but submit.” We were told: give up the counter-report, change the editorial board's composition, and hand over SAFAROV — that is, renounce him politically. I relate this not to defend myself, but because it leads to the question of the leaderless people. SARKIS was one of the leading officials; SAFAROV was an agitator and editor and also a principal mainstay. This assessment was made not only by the young people but by the leaderless group as well. What was the psychology of that group at the time? The young people seemed valuable to us then; among them was the NEP scum of that generation, which seized upon every word directed against the party and its leadership. By the very moment of the Fourteenth Congress we had reached a compromise with them in order to reject the political compromise. I rejected it, KAMENEV rejected it, and all the comrades rejected the proposal made by DZERZHINSKY on the eve of the Fourteenth Congress, which had the sympathy of all bureau members, in order to maintain the compromise with the leaderless group. That is how it happened; in this way we maintained the so-called unity rather than carrying out anti-party struggle at the Fourteenth Congress.

On the other hand, we assumed an enormous responsibility, I in particular. I was the first to demonstrate anti-party struggle to people, including those very young people. Many of you remember the scene at the Fourteenth Congress, when a small group stood against the entire party. That was my practical lesson, which subsequently developed into counterrevolution. That group connected with me, which I called the scum of the NEP, subsequently played a very substantial role. I will not recount the whole history between the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Congresses, since you have not asked me that question and know it well enough. But immediately before the Fifteenth Congress an outright split occurred between the so-called Zinovievites in the strict sense and those leaderless people, who openly rebelled, setting off an internal discussion. A genuine split took place. On the whole, that group was insignificant. Later the split healed and we came together again, but the scar remained. You know the history of previous factions: when a rupture occurs, it never heals completely, and neither did this one. After Safarov's exile, the leader of these leaderless people was RUMIANTSEV; KOTOLYNOV played an important role, as far as I understood from the indictment in the case of Comrade KIROV's murder. We sought a rapprochement with them, and many of them looked down on EVDOKIMOV and wanted to deal only with me. They turned all our accusations of bureaucratism against our own group, accusing EVDOKIMOV and BAKAEV of it. We courted them and reached agreement that there would be a discussion, and so forth. This is important for history. That group subsequently played a very considerable role here \[44\].

Everything I said about the desire to preserve cadres in order to spread rumors and secure a change of leadership applied to Leningrad, indeed first and foremost. In 1932, when there was a general revival of activity and an attempt to reach an agreement, which I described in greater detail in my written testimony, we naturally began to look at what was happening there. I met LEVIN in 1932, but we had no organizational discussions. He understood me at half a word; I was an authority to him and he was an authority to me. I knew that this man from the “leaderless” group would act as we directed, and so forth. Was he an authorized representative of the Moscow Center? He was never formally appointed. No one passed such a resolution, but in substance he was. In fact, we all regarded him as the most dependable man. BAKAEV knew him especially well, and I knew him very well too. He was one of the principal organizers of anti-party struggle in Leningrad, seemed a mature man, and in fact we saw him as someone who could maintain contacts and so \<forth\>.

I gave him no assignments. I did not instruct him to form a center or meet anyone, and so forth, but that followed from what I said. I told him my assessment, my view of conditions in the countryside, which I shared with all those of counterrevolutionary sympathies. I repeated to him completely false figures about how many people had died of hunger in Ukraine, and so on. I certainly told him that. I cannot now remember when he visited me. I cannot say, because I do not wish to assert anything I cannot remember with complete clarity. I do not remember whether he visited me before or after the Ryutin platform. I seem to recall that it was before, because I saw him in the summer, whereas I received the platform after people dispersed from their dachas, when the rains had already begun.

I say this because, had I seen him after that platform, I would undoubtedly have told him about it, since I did not consider it a great secret.

In general, I conveyed to him the whole tenor of my political feelings: the mood of panic, the lack of faith in the enormous operation undertaken by the party, all that bitterness, and the desire to whisper with all the anti-party groupings. Perhaps I did so cautiously, in diplomatic terms, but I did convey it. And naturally I understood that he would repeat it all to someone he trusted. I have no doubt about that.

It is also known that before my departure into exile, through LEVIN this whole collection of attitudes went from me to Leningrad. But I was convinced that they had no center. To be sure, I thought the same about ourselves.

Of course I knew that quite a number of people had gathered there, that many returned to Leningrad after having spent several years elsewhere, and that LEVIN knew many people.

In 1933 and 1934 I did not actually see a single person from Leningrad; I cannot now recall any such occasion... And because of that tangle of feelings I have described, because of the twofold feeling that I had to pull the thorn out at all costs, and so \<forth\>... well, I knew in 1933–34, I knew perfectly well, of course, that these elements existed, that nobody had dis\{solved\}\*solved\* them, not formally, that is, but also that they had not once heard from me that it was time to stop, that we had been wrong, that the Central Committee and Stalin had been right, and so forth.

Of course, had I thought it through, I should have understood this, but I treated the matter frivolously, gave it little thought, and it went no further than an attempt to seek YAGODA's advice.

Of course I could not fail to understand that these elements existed, but I was convinced there was no organization there. So when I write that I knew nothing of any formal organization or the existence of a center in Leningrad, I was telling the truth. It is not in my interest to muddle things. I know that I could only complicate my own case. I understand that I am in a rather unfavorable position, that everyone understands that, yes, the fact existed. The fact existed\! But I did not know that fact, that a constituted organization existed or who belonged to it.

It sounds contradictory, like a mosaic, as Comrade AGRANOV said after one of the interrogations. True, a mosaic, but a mosaic drawn from life. It does nothing to help me; it can only worsen my position, in the sense that “even at the very last moment you refuse to tell the truth.” But I am telling everything as it was.

I could not fail to understand; with even a little effort against my own frivolity, I should have realized what I had overlooked, that there were active people there, and so on and so forth. I spoke with LEVIN in 1932, but did not know there was a center there or who belonged to it. No organization was discussed.

As for the “leaderless” people, when I thought of them I supposed they were the very people who had become most involved in party work. They were readmitted to the party more readily: they were younger, more energetic, rank-and-file members, workers, and so on. Thus, if I thought of them at all, I assumed they probably had indeed returned.

As regards 1933–34, I went nowhere and knew nothing. I knew of EVDOKIMOV's trip, that he had been to one of the factories; he came back and told us about it, but not with any such information. He spoke chiefly about how he had visited Smolny, how he had been received, how the late KIROV had asked whether ZINOVIEV and the c\<omrades\> \[45\] could be trusted, and so on. In 1933–34 I heard nothing from there, and no one came to see me.

That is how matters stood.

CHAIRMAN: Nevertheless, your Leningrad associates considered you to be their leader?

ZINOVIEV: I consider it the court's task to determine whether what I say is true or false, what corresponds to reality and what does not. I cannot take all the responsibility for Leningrad. Naturally, I could not have been unaware of their existence as individuals; but what existed there and what it was called, I did not know. Yet whereas my general attitude in 1933–34 was as passive as could be in this respect, theirs was quite the opposite. In this case the two diverged, and the contradictions I spoke of arose; but it was a contradiction that really existed in life.

Much of this arose because double-dealing lay at the foundation. Had there been complete honesty within the party, there could have been no ambiguity.

CHAIRMAN: Tell us about the “Leningrad Center.”

ZINOVIEV: I now turn to the chief and fundamental subject: an event of worldwide significance, not only for our country, with political and moral importance. I also understand the state of mind of every person, every comrade who, with all their traditions and feelings, is overwhelmed by the knowledge that Comrade KIROV has been murdered; that knowledge is a tragic fact. We are double-dealers, that is true. Let me say a few words about another aspect. It is true that at first there was fierce struggle. I shall speak about my personal attitude toward Comrade KIROV. Was there much bitterness against him? Of course there was. Then came the story of rumors circulated about his biography. I did not spread them. At that time ZALUTSKY became involved in that affair, I hurried to ORDZHONIKIDZE and begged for amnesties, and so forth. I was not the one who started those rumors. Was I KIROV's equal? Certainly not; that was the first stage. Later, in the early years, there was talk along these lines: KIROV \[46\] works, travels to Moscow; there was talk, yes. Nevertheless, KIROV \[47\] had won such an acknowledged position in recent years that even people who continued to sympathize with our counterrevolutionary platform said that no, he was a genuine man; in recent years there was no bitterness against him in our circle, in that group which we must call the “Moscow Center,” so much so \<that\> this was the case.

The point is that the man had established his standing; we understood that he was not the worst of our opponents. Who did not hear his speech at the Sixteenth Congress? KIROV said then: “ZINOVIEV seems to be beginning to understand how one ought to speak in the party” \[48\].

Everything we heard about Leningrad naturally concerns that group of people. But I have the full right, including the moral right, to say that during the last five years there was no special group directed against KIROV in the Moscow Center, nor could there have been one. On the contrary, the feeling was that he was a fair and objective man.

I learned on the sixteenth that Sergei Mironovich KIROV had been murdered by this gang. When I learned of it, I was very troubled about the background to it, and so on. My conjecture was that they were former counterrevolutionaries, and so forth.

When MOLCHANOV arrested me and asked whether I knew what had happened the day before, I answered that of course I did not: I had not attended the party activists' meeting, knew nothing about it, and had not heard that version. I was told, “You now accept moral responsibility for this murder,” but I replied in utter indignation: “How can I accept it? I can condemn it, meet it with curses, hatred, and so forth.” I was able to make the comparison and understand only after reading the indictment.

Only after reading the indictment did I learn that there were six people, by name: LEVIN, KOTOLYNOV, RUMIANTSEV, MANDELSTAM, MIASNIKOV, and TOLMAZOV. The last of these (TOLMAZOV) I regarded as a particularly quiet fellow, and \<that he\> possessed good qualities, or so it seemed to me.

I knew those six personally.

The last thing I must confirm is that I personally knew those six men.

Those six men were the ones to whom we first showed the way into anti-party struggle, beginning with the Fourteenth Congress and continuing up to November 7, and so forth. The third thing I learned was what these people invoked: they said they had acted in the name of the platform, and some even said they had acted to settle personal scores. Having absorbed and thought over these facts, I must state clearly that moral and political responsibility for this fascist, shameful, accursed horror rests upon the group that associated with them. A large proportion came from the “leaderless” group, but along what path had we led them, so that they put a leader out of action and proved to be fascists? I did not teach them that. Throughout my life I kept far from terrorist acts. However entangled I became, however many crimes I committed against the party, however deeply I remained mired in the swamp of counterrevolution, I never went that far. But people were found who took that step, and in that sense I bear responsibility. It was very difficult for me to acknowledge. Anyone who tries to think through this tragedy will see that its essence lies in the murder of Comrade KIROV. This admission was extremely painful to me, and I must repeat before the court that I must assume this responsibility, as must every one of us.

The indictment further states that I knew of the Moscow Center, including the terrorist sentiments. I said that I pleaded fully guilty but wanted to make a reservation concerning precisely that passage. It still seems to me, if I may put it this way, unfair, as though I knew of terrorist sentiments and encouraged them. I knew of anti-party sentiments. I now see that these words are too slight, too diplomatic; in essence, I knew that rumors were being circulated against the party leadership, I knew of the slander, but I did not interpret it as terrorist sentiment, and even if I inflamed it, I did not do so in the sense of imagining that it could lead to terrorism. That never entered my head. A whole month has now passed since Comrade KIROV's murder, and everyone lives under the impression of that event. We lived in circumstances in which there had been no assassination attempts, apart from the one against Comrade LENIN. That form of struggle did not exist, and people had even begun to forget about it. True, there had been the shooting of Vorovsky, but on the whole people had moved away from such things. Of course it would be very good to make even a small attempt to apply Marx's analysis to what happened: what had people's psychology been beforehand, when I inflamed those feelings, and what could I have understood by that word? The country was advancing, and on the day after bread ration cards were abolished that shot was fired at Comrade KIROV. Of course, this can be explained very well, and it was explained long ago: precisely when the enemy had essentially been defeated, when the working class was overcoming obstacles with iron strength and pressing forward with tremendous force, those slanderous words rang out.

I tell the court that I knew of the anti-party sentiments; I see where they led, and it is a fact that they led to this result. But until then it had never occurred to me that this was called terrorism. I knew it was anti-party, but I did not know I was inflaming terrorist sentiments. That was not the case. I must say that this is how it turned out in practice. It would be absurd to raise an abstract question such as: had there been no Moscow Center, had we laid down our arms five years earlier, could some group have arisen in Petersburg? That is an abstract question, whereas the court is interested not in an abstract possibility but in what actually happened. In reality there was such a group, and those people were among the ones whom we first led into anti-party struggle. They were among those with whom we failed to break, whom we concealed from the party, many of whom we knew — and I personally knew six of them. True, I had not seen them for a long time, but the fact remains that I knew them and in that sense was connected with them; and in that sense I offer up my head...

CHAIRMAN: You must also speak of those in whom you stirred up discontent and bitterness toward the party leadership.

ZINOVIEV: Yes, quite right, but I did not realize where it would lead. I wanted to touch on one more small question.

When I was arrested, I was stunned and immediately wrote a note to Stalin (I do not know whether he received it), saying that in recent years I considered myself guilty of nothing, neither in deed nor in thought \[49\].

When I learned all the circumstances of KIROV's murder (for I had written the note before I knew), I was constantly troubled by what I had written. What had it represented on my part? On the one hand it was an impulse, because at the time I genuinely considered myself innocent. I had not seen anyone for a long time; I had not spoken at the congress because I believed my address had been sincere; I dreamed that the period of political estrangement would somehow come to an end. For all these reasons I genuinely considered myself innocent, and I wrote the letter on a sincere impulse.

Objectively, of course, that was not so. Certainly, during those years too there was much that was anti-party in my thoughts and words. I did not regard it as counterrevolutionary, still less as terrorist, and so forth. Only now, under the weight of the murder itself, under the weight of everything I have heard here... For instance, what I heard about NATANSON was news to me; I knew him and thought he was a Trotskyist; I never knew that I could in any measure be held responsible for him \[50\], or for LUKIN, whom I considered a thoroughgoing Trotskyist. In practice, however, a tangled web of relationships emerged.

These are, of course, details that do not change the essentials.

That is everything I wanted to tell the proletarian court. If anything needs to be added, please ask.

My wish is to tell everything to the very end... I am filled with genuine repentance. I see where this affair has led. If I speak of my sincere intentions, my devotion and the \<rest\>, it is not to move anyone to tears, because the only thing left to me is this: if the court, if the party, gives me even the slightest opportunity, or any opportunity at all, to atone, on my knees if necessary, for the enormous and heavy guilt I bear before the working class and the party, I shall be profoundly grateful.

INTERROGATION

## OF LEV BORISOVICH KAMENEV

CHAIRMAN: Defendant KAMENEV, what can you say concerning the charge brought against you?

KAMENEV: I would not wish to occupy the court with lengthy declarations for which there is now neither time nor place. I shall therefore begin by stating that I agree entirely and fully, down to the last formulation, with the analysis of the activity of the counterrevolutionary group to which I belong, made here by defendant EVDOKIMOV.

It seems to me that two points in his speech were emphasized with particular importance and success:

THE FIRST is the error and harmfulness of dividing into stages the activity of the organization for participation in and leadership of which I find myself in the dock. I myself tried to take that path during interrogation. Why? The reason is obvious and need not be explained. Legally, one might attempt to prove that my participation was extensive at one stage, less extensive at another, and very slight during the last two years.

But this is not a legal trial; it is a political trial. I have one last opportunity to address the court of the proletariat, the party — and through this proletarian court the entire country — and I do not wish to burden either the court or those who will take an interest in my fate with my personal impressions or attempts to diminish my guilt.

In fact, much of what SAFAROV said here and what GORSHENIN said here was unknown to me. But this can now have no significance for me, and I fully understand that neither the investigation nor the court will focus its attention on it.

Yes, I did not know there was a Moscow Center. But what follows from that? It did exist — it has been proved. Tomorrow the whole country will be convinced that this center existed, while I, you see, did not know. This only shows that I was blind; only that, having lived more than fifty years, lived through three revolutions, and now being imprisoned for the eighth time, I managed to become so blind that I failed to see this political center, which I myself had actively helped by actions and inaction, by words and silence.

I can say that I did not know about the connection between Moscow and Leningrad. But I ought to have known\!

If I drew any political conclusions from the political experience of three revolutions, I ought to have taken an interest in what those people were doing whom I had educated for eight years, and from whom I withdrew in the ninth. Ought I not to have taken steps to come and stamp into the ground the harmful seeds that I had sown? I did not do so. Therefore what significance could my statement have, apart from a legal significance unworthy of the circumstances in which we find ourselves? I remember the situation in the country and all the attempts to reduce my participation in this affair. If it were possible historically to divide our organization's past and separate the years 1930, 1932 and 1933, the shot of December 1 merged all those stages into one and coloured it in a single colour from beginning to end. For me, after everything I learned following my arrest in this case, from the investigation materials I examined and from what I have heard here now, this is the only path leading to the pit in which we now find ourselves. I follow EVDOKIMOV as well in moving from the subjective to the objective discussions supported by ZINOVIEV. Subjectively, we wanted to serve the working class, but we served counterrevolution. We should not be, we do not deserve to be, even ordinary party members; nothing testifies to that more clearly. What conclusion can be drawn? We were regarded as diplomats who for many years served as guides of the working class; yet no one seems to have considered us fools throughout those years, while it turns out that for more than eight or ten years we served counterrevolution. It is like replacing solitary confinement with a lunatic asylum on grounds of feeble-mindedness.

It would be strange to deny that the tremendous processes that have occurred in our country over ten years — and I personally regard industrialization as achievable — took place under STALIN's leadership in the highest combination of those two enormous tasks. That is without mentioning that the undertaking of industrialization was in itself unheard of and that the party possessed LENIN's idea concerning industrialization (the matter of collectivization had already been begun under him); yet in any country, especially one such as ours, that other task had not even been posed, theoretically worked out, or begun. Thus, while those two tasks were being carried out, while the maneuver consisted in conducting them in such harmony as not to shake the country apart, it was impossible to suppose that all counterrevolutionary tendencies would not consolidate and grow stronger. Everything said about our candidacies and the struggle to win us over only shows that counterrevolution chose precisely us, however shameful it \<was\> for us, as an instrument in its struggle against the proletarian revolution.

Those blocs that people tried to establish, and that accompanied the whole course of our history, were of course not accidental: those elements of the counterrevolutionary classes and those restorationist tendencies wanted to fight against the dictatorship of the proletariat under one banner or another. This was expressed in our approaching each group without adopting the standpoint and test that LENIN taught us: “If you speak with a political group, first ask for its program.” Yet we asked no one, neither the Ryutin group nor anyone else.

SAFAROV, who here depicted this most important historical process, significant not just for us but for the future development of the world revolution — a process from which people must learn in order to avoid it in future — presented a sort of novel in which he played some ringleader's part, discussing who got in first, who second (into the railway carriage). I do not say this to excuse myself. Previously I did not remember, but now I recall that ZINOVIEV once told me SAFAROV had visited him and proposed some sort of bloc. I said that I did not want to enter any bloc and would not take part in anything with SAFAROV, for I had never trusted that man. ZINOVIEV can confirm it. I was not opposed to conversations: I spoke with TOLMAZOV \[51\], LOMINADZE and SHATSKIN \[52\]; but I did not want a conversation of the kind SAFAROV proposed. Whether or not I wished to talk, the essential issue was whether our aims coincided. Whether or not we dissolved ourselves, we saw no other path than the one that wa\<s\> outlined within those counterrevolutionary anti-party groups. For that alliance to find support, we began with difficulties; we played upon the difficulties of the proletarian dictatorship. The whole course of events cannot be imagined in any other way. It must be said plainly: we staked our hopes on difficulties. We collected facts in the hope that the Central Committee might begin negotiations, which was our secret dream, or we wanted to force it to negotiate with us. I personally wrote of my feelings that I always tried to reach the point at which the Central Committee itself would want to negotiate with us. I cannot say that I held the position mentioned by someone here, and I am truly shaken by how much I did not know. ANISHEV spoke here; this is the first time I have seen him and the first time I have heard his surname. He said that people were counting on strikes and the peasant movement. That is of no consequence for my political line or my personal fate. I must say that I am not a coward by nature, but I never counted on armed struggle. I always lived in the expectation that a situation would arise in which the Central Committee would be forced to negotiate with us, make room for us, and give us a place. That was not so at all times, comrades; during the last two years I had no such dreams, simply because I am neither a dreamer nor a fantasist. There were fantasists and adventurers among us, but I do not belong to them.

In 1932–33 I understood that we had no chance, no conditions in which to struggle against the party. I began to withdraw from this. I placed my personal hopes elsewhere, on the path of development and integration into the party. Here I committed the greatest error, because \{those\} crimes and mistakes \{which\} arose from my past between 1925 and 1932 had been repeatedly discussed by the party. If we take the party and congress resolutions and read them, we shall find a complete analysis of our actions. My other error was that, once I had become disillusioned with our methods, I thought I could simply leave, that my personal withdrawal would somehow free me of responsibility for everything I knew, whether or not I had withdrawn. It made no difference whether I had begun to write about literature. The bitterness bore the poisonous fruit we know. That fruit was not accidental, and until very recently I was under the greatest delusion, imagining I could shut myself off from it by ceasing to attend meetings and stopping seeing certain comrades. When I did meet them, I did not consider what political idea was involved. When I saw that there was no political idea, that people did not know what they wanted, that our meetings had become a negation of any political program, I began to draw away. I was sincere in withdrawing, but my crime consists in wanting to withdraw \[53\] without settling my personal and political accounts, which I should have settled. But this — as ZINOVIEV says, and just as I concurred in EVDOKIMOV's characterization — I cannot accept as ZINOVIEV presents it. It is not that I attach importance to certain historical memories connected with personal psychological explanations. It is a narrow conclusion to say that one must denounce and reveal the secret: that would be a good thing if followed by the real disclosure of the whole affair, but it was not. It might have been justified yesterday or the day before, and historically justified too. Today we are in the dock, and tomorrow the whole world proletariat will learn of it. If anyone in the proletariat takes the path of opposition, he must reveal his secrets. That is a stunted conclusion, and it is wrong and mistaken to put it forward without applying it to oneself.

In my testimony I did not follow the shortest route. I do not want to burden the court; it will understand how and why this happened. I could not simply take this enormous crime upon myself; I did not know that I might be arrested in connection with this case and only learned so when I was arrested. While I was in custody, before I read the indictment, when the comrades questioned me during interrogation, I could not imagine it. I say this not to excuse myself, but to explain the long course of my testimony. I told the truth; if I forgot anything, I am ready to add it. I remember organizational matters poorly, since I was never an organizer and had no interest in such matters. In recent years I took no interest in them. But there is one point in this testimony about which I kept silent because I thought ZINOVIEV should mention it. I listened to ZINOVIEV's speech with trepidation. I waited to see whether his conduct would force me to speak, and in the end it did. I must say it because I consider that the tactic he himself recommends, of which he spoke so much — that everything must be said to the very end — was not demanded merely today or tomorrow; we are already too late. I am sitting in this dock because I was late in doing what I myself felt deep down ought to be done.

So it must be done today. Yes, not put off until tomorrow, because we failed to do today what we ought to have done, and the following day NIKOLAEV's shot rang out.

Why then, ZINOVIEV, if you genuinely intend to carry everything through to the end, did you not say what you reveled in, what literature you reveled in during the very last days of our meetings? Why did you not say how you received and assessed your removal from the journal “Bolshevik,” and what political conclusion you drew from it? That mattered for characterizing your state of mind at the very end.

I cannot recall individual conversations from 1929, 1930, 1931 or 1932, but I remember what happened in August, September, October and November 1934. Is it not true that you assessed your removal from “Bolshevik” not as the result of your own error, but as further proof of our old counterrevolutionary notion of Stalin's personal dictatorship, of a personal arbitrariness that allowed him to cast people aside and command not only in practical but also in political matters?

Did you not tell me at that time that Stalin was conducting this revision of questions concerning the ideological heritage of Marx and Engels arbitrarily, wrongly, and in an un-Leninist manner; that he contradicted Lenin's principles on the matter not only in form, but that you could prove with quotations from Lenin, Marx, and Engels that Stalin was wrong, and that the only reason you did not prove it was that there was no room for theoretical work?

I do not know how long you intended to postpone that admission. I think you ought to have made it today, if only to confirm by a single deed the general declaration you made.

But did not your removal from “Bolshevik” lead you, after obtaining access to Trotskyist literature, to tell me about Trotsky's plans in a manner that can only be described as sharply anti-party, directly opposed to what you said in your speech to the Seventeenth Congress and in earlier statements?

And if I say this, it is only because I believe there must be an end, a point at which everything must be honestly disclosed. Or will that end never come? What is there left to wait for?

I do not want to wait for anything... At the Seventeenth Congress I used the phrase that I no longer wished to go on carrying a donkey's skin \[54\]. At the time this caused a stir in the hall and was not fully understood. The fault was mine, of course, because there too I failed to speak to the very end.

But you, ZINOVIEV, ought to have known that when I told the Seventeenth Congress I wished to cast off the donkey's skin, I meant you — the Leningrad people — the alliance with whom was truly the most accursed day of my life, an alliance which I subsequently dragged along through weakness and inertia because I had no other milieu, and from which I had lately been withdrawing.

ZINOVIEV must say that when we were going to the Seventeenth Congress I said I would begin my speech with these words: “I speak for myself alone; there is nobody behind me or alongside me.”

But the fact that I did not dare... because I fully realized that the Leningrad group led by ZINOVIEV had embarked on a counterrevolutionary path and continued along it despite all the proof that it was false, continuing without any idea, any definite goal, or even any determination to struggle (for one can know how to struggle even for an unjust cause), reducing political struggle to the most miserable, almost domestic forms — petty intrigues — when I felt this, I tried to leave. I did not leave because I lacked the strength to break away from that counterrevolutionary group.

Whether I knew of the Leningrad organization or not, I consider myself, here before the portraits of these great builders of socialism, a criminal because I lacked the strength to leave myself and take with me those whom I could have taken. It seems to me that had I mustered enough strength to raise the question radically within the group, with even a tenth of the energy used in the struggle against the party, I could have taken people like SHAROV and BAKAEV with me.

That is why I bear the curse not only of Comrade KIROV's corpse, but also of the fact that these comrades are sitting here.

INTERROGATION

## OF ARTEM MOISEEVICH GERTIK

CHAIRMAN: Defendant GERTIK, tell us: was your apartment in fact the headquarters of this organization or was it not? What was your work? Were you a member of the “Moscow Center”?

GERTIK: In 1926–27, up to the Fifteenth–Sixteenth Congress, my apartment was the only headquarters in Moscow, and the whole Zinovievite organization that existed in Moscow, arrived from the provinces or Leningrad, or lived in Moscow passed through my hands.

At that time, immediately after the Fourteenth Congress, a Zinovievite center was organized. I do not remember how many people joined it, but it consisted exclusively of members of the Central Committee and the Central Control Commission, although others sometimes attended its meetings. A center of Zinovievites and Trotskyists was established. SMILGA was also there. I was not a member of that center, but when its members were absent I would join it.

CHAIRMAN: As an alternate member of the center? For whom did you think it necessary to vote?

GERTIK: For ZINOVIEV, of course. There was discipline then; there was an organization that talked and elaborated a whole theory.

After the Fifteenth Congress the organization was dissolved in the outlying areas, and so forth.

I was sent to Tashkent around January 15, where I spent two years. I returned to Moscow only in November 1929. So when ZINOVIEV speaks of the center that existed in 1928–29, I was not there, although I remained close to it and played the part of an organizer. Certainly I was not in Moscow; I was in Tashkent. I visited Moscow once, in July 1927, stayed one day, and went away on holiday.

After returning from leave I visited ZINOVIEV in Kaluga and then went to Leningrad, but at that time I carried out no organizational work. My apartment remained a center; my wife lived there, and she too was a member of the Zinovievite group. In that respect everything was tightly arranged: all the wives were with us. In keeping with the old tradition, many people passed through my apartment; those engaged in organizational work met there, and this was precisely the time when the core group that constituted the Zinovievite group was consolidating. I cannot recall exactly who was in Moscow besides ZINOVIEV — perhaps GORSHENIN, KHARITONOV and FEDOROV.

When I returned to Moscow in 1929, I immediately renewed contact with ZINOVIEV and the Zinovievites there. The apartment continued to be a center, but there was no organizational work then, only everyday activity. There were no new people, so our task was to preserve our cadres, keep in touch with them and keep track of them.

CHAIRMAN: Who was in charge of cadres among you?

GERTIK: I do not know what happened in 1928–29, but when I returned we had no formally constituted center in charge of that. KHARITONOV and I dealt with it; whoever came to see you was told the latest news, and that was all.

The question of preserving cadres was not simply a matter of retaining contact with an individual, but of preserving a person with particular views and qualities, maintaining in him the conviction that the government would fail to cope with governing the country and that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV would therefore come to power. What were your leaders' slogans?

We preserved cadres for two purposes. First, if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were brought into the leadership without any struggle, they needed people around them; they should not enter alone as a pair but have loyal people of the same political outlook who could support them.

Second, we spread rumors of disagreements within the Politburo and the Central Committee. I did not invent those rumors, but I circulated them, and others circulated them too. We received them from various sources. If the members of the center issued directives, if we had well-trained cadres and carried out our assignments, we could come forward more or less armed — that was why we preserved cadres. But I had no special function assigning me responsibility for them. Until 1930 this was convenient in practical terms as well: the apartment had a separate entrance. After 1930 I moved into a new apartment whose landlord was a Communist, so we had to scale down our activity. We had to establish clandestine arrangements.

CHAIRMAN: To whom did your functions pass after your departure?

GERTIK: Some functions remained with me. In 1930 many people visited me. There were considerably fewer in 1931 and even fewer in 1932. Personally, I believe that from 1932 onward my apartment could in no way be regarded as a center. I seldom saw ZINOVIEV, but Muscovites still visited the apartment; large numbers could not gather, so groups of five met and discussed counterrevolutionary matters.

CHAIRMAN: How many times did you travel to Leningrad?

GERTIK: In the summer of 1930 I went to Leningrad but saw nobody; evidently nobody was there at the time. In 1931 I went twice. The first time I saw TSVIBAK, and the second time BASHKIROV and LEVIN. I remember they came to my sister's apartment, where I was staying. In 1932, between January and June, I visited five times. I did not go to Leningrad after June. I was there on OGIZ business, under a decree of the Council of People's Commissars. I cannot say I used the trip solely for our purposes, since, had ZINOVIEV or EVDOKIMOV told me I needed to travel, I would have arranged a fictitious business trip and gone. On this occasion, therefore, I travelled on institutional business while also using the trip to maintain contacts with Leningrad.

During these visits I met LEVIN twice. Of the five journeys, on two I met nobody because it was technically impossible, and it was also late July when everyone was away. On the other three occasions I saw LEVIN; twice I went to his apartment. We called on BASHKIROV and then went to LEVIN, who always had a new apartment. Once BASHKIROV, LEVIN, MIASNIKOV and I met together; MIASNIKOV had been specially invited, since I had not seen him for several years. Another time I went to BASHKIROV's place, where MIRONOV happened to be present quite by chance; to this day I do not know his political convictions. He was from Leningrad before the Fifteenth Congress, but afterward I lost track of him. In any event, when I arrived they were discussing work matters. One was in charge of bread baking and the other of transport, so they had their own professional business to discuss. But MIRONOV evidently remained a Zinovievite, because LEVIN did not warn me to be cautious with him. I happened to meet PERIMOV in Moscow, but we drank a great deal and no conversation took place.

CHAIRMAN: Did members of the Leningrad Center visit Moscow and receive directives?

GERTIK: Yes, they visited Moscow. LEVIN himself, and KORSHUNOV of the Leningrad Center, maintained contact with Moscow until 1932. That cannot be denied.

CHAIRMAN: Until the very latest period?

GERTIK: After 1932 I met no one from Leningrad, either in Moscow or Leningrad. Once I visited TSVIBAK, but we did not discuss those matters because outsiders were present, and I met no one else. After 1932 I knew absolutely nothing.

I want to say that although I did not meet them, other members of our organization did. After 1932 we reduced and changed our activity, but as the organization continued to exist there is reason to suppose that contact between Moscow and Leningrad persisted. Personally, however, I no longer maintained that contact.

CHAIRMAN: Did you know that LEVIN was the leader of the Leningrad Center?

GERTIK: I did not know he was the center's leader.

CHAIRMAN: Did you have financial resources? Who paid the expenses?

GERTIK: Personally, I only travelled on official business.

CHAIRMAN: At the expense of Soviet power?

GERTIK: If necessary, I would have travelled at the expense of Soviet power.

CHAIRMAN: Did you combine your official trips with the affairs of your organization?

GERTIK: I will not say I did not travel, but I received no specific directives about Leningrad from ZINOVIEV, because I considered them unnecessary. The general line was clear to me: maintain contact with the cadres and pass on information.

Comrade MATULEVICH: Did you regard it as your duty to obtain information in Leningrad and bring it to ZINOVIEV's attention?

GERTIK: Yes, I brought it to his attention once in January, and later I met EVDOKIMOV in August and informed him. Thus all the information reached ZINOVIEV.

CHAIRMAN: Were you a member of the Moscow Center until the very end?

GERTIK: Yes, I believe I was. Evidently I was. I wanted to say a few words about our relations within the center...

CHAIRMAN: Please do.

GERTIK: A whole series of comrades have spoken here today. Whether or not I was a member of the center is unimportant. Of course I was\! In any event, I was a very active worker. But I must say that I first learned a number of facts only during the preliminary investigation or here today.

I fully endorse the statement made by EVDOKIMOV and consider it entirely correct. But there is one point I heard for the first time today, confirmed by both SAFAROV and ZINOVIEV himself: that the program of the Trotskyist bloc remained our foundation. Unfortunately, neither I nor the other comrades were ever told this. On the contrary, we regarded that program as a compromise to which we had agreed out of necessity. We believed that its time had passed; BRAVO and others can confirm this. From 1930 onward the program had outlived itself. There was nothing more to say about it. We had no program of our own and no particular objections to the program the party was carrying out, but we disagreed with the methods by which it was carried out. “If we were carrying out collectivization, if we were carrying out industrialization (and so forth), it would be another matter\!”

I state with full responsibility that had we been told this frankly at the time, most of the comrades would have left (I shall not speak of myself).

Second, there is the matter of talk about blocs with the right, the left, the Trotskyists, and so forth. I learned about that only here. I do not say this reduces my guilt, but I want to speak about the methods employed. It was not merely double-dealing but double double-dealing: they deceived not only the party but also their own comrades in the opposition.

CHAIRMAN: In other words, some things were concealed?

GERTIK: Not merely some things, but the essentials. To tell the truth, that did not surprise me greatly, and here is why: I myself was doing active work at the time. Many comrades worked just as actively as I did, and so forth. But do you know how the bloc with the Trotskyists was arranged? Without us, at meetings of the center\! And we were merely informed\!

We were forever crying out that the Central Committee acted wrongly, failed to discuss questions with the party, and that there was no democracy. But what about ourselves? Exactly the same thing.

ZINOVIEV laments that he was not arrested on the seventh, while I curse myself for something else: I opposed the bloc at the time, I persuaded a number of good, steadfast comrades that we would not enter such a bloc, and I curse myself for not carrying the matter through to the end. I compromised with ZINOVIEV. It must be said that the forces were unequal: ZINOVIEV presented fac\<ts\>, evidence and so forth; in short, he convinced me that the objective was much higher... That is why I curse myself.

It is clear from the witness's testimony and that of the defendants that I played an active role. I have nothing to add. Everyone considers me a member of the center. I also regarded myself as active, but I did not think we had a center. As for negotiations, I knew nothing except the famous talks between BUKHARIN and KAMENEV in 1928. They told us only about that recorded conversation between KAMENEV and BUKHARIN, nothing more. Everything was concealed from us. The negotiations were concealed from us, above all from us.

RUTKOVSKY \[55\] also told me that talks had been held with UGLANOV. Perhaps I was in Tashkent at the time. But SHAROV took a very active part, I took a very active part, as did others, yet neither I, nor SHAROV, nor GORSHENIN heard a single word. Not one word was said to us.

I can say for myself that I would not have entered an organization that negotiated to the right, to the left, and so forth. After all, we had a definite aim, admittedly a wrong one, admittedly a narrow one, but that such dirty, unprincipled work was being done... No\! I will not speak for myself, but I state firmly that people like BRAVO and GESSEN would certainly have left and not remained if they had known. I think that was the sole reason everything was hidden from us. There was no other.

Such were our methods of work and our relations. I say this in no way reduces my guilt. I do not relate it to claim that I am less guilty: I am guilty enough and fully acknowledge my guilt. I admit that this tragic murder by vile people was undoubtedly the outcome of our work. But I say all this so that the whole truth may be disclosed to the court.

It is all true, everything that took place among us. Allow me one more thing\!

CHAIRMAN: Have you finished? Do you wish to say anything else?

GERTIK: I want to clarify one more question. How can it be explained that we remained counterrevolutionaries at a time of such great construction, when the party was straining every effort, building industrial giants, and the whole world acknowledged its successes?

What was the reason? Many people went underground. There really was a struggle for the party, a struggle for Soviet power.

I have thought about this a great deal, and of course there is no single answer. The principal thing is undoubtedly what STALIN said about us: from the first day of our existence we served as a mouthpiece. Second was our blind attachment to ZINOVIEV's ideas. We knew the old ZINOVIEV, and to the very end he remained the old ZINOVIEV. A third reason, perhaps the main one, was this: we invented an entire theory of our own according to which we were doing nothing wrong, harming the party in no way, were not double-dealers and were not deceiving the party. We spoke among ourselves in confidence. We never took anything beyond our circle. In our economic and other work we performed our duties fully and fairly well; we were shock workers and decorated employees, and there was much more of the same. That self-induced slumber became ingrained in us and lasted for years, for eight years. Year after year, month after month, we did this work for eight years and lulled ourselves to sleep for eight years. When I was arrested for the first time, it was not enough. It was our own Soviet power that arrested me. I told all the facts: whom I had met and when I had been in Leningrad. I recounted the rumors, but concealed the rest. Yet I did not conceal it deliberately.

I concealed only one fact concerning BAKAEV; otherwise I hid nothing. I told everyone in the greatest detail exactly what I had been accused of during interrogation, and I told every person I met that it was correct. EVDOKIMOV is sitting here and will not deny it. There were no other conversations. I told everyone that all this was correct. Only when that tragic act occurred, when it struck us on the head like a heavy hammer, did we begin to understand — and not even immediately — how hopelessly we had crawled into the swamp of Menshevik counterrevolution and what a terrible crime we had committed against the party. And for the first time in eight or nine years I say before this court: “There is not one fact, not one word from those nine years that I could not tell today to the court, to the whole party, and to the entire working class. I may forget something, but I do not wish to conceal anything or diminish my guilt; I acknowledge it in full. There were such feelings, such a sick psychology.

INTERROGATION

## OF IVAN IVANOVICH TARASOV

CHAIRMAN: Tell us briefly about all your crimes against the party\*?\* \{in general terms?\}

TARASOV: I have heard a number of new and unexpected things here. I have learned that in every struggle and undertaking all sorts of vacillation and doubt are understandable. I want to give myself a brief answer to one question.

What matters to the party and the working class in this case is not the court. Was it accidental or not that NIKOLAEV fired at KIROV, that the murder was directly organized by KOTOLYNOV, RUMIANTSEV, TOLMAZOV, KHANIK and ot\<hers\> — that is, by that section of the youth who in 1925 departed from the Bolshevik path and followed those who proved to be political adventurers and traitors — KAMENEV, ZINOVIEV, TROTSKY and their closest associates?

In this connection I want to speak of my own part.

Only now, during the preliminary investigation, have I thought this through. I have answered myself with complete certainty that it was no accident that KIROV was murdered by those whom ZINOVIEV succeeded in drawing, in 1925, onto the path of anti-party struggle, which subsequently developed into counterrevolution.

Why is that so? We have heard here about various ideas, forms, programs and foundations of principle. All this unfolded over the ten years in which I followed ZINOVIEV's path. It concerns a change in the party leadership, setting against the Leninist-Stalinist leadership of the Central Committee another group that was to replace it, headed by ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV and the others who led that grouping.

I shall now show concretely, with an example, how this is exactly what happened.

The first period of struggle was between the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Congresses. I attended the Fourteenth Party Congress and remember that KAMENEV spoke after ZINOVIEV's political counter-report. He declared that we considered STALIN incapable at that time of uniting Lenin's headquarters around himself. This openly signalled the unfurling of the banner of struggle against STALIN and the whole Central Committee leadership. Over the next two years that signal was put into practice in ways more monstrous and vile against the great leaders than could scarcely be imagined: we invented and spread such stories, even among non-party people.

After the shot at Smolny I recalled two episodes in the history of the anti-party struggle against the Central Committee leadership. I was in Samara after the Seventh Komsomol Congress when, in conversation, one opposition work\{er\}\*er\* — a printing worker named LIBIN \[56\] — declared that we needed to act with weapons in our hands. This was before the Fifteenth Congress. Shortly afterward, in KOTOLYNOV's presence, certain Komsomol members and STREMIAKOV \[57\], a Moscow oppositionist who cannot be regarded as a serious man, did not deny the atmosphere of hatred toward the leadership that we were then cultivating. STREMIAKOV declared: “It is time to use dynamite.” I asked myself a question at the time and want to answer it now. I considered that this was not an idea that had simply cropped up by chance, but already a fascist idea.

Then came the return to the party. Under what pretext and slogan was this undertaken? It seems to me that everything was handled in a very strained manner. The Zinovievites wanted to shift blame from themselves onto the “leaderless” people: see, they broke away from me and did worse than they would have done had they remained close to me. That is not the issue. We, the “leaderless” people, among whom I counted myself, had only one directive: to return, because ZINOVIEV said that if anything could be done it could be done only within the party — understand that. And we understood. That is how I understood it.

When I was drafting the statement with KOTOLYNOV and RUMIANTSEV, I went to Emelian YAROSLAVSKY and to KAMENEV for advice on how to write it so as to concede as little as possible and get as much as possible through. Concede as little as possible, in the sense of whether it was even possible to rejoin the party without withdrawing our signatures from the platform. We spoke frankly with Em. YAROSLAVSKY. We went to KAMENEV at RUMIANTSEV's invitation to edit the text so that its readers would understand they had not, after all, surrendered. Then for two or three years we vigorously propagated the idea that, look, STALIN and the Central Committee are carrying out our platform. What was behind that, behind our platform? To put tho\<se\> who wrote it in the leadership. That was how it was understood. I shared this position from the Fourteenth to the Fifteenth Congress and approximately until 1932, in this policy of waiting for an opportunity to exploit particular difficulties, and so forth.

The year 1932 is the most characteristic in terms of exploiting difficulties in the struggle against the party. I recall a series of conversations. In 1932, returning from industrial practical training in Dnepropetrovsk, I passed through Leningrad and visited KOTOLYNOV, with whom I had close ties, as we had been friends since 1923. They called one of us Ivan Ivanovich “international,” and me Ivan Ivanovich “all-Union,” who worked within the borders of the Soviet Union. I recall several conversations with RUMIANTSEV. At that time the country was experiencing difficulties, and I must say frankly that I belonged to those who regarded these difficulties as the result of excesses by the Central Committee in industrialization. We thought that enormous resources had been committed to capital construction while workers had nothing to eat. When we discussed the difficulties, RUMIANTSEV expressed approximately this idea: he associated them with STALIN's handling of the middle peasant in ways that the middle peasant had not been accustomed to under LENIN. He said this explained the difficulties, that the peasants were resisting, that there were peasant uprisings, that collectivization policy was inconsistent and no Leninist policy existed. He concluded more or less as follows: “You know, fellows, I think the country's difficulties are so great that STALIN, to avoid perishing from internal troubles, is leading the country into war with the international bourgeoisie. Then it will be easy for him to explain to the working class and the international workers' movement why he perished: he will have died a glorious death fighting world intervention.” If one analyzes what that means, it means that STALIN and all the party leaders are leading the proletarian revolution to ruin. If that is so, the proletarian revolution must be saved from STALIN and the Central Committee. “Save Russia from the Bolsheviks.”

Is that a new slogan? From what arsenal does it come? Although on that occasion I was speaking not with RUMIANTSEV but with TSEITLIN, who repeated precisely the same idea, all this means that we were bringing into our arsenal of struggle against the party leadership what international reaction had put out in White Guard leaflets such as “Vozrozhdenie.”

But I took part in those conversations and, despite knowing these things, did not go to the party. It took such a tragic event for the party to learn of them. I failed to do so then, and therein lies my great guilt, an extremely grave guilt.

If one considers this single fact — that the young people following ZINOVIEV were active young people — the conclusion suggests itself: such leaders must be removed. That is to say, even in 1932 the central idea, the guiding axis, remained the same: to change the leadership, replacing it with one headed by ZINOVIEV. This became an axiom, something taken for granted and beyond dispute; only the timing, forms, possibilities, occasion, and so forth remained in question.

Was it accidental that this very idea — that the leadership was taking the country to war in order to die gloriously and give a fine account of itself to the working class — was voiced by RUMIANTSEV and then repeated by TSEITLIN? Was that a coincidence? The question is that someone must have inspired it. RUMIANTSEV cannot have invented it. I do not know who invented that slander or put it about at the most tense moment in the country, but it unquestionably existed. It could have come from the source BAKAEV indicated: ZINOVIEV's apartment and his associates.

In general, we had the following arrangement. Because I had close ties with KOTOLYNOV, and because KOTOLYNOV, RUMIANTSEV and other young people were returning to the party, although I lived in Moscow I always received the latest news from Leningrad.

So in 1932 KOTOLYNOV took me to NATANSON's place. There the question arose that the Leningrad worker was even worse off than the Moscow worker, because there was absolutely nothing to eat. NATANSON then said: “The devil knows what our leaders are doing: they are building a monument to themselves in their own lifetime — they have invented VIEM (the All-Union Institute of Experimental Medicine) \[58\].” This fact also shows one of the vile ways of exploiting difficulties. Yet when the triumphant advance of the First Five-Year Plan, when life itself in a matter of days dispelled all those platforms, all that remained was counterrevolutionary slander against the party, now exposed. This took precisely the form of double-dealing. And that same double-dealing found expression in cliquish circles, which made it possible to combine party membership and continued presence in the party with the vilest methods of slander, monstrous fabrications and lies against the party and its leadership.

If I now answer the question I posed at the beginning, I must say that the rise of the young fascist thugs who murdered KIROV was no accident. There is one unbroken chain from the unfurling of the banner of struggle against the Leninist-Stalinist leadership proclaimed by the Leningrad opposition at the Fourteenth Congress; it passes through two years of struggle until the Fifteenth Congress, continues with the duplicitous return to the party, with the use of party membership, and extends until 1934 through cliquish circles.

NIKOLAEV's shot was no accident, for NIKOLAEV, like the whole terrorist group, emerged from Zinovievite cliquish circles. That is the main conclusion.

For ten years ZINOVIEV and his associates educated the young people in implacable struggle against the leadership, the necessity of changing that leadership and opposing another to it. As ZINOVIEV himself admitted today, he could not imagine himself as anything other than a leader.

The question is how to understand this. Those who murdered KIROV correctly understood ZINOVIEV and all those who engaged in dirty work. They correctly understood that the leaders had to be changed and decided to do it in their own way, because in those circumstances nothing else could be devised. How else could one replace the leaders? There was no ideological basis on which to approach the party and the working class. There was no hope of a “palace coup”\! And the most active young people, who “prefer deeds to words,” performed the deed. It is perfectly clear that in shooting KIROV they expected to see their group's leadership embodied in ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV and their other supporters. ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV became banners and inspirations for the terrorists. That is a heavy and terrible truth, but one cannot escape it. For decades I followed ZINOVIEV. As the indictment says, ZINOVIEV “acknowledged his errors with bowed head.” But one must raise one's head, look that truth squarely in the face, and speak of the Jesuitical \[59\] struggle against the party and its leadership right up to the last days. I answered that question firmly and without hesitation. I too was stunned when I was arrested. For almost a year I had met nobody. In 1933 I also had meetings, again in connection with KOTOLYNOV's business trip to Moscow. We gathered at FAIVILOVICH's apartment, and despite the fact that harsh voices were already being raised against the so-called leaders, we discussed placing ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV in the place of the man they wanted to remove from the Politburo. I heard it said several times that ZINOVIEV would never summon up the courage, that he would soil his trousers ten times before undertaking something like the liquidation of the kulaks as a class. Even at the end of 1933 we continued to hold conversations against the party, always revolving around the same central issue: leadership. When the conversation began, FAIVILOVICH's wife, TEREMIAKINA, described how KAGANOVICH worked in every area, solving both major and minor problems. Yet we still discussed whether, although KAGANOVICH worked well, he was fit to replace STALIN. I cannot remember who raised the question \[60\]. Stalin was alive and well and led the party, yet people discussed who could replace him. Was this accidental? No, it was not. It was the same thread. I also remember FAIVILOVICH saying plainly: “No, you know, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV are men who ought to have been brought into the leadership”; that the right conditions simply were not made for them, that STALIN would not let them come near. Although people abused their own leaders, they said: they may be bad, but you will find none better. First the platform and program, then under the pretext that STALIN rejected our platform, this fundamental idea of the former counterrevolutionary organization remained until the very last days. I then asked myself what my own part was in all this. After ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV fraternized with Ryutin, I came to believe that they could not lead the party and that the party would be no better off for it. I continued to meet people by chance and heard abuse directed at STALIN, but that does not change matters. Who prevented me from going to the party and telling them all this? Had I done so, perhaps the shot at Smolny would never have occurred.

In 1933 I already had some flashes of understanding after unpleasant conversations with RUMIANTSEV. I decided those talks would lead nowhere, and together with KOTOLYNOV we resolved that there was no need to go anywhere to talk. I failed to do for the party what it was entitled to demand of me. In recent years I considered myself to be standing on party positions. I did not rise above cliquishness and, therefore, though it seemed to me in the last period of my life that I had withdrawn, I had not truly withdrawn. I cannot absolve myself of responsibility for what occurred in Leningrad on the first of the month, and must bear severe punishment for it.

CHAIRMAN: Have you finished?

TARASOV: I have remembered another circumstance that was not recorded in the interrogation protocol.

I visited SAFAROV from time to time, all the more since he was the leader of the “leaderless” people. I happened to visit him when KOTOLYNOV came to town, because it was very difficult to talk with KOTOLYNOV alone. When asked whether he was a member of the Russian delegation to the Comintern, SAFAROV answered that he was not and then characterized matters by saying that, in fact, there was no Russian delegation in the Comintern: it was an empty place and no one seriously directed the Comintern. He maliciously ridiculed PIATNITSKY, a member of the Russian delegation. I also met and spoke with a number of other people, but these were the most important meetings, fully characterizing my guilt in this case and my participation in Zinoviev's underground counterrevolutionary organization.

If I have omitted anything, please put questions to me.

CHAIRMAN: I declare a recess until 10 o'cl\<ock\> in the morning of January 16 (1:30 a.m., January 16).
