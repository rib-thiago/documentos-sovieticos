INTERROGATION RECORD

Of SAFAROV, Georgy Ivanovich,

Dated December 23, 1934.

In response to the investigation's questions about my participation in the anti-Soviet work of the Moscow center of the Zinovievist organization, I testify:

It took me several days to “overcome the doubt”: could I, while accepting all political responsibility for the monstrous crime of the counterrevolutionary conspirators, at the same time hold responsible people less politically developed than myself? This doubt is the fruit of what was nurtured by the criminal struggle of the counterrevolutionary opposition against the Party, a fruit and remnant of that vile mutual protection which proved to be the soil that nourished the murderers. I returned to the Party after submitting a statement to the Central Control Commission, sincerely as I thought. In reality counterrevolutionary leprosy had left its traces on me. In 1928–1929 I worked in Saratov in the Territorial Land Administration and agricultural cooperatives, and had not the slightest thought of returning to the old dead end. My first years working in the Comintern passed in intensive work in the CI, and I think 1932 should be considered the year when I was sucked into the counterrevolutionary anti-Party mire. Precisely at the turning point of transition from the first to the second Five-Year Plan the old leprosy came to the surface with its sores. It began with conversations with MAGYAR, who was closest to me through work and past connections with my anti-Party activities. In conversations between the two of us the Party line was characterized as a line that had lost its equilibrium, as a swaying that threatened the bond between the proletariat and the peasantry. Had the Trotskyists, it was said in these conversations, carried out their line on the peasant question, the consequences probably would not have been worse. The Rightists, I said, could not have gone further in bureaucratic disregard for the workers' everyday interests. “The Comintern's line was defined as a complete absence of a line; the Central Committee takes no interest in this second-rate institution (our expression), the parties are declining, no leadership, lack of ideas.” Once MAGYAR reported that apparently, judging from a conversation with LOMINADZE, whom he often met, the Leftists were writing a platform. VARDIN, who had similar sentiments, learned of this from a conversation with me and ran to LOMINADZE. As later became clear, LOMINADZE became very alarmed and began questioning where this came from. Since MAGYAR had asked me not to tell him that it originated with MAGYAR, when LOMINADZE came to me I answered evasively, while at the same time a conversation about affairs began: I expressed to him my considerations about the situation in the spirit already described. First he asked whether I had written with conviction about “Lominadzeism” in my book “Problems of the National-Colonial Revolution,” published in 1931. I replied that I had thought so then, but would now write differently. After this LOMINADZE expressed general agreement with my assessment of the situation but suggested greater caution, particularly saying nothing to anyone about some platform being prepared: in reality none existed.

As far as I recall, my meetings and conversations with Sergei MRACHKOVSKY and I. N. SMIRNOV date to this time. MRACHKOVSKY came to Moscow on his railway's business. He met me; at first I (this must have been the pangs of the miserable remnants of Party conscience) told him that TROTSKY and his associates abroad had turned completely into agents of VANDERVELDE [1]; that on grounds of principle it must be recognized that on the question of building socialism in one country STALIN had proved entirely correct; that the forecast of Thermidor had proved worthless. But, I continued, the absence of internal Party democracy and bureaucratic separation from the masses weakened the Party and turned its line into a spontaneously emerging resultant. My conclusion was: we must have courage; let two dozen old Bolsheviks openly address the Central Committee with collective signatures setting out their view of things. This “truth” was worth suffering for. MRACHKOVSKY reluctantly assented (evidently to avoid breaking off the conversation) to the part where I spoke of the bankruptcy of Trotskyism, agreed with the assessment of the situation, adding that repression had gone as far as it could. As for the conclusions, he said they needed thought. Did I see ZINOVIEV and EVDOKIMOV, he asked. I said that so far I did not. He regretted this. Afterward there was a meeting with I. N. SMIRNOV, with whom the conversation was a repetition. Around this time MAGYAR reported that ZINOVIEV wanted to see me and appointed the time and place: Ilyinskoye, at ZINOVIEV's dacha. MAGYAR maintained fairly frequent relations with ZINOVIEV, but said little specifically about his meetings, mostly passing on gossip he had heard about difficulties. At the dacha at Ilyinskoye I met ZINOVIEV and we went into the garden. The conversation began with the state of affairs in the CI. I expressed the view that the parties had lost their feel for reality and merely repeated: “Social fascism is the main danger,” until matters reached breaking point, when they would begin inventing something like the Anglo-Russian Committee, unable to apply the Leninist united-front tactic.

ZINOVIEV: Yes, MAGYAR told me. “I think the same. How could MANUILSKY and PYATNITSKY lead the CI?”

Then the conversation turned to the situation in the country. I spoke about the threat of a rupture of the bond, about the Party being deprived of the opportunity to check its course. That local secretaries each acted in their own way. I said that the former Trotskyists too had “learned a great deal” (MRACHKOVSKY, I. SMIRNOV). I conveyed their assent about the opposition's “mistake” on socialism in one country. ZINOVIEV interrupted: “Yes, yes! Is that the issue now? Now everyone can find a common language.” My conclusion that we must address the Central Committee with a statement assessing the situation was met by ZINOVIEV as follows: “What will the Central Committee, the ZHDANOVS, decide? I recently saw TOMSKY about a literary matter—he agrees with us completely. He says: a pitiful spectacle. He has to go there. And then: you know that Asiatic manner—he can pour poison into your ear and you will not notice.” This referred to I. V. STALIN. He had uttered this phrase more than once in 1925–1927. “He will concoct an amalgam.” The baseness of my fall was reflected in the fact that not only did I listen without protesting to this vile phrase, but asked: “How then do you, your Zinovievist colony, live so openly that from one end of Moscow to the other one hears who is saying what? Better to submit a statement from a group of old Party members to the Central Committee than to whisper like this.” ZINOVIEV said: “You are always in a hurry. Lev Borisovich (KAMENEV) has a draft by STEN. Meet KAMENEV and acquaint yourself with it. Incidentally, STEN is a very clever fellow. I have got to know him better. Lominadze's young people are STALIN's best activists, and they too are dissatisfied with the situation. We must meet more often; arrangements can be made through MAGYAR. Meet EVDOKIMOV.” The meeting ended there. Afterward I visited EVDOKIMOV, but for the conversation he arranged a meeting at my home. My wife, from whom I concealed all this counterrevolutionary conspiratorial activity, after seeing EVDOKIMOV leave my room, asked: “What, back to your old ways again?” I began trying to convince her otherwise. The conversation with EVDOKIMOV concerned the general assessment and added nothing new. EVDOKIMOV's summary: “It is good that we are talking to each other again, not like dogs.” Incidentally, ZINOVIEV too, at the meeting at his dacha, objecting to a “hasty decision” to appeal to the Central Committee, said: “Look how much time it took—years!—for you and me to meet on this felled stump” (on which we were sitting). This was of course to be understood as “time is working for us.” I did not meet KAMENEV and did not see Sten's platform. About its contents—“only by hearsay”—either MRACHKOVSKY or SMIRNOV told me: its core was supposedly the demand for internal Party democracy and a reduction in the pace of industrialization, but “something is wrong about the collective farms; they propose dissolving the state farms and collective farms.”

Around the same time I visited the GERTSBERGS in Dangauerovka (him and M. POZDEEVA) and expounded my anti-Party views.

At the second meeting, approximately in mid-1932 if I am not mistaken, at SMIRNOV's apartment, MRACHKOVSKY asked: “Have you not visited Enichka (Evg. A. PREOBRAZHENSKY)?” Now, naturally, it appears that I went to PREOBRAZHENSKY on MRACHKOVSKY's referral, but then I went as though to check myself, since at moments when I drew back from my anti-Party frenzy I acutely felt that I was plunging into some abyss from which I had only recently climbed with great effort. PREOBRAZHENSKY, whom I visited approximately in the autumn of 1932, replied to my thesis about the threat of a rupture of the bond that nobody could propose anything different from what the Party was doing, and the socialist transformation of the peasantry could not be achieved without a series of difficulties and tensions. On internal Party democracy too he rejected my arguments, saying that in the given class situation the Party was doing everything necessary.

When MRACHKOVSKY left Moscow, I think in the middle or autumn of 1932, those who came to his carriage to see him off were myself, SMIRNOV, EVDOKIMOV and also Pyotr PEREVERZEV. VAGANYAN [2] was also there, and MRACHKOVSKY “reconciled” me with him on the spot (we had not been greeting one another because of old disputes). Arriving at the carriage later than the others, I found everyone listed there. I did not know that EVDOKIMOV had become close to MRACHKOVSKY and I. N. SMIRNOV, since in conversations with me he on the one hand and MRACHKOVSKY on the other tried in every way to give no sign of a direct connection between them. This was so unexpected that even MRACHKOVSKY, noticing my surprise, said: “Do not be embarrassed, everyone is one of us.” Then there was whispering in different corners, with the expression “our collective farm” repeated in various forms. When we began saying goodbye, MRACHKOVSKY said: “If you want to write, send it with someone traveling that way. Part of our collective farm remains here. Find I. N. SMIRNOV or call this woman” (some woman was there, called by a diminutive I have forgotten; I was given her telephone number, which I lost—it was written on a scrap of paper).

MRACHKOVSKY arranged one of the meetings with me at UFIMTSEV's apartment, but MRACHKOVSKY did not come.

I cannot establish the date exactly, but I think in the autumn of 1932 Muska NATANSON met me outside the CI building and began asking about my views: I expounded to her the same counterrevolutionary views I have already written about.

My conversation with Andrei TOLMAZOV and SEREDOKHIN, who came to see me at the CI, proceeded on approximately the same plane: “The Comintern has neither rudder nor sails, a rupture of the bond threatens, there are no means of helping correct the course.” I have difficulty establishing the date of this conversation, but remember that TOLMAZOV and especially SEREDOKHIN began with a greeting to the effect that “it seems you have an understanding with ZINOVIEV.”

In 1930 I had sharp clashes at my apartment with RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV and NAUMOV, who condemned the Central Committee's newly adopted decision to raise livestock-product prices as a concession to the prosperous upper strata of the countryside at the worker's expense.

After my sharp clashes with him in 1929–1930, RUMYANTSEV met me again at the CI only in 1931, when he came to invite MAGYAR to give a report at the Vyborg House of Culture. He proposed that I too go to give a report. This meeting was in passing, and I do not remember any political conversation.

I saw KOTOLYNOV once or twice in 1931–1932 in Leningrad, when I called at his apartment during visits to the Leningrad Oriental Institute. I cannot recall exactly, but probably described to him the situation in the Comintern in an anti-Party spirit. Probably he and TARASOV visited me in Moscow a couple of times in 1932–1933. There were no detailed political conversations with them, but the general tone and content of individual remarks and statements on my part were anti-Party.

One way or another, RUMYANTSEV, KOTOLYNOV and others could obtain the most detailed information about my anti-Party directives and views from the regular reports of the Zinovievist leaders, from M. NATANSON's information, and from TOLMAZOV's and SEREDOKHIN's accounts.

In 1932 and possibly early 1933 I visited I. SMILGA twice. Once was when he still worked at the USSR Gosplan. I came to SMILGA to set out my counterrevolutionary views. He supported the thesis of a rupture of the bond, said that at Gosplan he advocated a “collective-farm NEP,” that grain prices must be raised and collective farms given completely free access to the market. When I expressed doubt whether this would not be capitulation to the kulak, he quickly turned to “palace affairs,” as he put it, saying that there was no need for haste, that one must wait “until the hostile groups in the Central Committee clash.” He indicated that he “admired KAMENEV's rare economist's mind,” with whom he was publishing memoirs and other Academia editions.

The second time I visited SMILGA was before his assignment to Central Asia. He was very frightened and kept repeating: “Wait, Georges, until they fight among themselves without us.” This was the song all the Zinovievists sang, constantly sharpening the question of replacing the leadership, the inevitability of their being brought into the leadership, and so forth.

I must add that after the defeat of the Sten-Ryutin counterrevolutionary organization I came to SMIRNOV with the question: “What should we do if the Party catches us red-handed? Go to prison for Ryutin's kulak views and evade things as unworthily as ZINOVIEV before the Central Control Commission? Would it not be better immediately to address the Central Committee with our political statement, distinct from Ryutin's position?” (In essence, of course, this was one common counterrevolutionary position of all the former oppositionists who raised the banner of struggle against the Party.) SMIRNOV replied that each “household” should decide for itself. He would say under interrogation that the Ryutinites were victims of the Stalinist regime. It was apparent that he and his company had decided to fold up and not disclose their secrets.

At approximately the same time I expressed to MAGYAR my indignation at ZINOVIEV's lack of character and SMIRNOV's diplomacy. I also said this to EVDOKIMOV, whom I met.

During this period MAGYAR met LOMINADZE, who was depressed by the “incident” with STEN, which he explained by “GPU methods.” GORSHENIN constantly informed MAGYAR about Zinovievist affairs and ZINOVIEV's needs in exile; through him MAGYAR passed his information to ZINOVIEV and company, evidently including information about my sentiments.

I sank to the deepest bottom of that internal emigration concealed behind a Party card, which created among a narrow handful of degenerates an atmosphere of ineradicable and inextinguishable hostility toward the Leninist-Stalinist Party leadership. Year after year, month after month, from one conversation to another, furious hatred toward I. V. STALIN and the Politburo members was cultivated. Constantly, on every occasion and pretext, the question was raised of this leadership's unfitness, of the first serious trial inevitably causing its disintegration and a clash of hostile groups over power. People were trained to seize on every piece of gossip, every counterrevolutionary rumor about disagreements and struggle in the Politburo. People were systematically trained to see in the thousand-times-bankrupt counterrevolutionary leaders the entire meaning of the October Revolution, the entire meaning of the struggle for communism, which they prostituted into the most vulgar, the lowest fascist caricature. Abroad, in the press of the vile dogs of the bourgeoisie and murderers of workers, TROTSKY portrayed the Soviet Union as a degenerated bourgeois-bureaucratic state. And the ZINOVIEVS and their ilk repeated the same in their own fashion, passing all this vileness along the chain of their counterrevolutionary organization. With little reservations about the Central Committee's correctness on the question of the victory of socialism in one country, I entered a political conspiracy with this scum, becoming one of its transmission belts. Ever since the anti-Party struggle of 1925–1928, ZINOVIEV liked to repeat: “We can always form a Central Committee; STALIN has no such people.” And the threads leading to the bullet that struck the great Bolshevik KIROV run from Kamenev's New Year toast in 1926 during the Fourteenth Congress, from the cries about “two thousand opposition proletarians thrown into the street,” from the struggle against the Party under TROTSKY's leadership in 1926–1927, from November 7, from underground tea parties, “information,” gossip and “meetings,” from the broad expansion of counterrevolutionary activities, especially in 1932–1933, from visits by “informants” to Leningrad, and so on and so forth. LENIN taught that a political leader answers not only for himself but also for his supporters. Counterrevolutionary duplicity, which became a system of underground influence on Party life, inevitably had to grow, and did grow, into counterrevolutionary banditry.

I bear full responsibility for RUMYANTSEV and KOTOLYNOV, since they were educated under my influence from 1924, when I was the Party leader of the Provincial Youth Committee. Under my leadership in 1925 they organized an anti-Party reception for the representative of the Party Central Committee at the Leningrad Party Conference. Under my leadership they passed through the entire school of Trotskyist struggle against the Party. From exile in Achinsk I fed them counterrevolutionary ideas through that same nimble MAGYAR. My later return to the Zinovievist ranks, with the Zinovievists' announcement “Even the furious one has come to us,” must have inflamed their counterrevolutionary activity.

I think that from the middle of 1933 I felt how far I had gone into the counterrevolutionary quagmire. I became convinced that only the Leninist-Stalinist Central Committee headed by STALIN knew how to lead the country to socialism through obstacles and barriers. What we counterrevolutionary bankrupts called swaying was in reality steely Bolshevik firmness. Having lost the ability to distinguish revolution from counterrevolution, we not only lost all elementary stability but degenerated so far that we lost the ability to distinguish a line from swaying. I tried to bury myself more deeply, headlong, in work, but of course lacked the courage to come before the Party as a confessed criminal. I asked the Central Committee to transfer me to other work, feeling that I had departed from the great life of the socialist motherland, from the creativity of millions, from their genuine needs and interests. Fool that I was, I thought I could make work on the problems of the national-colonial revolution into a shelter, a refuge from the pangs of the remnants of Party conscience.

I write about this not to find even the slightest justification for myself. It is only an explanation that highlights and increases my responsibility. Had the vile nest been destroyed earlier, the murder that surpassed in its abomination the murders of Karl LIEBKNECHT and Rosa LUXEMBURG would not have occurred. From the Party and Soviet power, in the name of my struggle in its ranks until 1924, I ask as a mercy the harshest punishment.

Written in my own hand: G. SAFAROV.

INTERROGATED BY: HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD, PETROVSKY
