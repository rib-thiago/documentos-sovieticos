INTERROGATION RECORD

of ELKOVICH, Yakov Rafailovich —

January 17, 1935.

QUESTION: Tell us in greater detail about the structure, organizational ties, and directives of the Zinovievist organization and your practical work within the organization.

ANSWER: In addition to my previous testimony, I can state the following: both the Moscow and Leningrad centers basically took shape immediately after the Fifteenth Congress from the Zinovievist part of the former Trotskyist-Zinovievist centers. The Moscow center included ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, KUKLIN, KHARITONOV (later, beginning in 1930, his organizational ties with the center, particularly with ZINOVIEV, were somewhat weakened), SHAROV, GERTIK, and, beginning in 1931, GORSHENIN as well. From its very first steps this center's activity was directed toward preserving the Zinovievist cadres at all costs, persuading all Zinovievists to join the statement of the “23,” and on this basis returning

them to the party. Moreover, since agitation for joining the statement of the “23” was not accompanied by an exposure of the treacherous, bourgeois-restorationist, capitulationist, defeatist platform of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc, the entire meaning of this fundamental double-dealing maneuver, which conditioned the further counterrevolutionary degeneration of the Zinovievist organization, amounted to securing the return to the party of all expelled Zinovievists so that they could “sit it out” within the party. Particular attention in this respect is devoted to Leningrad. Center members EVDOKIMOV and KUKLIN and GESSEN, who was close to the Moscow center, go to Leningrad for a lengthy period. Members of the Leningrad center LEVIN, SHURYGIN, and Timofei DMITRIEV frequently come from Leningrad for directives from ZINOVIEV and other members of the Moscow center. While ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were in Kaluga, the work of the Moscow center and its connections with Leningrad and the provinces were not weakened in the slightest.

Visits to ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV in Kaluga were regular and systematic. Not only members of the Moscow center but also members of the organization from the provinces visited him. Personally, on one of my trips from Smolensk (I was then working in Smolensk) to Moscow, I went to Kaluga with organization members GORSHENIN and KAREV. Just before our arrival, ZALUTSKY, who had been “staying” there for a considerable time, had left Kaluga. As far as I now recall, the main meaning of ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's remarks in Kaluga was as follows: preserve the Zinovievist cadres at all costs (this directive runs like a thread through all the subsequent counterrevolutionary activity of the Zinovievist organization), secure the return of all Zinovievists to the party, because “time is working for us, for the Zinovievists.” ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV explained this thesis approximately as follows: although the party leadership had smashed us Zinovievists, it had at the same time basically adopted our line (this provocative slander that the party leadership had borrowed a number of propositions from the opposition was widely circulated in the Trotskyist-Zinovievist camp in 1928–1929); precedents of this kind had occurred before in party history; LENIN, in his time, having defeated SHLYAPNIKOV, took the idea of the purge from him; and if that was so, an increased role for the Zinovievists in the party, and even a return to the leadership, was inevitable. I too put this directive of the Zinovievist center for a double-dealing return to the party into practice in Smolensk, agitating for adherence to the statement of the “23” among the participants in the Trotskyist-Zinovievist opposition in Smolensk, which I had headed in 1926–1927.

Together with me, this work in Smolensk was conducted by Alexander KOROLEV, an active participant in the Zinovievist organization. In particular, I passed on this directive to assemble the cadres to V. VINOGRADOV, who was leaving Smolensk for Leningrad.

From 1929, the illusions that the party and its leadership would return the Zinovievists to leading work disappear, and approximately from the end of 1929 the Moscow center headed by ZINOVIEV begins a systematic, stubborn, persistent campaign among members of the Zinovievist organization to inflame hostility and hatred toward the leaders of the party and Soviet power. Discussion of any question of the policies of the party and Soviet power was accompanied by the most vicious attacks and the most poisonous slander against the party leadership. All this, combined with gloating, malicious savoring of the difficulties and their monstrous inflation and exaggeration, conditioned the transformation of Zinovievism into an internal White emigration.

The campaign of slander, hostility, and hatred toward the leadership of the party and country was inspired, headed, and organized by ZINOVIEV, primarily through the poisonous, slanderous accusation that the leadership lacked Marxist-Leninist foresight and that its leadership was therefore supposedly empirical, which “costs the country dearly.” In previous testimony I spoke of my meetings with ZINOVIEV and other members of the center in 1929–1934 and of their directives aimed at inflaming slander and hatred toward the party leadership, as a result of which NIKOLAEV's shot followed.

Turning now to the structure of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization, it must be emphasized that beginning in 1928–1929 it had fully developed forms. Until 1932 the Moscow center maintained its principal ties with members of the organization in the provinces mainly through GERTIK. His home was, so to speak, the main headquarters, where members of the organization arriving from the provinces received their initial information and directives. Moreover, until 1932 gatherings of members of the center and members of the Zinovievist organization living in Moscow took place there.

During my visits from Smolensk in 1928 and from Sverdlovsk in 1929, 1930, and 1931, I met center members EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, GORSHENIN, and KUKLIN there, as well as PERIMOV, BRAVO, BASHKIROV, KOSTINA, KOZHURO, N. GORDON, NAUMOV, ZALUTSKY, and others.

From 1932, following GERTIK's arrest, GORSHENIN's apartment becomes such a headquarters for both provincial members of the organization and Muscovites; by this period (1932), GORSHENIN's role can be defined as that of head of organization for the Moscow center.

Of the provincial connections of the Moscow center of the Zinovievist counterrevolutionary organization, besides myself (SVERDLOVSK), I know of connections with Gorky (FURTICHEV — contact through GORSHENIN), Tagil (OLKHOVSKY — contact through BAKAEV), Novosibirsk (KAPITONOV — contact through GORSHENIN), and Rostov (DMITRIEV, GORDON, ROTSKAN — contact predominantly through KUKLIN); furthermore, until 1931–1932 contact was maintained through KHARITONOV with Voronezh (Olga RAVICH), Leningrad (BASHKIROV), and myself (Sverdlovsk).

The Moscow center's principal connections with Leningrad were concentrated mainly in the hands of EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, and partly SHAROV. EVDOKIMOV also maintained contact with Leningrad through the concealed, I would even say carefully clandestine, Zinovievist BOGRACHEV, who frequently traveled to Moscow in connection with his work as deputy chairman of the Leningrad Soviet. I have already spoken in previous testimony about how EVDOKIMOV instructed BOGRACHEV regarding ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's expulsion from the party (the bloc with Ryutin and Sten). At the same meeting at EVDOKIMOV's, I arranged with BOGRACHEV that, if I traveled to Leningrad, I would approach him for a reservation to obtain a railway ticket for the return journey. When I visited Leningrad at the end of 1932, I obtained the reservation from BOGRACHEV at Smolny.

The two-way connection with Leningrad, as far as I could judge during my visits to Moscow and Leningrad, was particularly well and firmly established. As I have already indicated in previous testimony, my trips were also used to maintain contact with Leningrad and convey certain directives of the Moscow center there.

During my trip to Leningrad in 1929, I met with T. DMITRIEV, V. MATVEYEV, and A. KOROLEV. I met each separately at their apartments. The conversations mainly centered on questions of the purge. I clearly remember the conversation with DMITRIEV: he complained that his purge had been difficult, that the purge commission was no longer satisfied with adherence to the statement of the “23” but had demanded a detailed criticism of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist platform, finding his initial speech inadequate and unsatisfactory; that similar setbacks were occurring for other members of the Zinovievist organization who did not want to “give” more than had been said in the statement of the “23.” As a result of the conversation we concluded that one should not hesitate to make a detailed criticism of the Trotskyist-Zinovievist platform in order to preserve the Zinovievist cadres. This was essentially also the substance of the conversation with MATVEYEV, whom I also met when he visited Sverdlovsk in 1933.

During my trip to Leningrad in 1930 we gathered at GAIDEROVA's. Present were GAIDEROVA, MILOVA, BASHKIROV, Vl. LEVIN, and myself. Whether Timofei DMITRIEV was then at GAIDEROVA's I do not remember, but in any event I saw and spoke to him during that visit.

At the meeting at GAIDEROVA's I conveyed the Zinovievist directive and assessment of the state of collectivization (the slanderous assertion that leadership was proceeding “by groping,” that its empirical character cost the country dearly). At that same meeting it emerged that members of the Zinovievist organization had already received this Zinovievist directive through Leningrad center member V. LEVIN, who had traveled to Moscow.

The conversation also touched on the position of TARTAKOVSKAYA, whom everyone considered “one of our own” — an active Zinovievist — but said they were afraid to deal with her because she was of an adventurist disposition. I remember joining in this assessment, stating that I had always considered TARTAKOVSKAYA an adventuress. From my trips to Leningrad in 1930 and 1932 I gained the impression that GAIDEROVA had the same sort of headquarters in Leningrad as GERTIK in Moscow.

I have already partly spoken of my trip to Leningrad in 1932 in previous testimony. The fact that GORSHENIN in Moscow knew perfectly well NATANSON's attitude in Leningrad toward ZINOVIEV's bloc with RYUTIN–STEN, and in December 1932 gave me a special assignment to speak with NATANSON during my trip to Leningrad, already struck me at the time as emphasizing the excellently established connection between the Moscow center and Leningrad. I recounted the content of my conversation with NATANSON and ANISHEV in previous testimony. Besides them, during this visit I had conversations of the same kind, amounting to a repetition of ZINOVIEV's slanderous “directive” that the party leadership headed by Comrade STALIN wanted, by taking reprisals against him, to “cover up the breaches in agriculture,” with center member SHURYGIN and also with BASHKIROV. In conversation with me, SHURYGIN, describing his contacts with Zinovievists from the Moskovsko-Narvsky district, particularly emphasized his closest connection with Foma NALIVAIKO.

During that same visit in 1932, we arranged by telephone to meet with organization member Moisei YAKOVLEV (he conveyed his wish to see me through SEDYKH, the representative of the “Ural Soviet Encyclopedia” in Leningrad), whom I had met several times during his trips to Sverdlovsk and also while on leave in Essentuki (I shall speak of these meetings in my further testimony); however, the meeting did not take place because I was hurrying to leave.

My sister Tatyana ELKOVICH, who met GORBACHEV in connection with her GIZ work, passed on GORBACHEV's request that I telephone him to arrange a meeting. But I had no time to telephone him.

On this trip to Leningrad I also saw Nikolai MATORIN, who, learning of my departure from YAKOVLEV, came to see me off at the station. However, I had no political conversations with him at the station because, besides MATORIN, Comrade BABAILOVA was seeing me off. My next and last trip to Leningrad took place in November 1933. This time, however, I refrained from meetings and conversations in Leningrad with members of the Zinovievist organization. I had only one very brief meeting with BASHKIROV, which was not accompanied by any conversation on political subjects, since I had learned that after my visit in 1932 the investigative authorities had summoned my sisters. To an even greater extent this caution on my part was explained by NATANSON's arrest, which, as I learned, occurred in Moscow shortly after my December 1932 trip to Leningrad.

I shall speak in my next testimony of other connections known to me of the Zinovievist counterrevolutionary organization in Moscow and Leningrad, and of meetings with Zinovievists in Sverdlovsk.

Testimony written in my own hand.

ELKOVICH.
