INTERROGATION RECORD

Of FEDOROV Grigory Fedorovich

December 30, 1934

(Supplementary)

Question: At your interrogation of December 21, 1934, you testified that ZINOVIEV issued the instruction: “We must enter the Party at any cost; opposition cadres must be preserved within the Party in case of further disagreements with the Party.” Describe this in greater detail.

Answer: The counterrevolutionary essence of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist organization became apparent long before the Fifteenth Congress, when it, the organization, trampled upon the limits set by the Party statutes and resorted to methods of struggle borrowed from the arsenal of the most notorious fascist scum.

The leaders of this counterrevolutionary organization — ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and TROTSKY — not only covered up these methods of struggle with vile cynicism, but themselves helped introduce them into the struggle against the Party.

At the Fifteenth Party Congress, the opposition employed a new poisoned weapon: the method of mass deception, the method of double-dealing. This vile method was also practiced quite extensively throughout all the subsequent years of the organization's struggle against the Party after the Fifteenth Congress. The statement to the congress expressing our submission and requesting our readmission to the Party, which was written by ZINOVIEV, was so double-dealing and so obviously sought to deceive the Party that, among all the oppositionists present at the meeting in ZINOVIEV's apartment, not one could immediately bring himself to sign this double-dealing document, permeated throughout with lies and deceit.

Evidently, at that time a number of oppositionists had not yet entirely lost their Party conscience. We were supposed to tell the Party congress that we agreed with all its

resolutions, whereas in reality it was precisely its resolutions with which we disagreed.

Then, at this same meeting, held on the last day of the Fifteenth Congress, ZINOVIEV declared that “either we sign this statement, and then there is hope that we will remain in the Party, or we do not sign the statement, remain true to the purity of our principles, but then we will find ourselves outside the Party.” To this LASHEVICH declared: “You are the leaders, so you sign it, and if you consider it necessary, then we will sign as well.”

ZINOVIEV was the first to sign this statement. LASHEVICH followed him, and then all the others present. At this meeting were ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, LASHEVICH, EVDOKIMOV, KHARITONOV, BAKAEV, SHAROV, NAUMOV, GESSEN, KUKLIN, ZALUTSKY, GERTIK, and myself. I do not remember who else was there. Thus, this meeting marked the beginning of organized double-dealing and deception of the Party.

When the congress proposed that everyone wishing to return to the Party submit an individual statement, this was already easier to do, since it was already the second deception of the Party. After the Party congress ended, the opposition faced the question of what to do next. Another meeting on this question was convened at ZINOVIEV's apartment, attended mostly by the same people as the first meeting.

The following decisions were adopted at this meeting:

1\) Preserve the entire opposition organization.

2\) Preserve contacts with the localities by establishing “friendly” correspondence. Entrust contact with the localities to GERTIK, NAUMOV, and KHARITONOV;

3\) Do not convene meetings, but confine ourselves to “comradely” encounters;

4\) Try to persuade everyone who had not submitted an application to join the Party to do so;

5\) Explain to all members of the opposition that the task of the day was to remain in the Party by every means.

Then, at this meeting, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV delivered informational reports. The main substance of their reports was that the questions of the country's economic and political life had not been resolved by the congress. The congress had concerned itself more with the opposition than with the questions of the country's economic and political life that ought to have been resolved at that congress. The general conclusion of ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's reports was as follows: economic difficulties were approaching that the present leadership would be unable to handle. Consequently, it was necessary to be ready for new complications within the Party. A united opposition should play the role of a factor correcting Party policy and hindering the Party in its turn to the Right.

We departed for the localities with instructions approximately along these lines. In particular, I was sent to work in Kharkov. My activity in Kharkov is of no substantial interest to the investigation, although I worked there for more than a year and a half. There was not a single oppositionist in Kharkov; consequently, I had no one to whom I could pass on the instructions I had brought.

In Kharkov in 1928, I received two or three letters from ZINOVIEV in which he asked me to send him information about the state of affairs in Ukraine. ZINOVIEV was particularly interested in questions of sowing and the mood of the workers.

At ZINOVIEV's request, I took out a subscription for him to one of the Kharkov newspapers. During my visits to Moscow that same year, 1928, I usually passed the materials I had in the form of clippings and notes to ZINOVIEV's secretary, BOGDAN, who sent them to Kaluga.

On one visit to Moscow in the summer of 1928, at ZINOVIEV's request, I traveled to Kaluga with P. ZALUTSKY. There, besides a general mutual exchange of information, an exchange of opinions on current political questions took place. The principal substantive question discussed was that of disagreements in the Politburo. No conclusions were drawn, since it was decided that not everything was clear yet and that we needed to see how matters would develop. In the evening, news of LASHEVICH's death arrived, which put an end to further conversation; ZINOVIEV went off to write an obituary, while ZALUTSKY and I began preparing to leave for Moscow.

Before leaving, we also exchanged opinions about LASHEVICH's funeral. We decided to request permission to bring LASHEVICH's body to Moscow.

At the same time, at this very meeting, attended by ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, ZALUTSKY, and myself, it was decided that as many oppositionists as possible should be drawn to LASHEVICH's funeral, with whom a series of informational meetings was planned. We were not permitted to bring LASHEVICH's body to Moscow, and nothing came of this plan.

During my official trips to Moscow in 1928–1929, I met almost all the former oppositionists, both those living in Moscow and those who arrived in Moscow at the same time as I did. It happened as follows: one only had to call at NAUMOV's or GERTIK's apartment and sometimes one would see there the entire contingent of oppositionists present in Moscow. These apartments were permanent headquarters in Moscow for both collecting and spreading various rumors. All information from other cities was concentrated there as well, and almost continuous meetings of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist organization took place there. I too went to these apartments, where I received “fresh” information. Outwardly, these were comradely encounters; there was tea and sometimes even wine. But the substance of these encounters was to inform one another and work out a common tactical line on all the main political questions. I do not remember any encounters at which political questions were not discussed and given a corresponding anti-Party assessment. These apartments played an especially important role in maintaining contacts with opposition groups working in other cities. As far as I know, contact was maintained personally and by correspondence. I do not know the procedures for these contacts, since I myself was in the position of a “representative from a locality.”

This was the situation until the chief anti-Party figures, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, arrived from Kaluga. With their arrival, anti-Party activity expanded considerably, since their apartments also became gathering places for the organization.

At the same time, the level of the meetings also rose. Not only rumors and gossip, but fundamental questions of Party policy began to be discussed. ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV raised all the questions to a “principled” level and gave these discussions an openly counterrevolutionary character.

The subjects of the meetings became such questions as industrialization, collectivization, the internal Party regime, questions of the Comintern, and so forth. Dark sides were sought everywhere in order, on that basis, to accuse the Party leadership of being incapable, without ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, of coping with the tasks facing the country and the Party.

A special place in these accusations was reserved for Comrade STALIN, who, as emerged from the discussions, was to blame for everything.

Usually present at all the meetings of our organization were ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, KHARITONOV, BAKAEV, SHAROV, KUKLIN, ZALUTSKY, GESSEN, GERTIK, NAUMOV, GORSHENIN, BOGDAN, ELKOVICH, RAVICH Olga, PERIMOV, OLKHOVSKY, and a number of others whom I do not now remember. At the meetings I attended, the persons listed above were usually present in varying combinations.

In essence, these opposition nests were already active cells of a counterrevolutionary organization at that time. One can only regret that the Party tolerated these centers of counterrevolution for so long.

Question: In your testimony of December 19, 1934, you state that the question of a bloc with the Right was discussed at a number of meetings. Relate all the facts known to you concerning the formation of a bloc between your organization and the Right.

Answer: On one visit to Moscow in 1929, I was at ZINOVIEV's apartment, where I learned that disagreements in the Central Committee had become a fact, that the new opposition was headed by BUKHARIN, RYKOV, TOMSKY, and UGLANOV, and that this opposition had extensive connections and support within the Party. The Moscow, Leningrad, and other organizations were named, where a substantial section of the active membership shared the standpoint of the new opposition.

The discussion, in which ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and I participated — I do not now remember whether anyone else was there — revolved around the questions of how serious this new opposition was; what its chances of success were in the event of an open stand; what attitude our organization should take toward it; and whether it was possible to place our hopes on this opposition despite our disagreements with it on a number of questions. The most important issue in our discussion was whether STALIN could be defeated in a bloc with the new opposition. This whole set of questions was taken for discussion to the headquarters, at NAUMOV's and GERTIK's. I remember well that these questions were intensely discussed at every encounter. The appearance of the Right Opposition brought an extraordinarily strong revival to the ranks of the Zinovievist-Trotskyist organization, which had already begun to disintegrate, and, with the appearance of the Right Opposition, firm hopes of an imminent revanche began to revive in this milieu.

All members of the organization, and especially ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV, saw in a bloc with the Right the possibility, through the combined efforts of the Right and our organization, of forcing the leadership of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) to bring ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the Politburo.

During discussion of the possibility of an agreement with the Right, a number of demands on the Right began to take shape within the organization. One of these demands was that the Right, without waiting for a final agreement with us, should put forward a proposal to bring ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV into the Politburo. The next

requirement was to define precisely the program of immediate joint actions after the formation of the bloc.

Alongside the great revival that the question of a bloc with the Right brought among the oppositionists, this same question also brought a certain internal differentiation.

Among some oppositionists, the proposal for a bloc with the Right met with no sympathy. The main objections to the bloc amounted to the argument that it would be impossible to explain even to our own supporters why only yesterday we were marching with TROTSKY, whereas today we were marching with BUKHARIN, whose right-wing statements and the struggle against them were well known to the entire Party.

I recall that NAUMOV, ZALUTSKY, and I were among those who objected. In any event, the objection to the bloc tied ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's hands and prevented them from embarking on a new adventure.

On my next visit to Moscow that same year, 1929, I learned that negotiations over the bloc were still continuing and that KAMENEV had met with BUKHARIN, but that no agreement on the bloc had been reached.

A conversation on the formation of a bloc between our organization and the Right took place between ZINOVIEV and TOMSKY, at which I too was present.

It happened as follows:

At the end of 1929, ZINOVIEV and I were staying at the same time in Sochi, at the Central Executive Committee's dacha. On one occasion, ZINOVIEV warned me that TOMSKY had arrived at the neighboring dacha and that a conversation about the bloc might take place with him. ZINOVIEV went on to point out that we needed to decide beforehand how to behave and what answer to give TOMSKY. ZINOVIEV suggested that, after the failure of BUKHARIN's and KAMENEV's conversation, the Right was embittered against our organization and that TOMSKY might not even discuss the subject.

During the conversation, ZINOVIEV and I decided that, if TOMSKY began a conversation about the possibility of a bloc, we should keep the conversation going and, in its course, learn about the mood of the Right and its intentions; for our part, we should avoid answering the question of forming the bloc and indicate that the matter would be decided in Moscow.

A few days later, the conversation between ZINOVIEV and TOMSKY took place on the seashore near the Central Executive Committee's dacha. Besides ZINOVIEV and TOMSKY, SHMIDT Vasily, UGAROV Fedor, and I were present at this conversation.

TOMSKY began the conversation by saying that he would not reproach ZINOVIEV for the failure of the conversation between BUKHARIN and KAMENEV, and that he, TOMSKY, considered the delay in forming a bloc with the Right a great mistake on the part of our organization; that the Right had quite sufficient forces to oppose STALIN; that the accumulation of these forces was proceeding quite successfully; that the Right would soon give battle to the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks); and that by that time it would be highly desirable to unite the forces of the Right Opposition and our organization and for both these organizations to act as a united front against the Party's Central Committee.

During the conversation, ZINOVIEV adopted an indefinite position. ZINOVIEV told TOMSKY that he could not now give an answer on the question of forming a bloc with the Right and that this question would be decided in Moscow; that he was not authorized to make any statements; and that he would inform his like-minded associates in Moscow of his meeting and conversation with TOMSKY.

At the end of the conversation, TOMSKY proposed establishing an exchange of information between the Right and our organization. ZINOVIEV agreed. On the question of forming the bloc, TOMSKY declared: “As for your taking action, decide that as events develop. If you reach any decision, let Grisha FEDOROV come and inform me about it.”

The course of events, as is known, ended in the complete defeat of the Right. I do not know whether there were any further negotiations or meetings between the Right and representatives of our organization. —

Taken down correctly from my words and read by me. —

FEDOROV.

INTERROGATED BY: HEAD OF THE 3RD SECTION OF THE EKO OF THE GUGB OF THE USSR NKVD, CHERTOK.
