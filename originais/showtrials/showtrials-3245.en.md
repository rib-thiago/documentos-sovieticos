INTERROGATION RECORD

OF EVDOKIMOV, Grigory Eremeevich, dated 14 December 1934.

EVDOKIMOV, Grigory Eremeevich, born in 1884, former Semipalatinsk Region, Pavlodar; resides in Moscow, 29/6 Third Tverskaya-Yamskaya Street, apartment 7. Head of the Main Directorate of the Dairy Industry under the People's Commissariat of the Food Industry. Wife: Ksenia Vasilievna, 48; children: daughter Anna Grigorievna TARASOVA-EVDOKIMOVA, 29, lives in Kuzbass; brother Mikhail, 52, lives in Leningrad, 28/25 Krasnykh Zor Street, apartment 23; brother Terenty, 46, contact lost; sister Elena, 48, lives at brother Mikhail's address. Without property. Education: three-class school; VKP(b) member since 1903, with an interruption from the Fifteenth Party Congress until June 1928. In Pavlodar in 1903–1909. In Omsk from 1909 to 1913, did no Party work. In Leningrad from 1913 until the October Revolution and afterwards until 1926. In 1908 under investigation for eight months on charges of belonging to the RSDLP. Arrested in Leningrad in 1915 and exiled; returned after the February Revolution.

QUESTION: The investigation has information that you are a member of the centre of the former Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc's counter-revolutionary organisation, which maintained contacts with the illegal counter-revolutionary organisation in Leningrad. What can you testify about this?

ANSWER: I personally did no work to sustain counter-revolutionary anti-Party sentiments outside the narrow circle of people I met: ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, BAKAEV, GERTIK, FEDOROV, SHAROV, KUKLIN, KOSTINA, GESSEN, GORSHENIN, KOZHURO, SAFAROV and SHATSKIN.

With these persons I discussed various current Party decisions. Our assessment of Party decisions and attitude towards the leadership depended directly on the country's situation. Our critical sentiments intensified during economic difficulties in the country and subsided as the Party overcame them.

Our sentiments in 1932 may illustrate this. At that time SAFAROV asked me to bring him together with ZINOVIEV to discuss the difficulties the country was experiencing. SAFAROV raised the question of how Party members who considered that in those circumstances the Central Committee was not taking the necessary measures to overcome the difficulties should react to them.

SAFAROV and I said — and this was the general sentiment of the persons listed above — that the main obstacle to overcoming difficulties was insufficient inner-Party democracy; that a regime had been established in the Party which did not allow questions about the country's situation to be submitted for discussion by the entire Party with sufficient completeness to ensure the maximum mobilisation of its forces to overcome the difficulties. In substance, we blamed the Central Committee for failing fully to inform the Party of the true situation in the country.

In my conversations with SAFAROV, ZINOVIEV and BAKAEV — I do not remember whether I spoke to anybody else — the question arose of submitting a statement to the Party Central Committee about the country's situation and measures we considered necessary. In discussing these “necessary measures”, we found nothing besides a discussion within the Party. ZINOVIEV's exile in connection with the Ryutin case put an end to all these conversations.

I recall that in 1929, when I was in Leningrad, Boris KUSHNER proposed at M. NATANSON's apartment a bloc with the Rightists based on creating a “Leninist Politburo”, that is, returning RYKOV, BUKHARIN, TOMSKY, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV to the Politburo. I sharply condemned this bloc at the time.

In conversations with ZINOVIEV, BAKAEV and others we returned to the “Leninist Politburo” question several times in subsequent years on various occasions. Thus, for example, in 1932 SAFAROV raised it in conversation with me as one possible way of establishing an appropriate Party leadership. After speaking with me, SAFAROV talked with ZINOVIEV at the latter's dacha. I do not know whether he raised the “Leninist Politburo” question with him, but afterwards ZINOVIEV told me SAFAROV's attitude towards him had changed fundamentally and was now the same as before their break.

Our circle seized on any rumour containing the slightest hint of disagreements in the Central Committee Politburo, always associating this with hopes that the disagreements would deepen and become the subject of more or less broad Party discussion, allowing us actively to intervene and seek a change in leadership. Such rumours about disagreements among Politburo members also circulated among us in 1934, but that year they were no longer linked to any prospects of changing the leadership.

I personally always opposed forming a “Leninist Politburo”, considering, first, that discussions of its personal composition were unprincipled without regard to our fundamental disagreements with the Rightists, which were far deeper than whatever at a particular moment caused us to criticise the Party line. Secondly, I considered the very hope of changing the leadership groundless. I was sceptical of rumours about disagreements in the Politburo. The most I considered possible for us was a statement to the Central Committee presenting our assessment of the country's situation.

During our discussions with SAFAROV about submitting a statement to the Central Committee in 1932, SHATSKIN asked whether we had a centre. If so, he proposed establishing mutual informational contact. According to him, they had no formally constituted centre, but he asked to be informed if we adopted any decision about submitting a statement. I answered that we had no centre and declared that even mentioning this was very dangerous. I categorically refused mutual information. Which organisation or group SHATSKIN represented remains unclear to me to this day. I remember conversations in our circle in early 1933 about depersonalising such leading functions as the chairmanships of the All-Union Central Council of Trade Unions and the Comintern. We assessed this as an attempt to prevent strong, influential collective bodies from forming around the persons heading these organisations, and to subordinate all branches of leadership exclusively to the Politburo, particularly Comrade STALIN.

Regarding the Comintern, ZINOVIEV considered that its leadership, represented by MANUILSKY, PYATNITSKY and others, was not equal to the situation, and that consequently the largest fraternal Communist parties in the West, such as the German Communist Party, lacked firm directives corresponding to the actual situation at the most critical moments.

Comrade KAGANOVICH's theses on organisational questions for the Seventeenth Party Congress were assessed positively by ZINOVIEV and myself. GERTIK and GORSHENIN opposed them. They argued that abolishing the Central Control Commission contradicted everything LENIN had written and said on the matter.

QUESTION: What can you say about the political sentiments of individual persons in ZINOVIEV's circle whom you listed?

ANSWER: In my opinion, those most loyal towards Party policy and leadership were SHAROV, KUKLIN and FEDOROV. Of these three, FEDOROV was the most loyal. After KOSTINA was expelled from the Party for anti-Party statements in 1932, worked in production and was reinstated, her sentiments changed sharply and she began opposing all anti-Party conversations. The others' sentiments are characterised by my testimony above.

QUESTION: When and at which Zinovievist meetings did you participate?

ANSWER: I was only once at a meeting planned in an organised fashion to address a previously specified question, at BAKAEV's apartment in 1932. We discussed how to react to ZINOVIEV's expulsion from the Party. In this connection there was a preliminary conference at GORSHENIN's apartment. Those gathered there were myself, GORSHENIN, KOZHURO and BAKAEV; I do not remember whether anybody else was present. We discussed how to behave at the forthcoming Party meetings devoted to the RYUTIN and company case, in connection with ZINOVIEV's expulsion. The same question was discussed at BAKAEV's meeting. Present were BAKAEV, KOSTINA, myself, SHAROV and GERTIK. We decided not to speak in defence of ZINOVIEV, acknowledging that he had again committed a major mistake that could not be justified, and that such interventions would complicate his own situation, since they would be interpreted as made with his knowledge.

The record has been read. Correctly taken down from my words:

EVDOKIMOV.

INTERROGATED BY — RUTKOVSKY.
