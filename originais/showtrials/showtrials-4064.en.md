DIRECT-LINE MESSAGE.

From Leningrad.

TO THE PEOPLE’S COMMISSAR FOR INTERNAL AFFAIRS OF THE USSR, Comrade YAGODA.

TO THE DEPUTY PEOPLE’S COMMISSAR FOR INTERNAL AFFAIRS OF THE USSR, Comrade AGRANOV.

I forward BAKAEV’s testimony of January 4 of this year. BAKAEV’s interrogation continues. HEAD OF THE EKO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD — MIRONOV.

Interrogation record of BAKAEV, Ivan Petrovich — January 4, 1935.

QUESTION: It follows from your testimony of January 3, 1935, about the anti-party Zinovievist group to which you belonged that this group was essentially counterrevolutionary. Do you acknowledge this?

ANSWER: Yes. I do. Since the course of events set the group against the party and Soviet power, it was undoubtedly counterrevolutionary in essence.

QUESTION: How did the counterrevolutionary group to which you belonged arise?

ANSWER: The emergence of the counterrevolutionary group should be dated to the time immediately after the 15th Congress of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks). A considerable portion of the former Zinovievist oppositionists who returned to the party did not abandon opposition views. At that time there were two paths before us all: the path the Trotskyists took, the path of creating a second political organization, the path of a direct break with the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), the path of direct counterrevolutionary struggle; and the path of returning to the party. We chose the second path, although this cost no little effort. I remember that at one of the meetings at Zinoviev’s apartment shortly after the congress, either ZINOVIEV or LASHEVICH pointed out that all of us former oppositionists must certainly maintain close relations with one another and not sever personal ties, otherwise we might be suspected of conspiracy. I think this was the first instruction from the leaders of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground to gather forces. These personal ties became the basis on which political contacts were maintained and developed, forming us former opposition activists who met one another into a closed political collective in which there was close mutual protection. We members of the counterrevolutionary group generally met at one another’s apartments, quite regularly, once a month, sometimes twice a month. The meetings took place at the apartments of ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and GERTIK. Alongside this, we met at my apartment and at the apartments of GORSHENIN, I. S., KUKLIN, A. S., and EVDOKIMOV, G. E. Every oppositionist who had not lost personal contact with any of us and came from Leningrad, Rostov-on-Don, or other cities knew that by meeting us he could obtain information on political questions and fresh news. In this respect ZINOVIEV’s apartment, always open to old political friends and comrades-in-arms, had an especially magnetic attraction for such elements. The information was always counterrevolutionary in essence. It covered every question of domestic and foreign policy and was usually presented in a politically sophisticated form. Thus the threads of political contacts gradually extended downward from ZINOVIEV’s apartment, through our apartments, to Moscow comrades, directly to Leningrad and other places; the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground was created and strengthened, and the Moscow counterrevolutionary Zinovievist center crystallized, its voice heeded by participants in the Zinovievist groups. Who belonged to this center? — The center included ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, EVDOKIMOV, FEDOROV, GORSHENIN, KUKLIN, SHAROV, and myself, BAKAEV. This center was elected by no one and appointed by no one. The comrades knew us and trusted us. The existence of an authoritative center led to the creation of stable cadres who followed us. These cadres were educated in a spirit of opposition and hostility toward the leadership of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), headed by STALIN. Our center had no positive program it could set against the party’s Central Committee. There was only malicious, hostile criticism of the party’s most important measures; this criticism matched that of the White Guard degenerates of “Latest News” [1], and therein lies the tragedy of our situation. In our struggle against the party, in our malicious attacks on it, we slid straight into the White Guard swamp, the counterrevolutionary swamp. Lenin was a thousand times right when he said that the logic of struggle leads every opposition to direct, undisguised counterrevolution. Such is the outcome one must acknowledge, whether one wishes to or not. In my subsequent testimony I shall frankly, concealing nothing from the party and Soviet power, tell everything else I know about the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist underground.

The record has been written correctly from my words and read by me — BAKAEV.

Interrogated by the assistant head of the EKO of the GUGB of the NKVD of the USSR — DMITRIEV.

Certified correct: Biryukov
