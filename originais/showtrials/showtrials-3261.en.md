INTERROGATION RECORD

conducted on December 15, 1934, by the HEAD OF THE 1ST SECTION OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB OF THE NKVD

RUTKOVSKY

GESSEN, Sergei Mikhailovich, born in 1898 in the city of Batum; his father, Mikhail Isidorovich GESSEN, was an inspector of the Siberian Bank before the revolution. Residence: Smolensk, 6 Sotsialisticheskaya Street, apartment 10. NKTP representative for the Western Region. Wife: Ivyanskaya, Gertruda Isakovna, aged 26; daughter Irina, aged 2. Completed secondary school; a member of the VKP(b) since 1916. A break from the Fifteenth Party Congress until June 1928. In 1916, at the underground printing shop in Leningrad's Vyborg district; subsequently a propagandist in the same district. Following arrest in the Narva district, he was charged under Part 1 of Article 102. He was not convicted because of the revolution. Military registration category: PP — 10.

Question: What can you testify concerning the counterrevolutionary, anti-Party activities of former participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc and your participation in the activities of this organization?

Answer: After the Fifteenth Party Congress, the former Zinovievists submitted a declaration accepting the congress's decisions, not because they were convinced these decisions were correct, but because of the crushing ideological and political defeat they had suffered in their struggle against the Party.

Inwardly, the former Zinovievists had not overcome their previous views and hoped that, under favorable conditions, they could put their old baggage into practice in one form or combination or another and secure a corresponding change in the Party leadership.

The anti-Party activities of the Zinovievist group in the period since the Fifteenth Party Congress should be divided into three periods. The first covers 1928 and 1929. During this period the Zinovievist group had a clear tactical plan. It counted on the development of the Rightists' struggle against the Party, which would produce conditions for independent action by the Zinovievist group and a change in the Party leadership. The policy of double-dealing practiced by the Zinovievist group followed from this tactical orientation. Negotiations were conducted with the Rightists while, at the same time, the Party Central Committee was given assurances of complete solidarity and loyalty. The Zinovievist group's tactical positions at that time are expressed in two well-known documents: KAMENEV's record of his conversation with BUKHARIN and ZINOVIEV's letter, which was kept by SHVALBE.

The second period covers 1930–1932. The failure of hopes for any reasonably successful action by the Rightists became clear. Retaining the same objective of replacing the leadership, the Zinovievist group had no clear tactical plan and set itself the immediate task of preserving the group while awaiting further developments. At the same time, the Zinovievists paid exceptional attention to the activities of other anti-Party groups and organizations. In particular, I met repeatedly at that time with the so-called Leftists (LOMINADZE and SHATSKIN), learning through them about their positions, activities, and so on. The Zinovievist group's activities became particularly vigorous in the second half of 1932, in connection with the general revival of activity throughout the anti-Party underground. At this point contacts with the Leftists (chiefly STEN) became particularly frequent, and efforts to sound out the attitudes of other anti-Party formations and discontented sections also intensified considerably.

The most characteristic manifestation of these links with other anti-Party organizations was STEN's delivery of the Ryutin platform to ZINOVIEV and the latter's concealment of it from the Party. At the same time, some former participants in the Zinovievist-Trotskyist bloc who had previously broken with ZINOVIEV began visiting him; for example, I know that SAFAROV began visiting ZINOVIEV during this period. The Zinovievist group's hopes rested on the expectation that the general weakening of Party discipline and cohesion, together with the undermining of the Party leadership's influence, would enable it to attempt political action. It was nevertheless considered that such action would be hopeless unless some crack appeared in the leading circle. Hence the particularly watchful attention to everything happening within the Party leadership, the gathering and circulation of various rumors about disagreements, friction, and so forth within the Politburo and Central Committee. I recall, for example, once speaking with KAMENEV (in the summer of 1932) about whether we had any chance of taking action, and receiving this reply: “As long as the top leadership is united, we cannot move. We have already made a mistake once; we cannot afford to make a second one.” As for talk of personal and political friction within the Party leadership, I particularly recall rumors about MOLOTOV's alleged dissatisfaction with his organizational position; these rumors were rather vague.

The third period covers 1933–1934. This period is characterized by a mood of hopelessness and lack of prospects. This mood resulted, first, from the definitive realization that any attempts to replace or undermine the existing Party leadership were completely hopeless. It became entirely clear that all calculations based on internal friction within it were false; that the Party would accept no other leadership; and that the influence and popularity of the Party leadership among active Party workers and the broad mass of Party members had reached unprecedented heights. Second, our inability to counter the Party's general line with any reasonably well-defined political position of our own became particularly apparent.

I can say nothing about ZINOVIEV's and KAMENEV's state of mind during this period. I did not see KAMENEV at all, and saw ZINOVIEV only once, shortly after the Seventeenth Party Congress, when he was still under the immediate impression of the favorable reception his speech had received at the congress and spoke chiefly of his impressions of the congress.

The record has been read to me. It has been written correctly, in accordance with my words.

GESSEN
