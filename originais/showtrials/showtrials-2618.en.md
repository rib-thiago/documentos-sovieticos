PRESIDING JUDGE: Defendant NIKOLAEV, you are charged with having, as a member of a terrorist underground counter-revolutionary group formed from participants in the former Zinovievist anti-Soviet group in Leningrad and a member of the underground counter-revolutionary terrorist centre, the “Leningrad Centre”, carried out on its instructions a terrorist act against Comrade KIROV on 1 December 1934. Do you plead guilty?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Fully?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: I have several questions for you.

Please state what you did before your arrest, that is, from the time you became an adult; what positions did you hold?

NIKOLAEV: I worked in production for 18 years. Half of them I was in production and half in Party and Soviet work, from the village soviet to the Regional Control Commission of the Workers' and Peasants' Inspectorate, at the K. MARX factory, in Vyborg and Luga Districts and also in the Leningrad Regional Party Committee.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In what capacity did you work in the Leningrad Regional Committee?

NIKOLAEV: As a referent.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What education do you have; what did you complete?

NIKOLAEV: After 1917 I studied at the Electrotechnical Institute, then prepared to enter …… [2] University, and in 1933–1934 prepared to enter the VKP(b).

PRESIDING JUDGE: You testified that you belonged to the Zinovievist anti-Soviet grouping in 1926–1927 and so forth. Which defendants in this case worked with you, or with whom did you have occasion to work?

NIKOLAEV: With KOTOLYNOV, ANTONOV, SOKOLOV, NIKANOROV, ZVEZDOV, POPOV, KHANIK and others.

PRESIDING JUDGE: You know half the defendants well?

NIKOLAEV: Yes, and SOSITSKY.

PRESIDING JUDGE: How do you explain that your other comrades were expelled from the Party while you escaped any penalty? How is that explained? By chance, or had your true character not been sufficiently exposed?

NIKOLAEV: Yes, by chance.

PRESIDING JUDGE: You gave a considerable amount of very detailed testimony about Comrade KIROV's murder, containing a number of facts also confirmed by other defendants in this case. We are interested in clarifying a few more details connected with your testimony; in particular, we are interested in KOTOLYNOV's role in organising the terrorist act against Comrade KIROV.

Do you confirm all your testimony concerning KOTOLYNOV? Can you add anything, or do you consider your testimony exhaustive?

NIKOLAEV: I wish to add something. At the confrontation KOTOLYNOV and SHATSKY denied participating in preparations for organising the terrorist act against Comrade KIROV, but I wish to confess before the court, which I did not indicate during the investigation, that confirmation can only be provided by a chest I concealed, fastened with a hoop, in my mother's shed, containing documents and standing among the firewood [3].

PRESIDING JUDGE: What is in the chest?

NIKOLAEV: Documents testifying to connections with these opposition leaders and to past work and so forth.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What period do these documents belong to?

NIKOLAEV: An earlier period. One of the opposition documents was recently shown to me; it was sewn into a sack, but I received that one through ANTONOV in 1932; I kept the other documents.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Where are they?

NIKOLAEV: On Lesnoy Prospekt, at my mother's.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Your mother does not know about this?

NIKOLAEV: No.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Where is this box or chest? Is it buried in the ground?

NIKOLAEV: Under the firewood.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Is the shed in the courtyard?

NIKOLAEV: No, downstairs, an ordinary shed below the first floor.

PRESIDING JUDGE: When did you last open this chest?

NIKOLAEV: It was placed there shortly before I committed the terrorist act.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Is there a whole archive there?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Documents from what period, what year?

NIKOLAEV: Beginning in 1934, we worked together, KOTOLYNOV and a whole number of defendants in this case, in Komsomol and Party work until 1931–1932.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Until 1932?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Documents of your joint work with KOTOLYNOV, or with whom else?

NIKOLAEV: With the whole group, and besides Leningrad, in Leningrad Region too.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Why did this chest come to be in your possession, in your keeping? Did you put it there yourself?

NIKOLAEV: Yes, all the documents were with me.

PRESIDING JUDGE: With you?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: You met KOTOLYNOV over many years of working together?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: And SHATSKY?

NIKOLAEV: Until 1927. Then SHATSKY left. He was away for a long time: he went to Stalingrad and returned in 1933, but I met him in February.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In February of what year?

NIKOLAEV: 1934.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you meet under the circumstances you testified to earlier?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: So you confirm that testimony as regards SHATSKY?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Do you also confirm his participation in preparing terrorist acts?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Do you wish to add anything in this respect concerning SHATSKY, or do you have no new information with which to reconstruct the entire picture? All this testimony of yours has been confirmed, so it would be desirable to illuminate the whole picture of this sad event.

NIKOLAEV: I have nothing new to add.

PRESIDING JUDGE: One more episode requiring clarification: you said this archive contains a document establishing your connection with workers in Leningrad Region. Which ones specifically, and what period?

NIKOLAEV: When I was in Luga.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Which of the defendants was connected with you?

NIKOLAEV: ZVEZDOV.

PRESIDING JUDGE: One more question concerning your consulate visits. There are several unclear points requiring clarification. You testified two or three times that you visited the Latvian consul three or four times?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Approximately when was your first meeting? When did your first visit take place?

NIKOLAEV: On a date in the twenties of September.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What was the subject of your first conversation?

NIKOLAEV: At first, a private matter. I told him that supposedly my parents, who had died some five or ten years earlier, had left property abroad. I paused, waiting… He asked what property: movable or immovable? I looked at him again and said: probably money. But the purpose of my visit was clear, and I immediately told him: about money.

PRESIDING JUDGE: This was the first time?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: And what did you indicate?

NIKOLAEV: That my parents had supposedly left money in Riga.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you give an address?

NIKOLAEV: No. The consul did not inquire about details; we agreed I would make further inquiries — where and what — in order to find out precisely through you what the parents had left for their children. Of course I have no parents there… And I asked him to receive me again. Now, I said, it is inconvenient here (the secretary was sitting there [4]); better receive me another day and earlier than the usual reception, in the morning, around nine or ten.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you touch on questions of Party policy in the first conversation?

NIKOLAEV: No.

PRESIDING JUDGE: When were you there the second time?

NIKOLAEV: Again in September, right in the midst of an air-raid alarm. This time we talked alone, and I felt freer before him, felt a certain support.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you speak quite freely?

NIKOLAEV: Yes, yes. The consul looked so sympathetic that I treated him trustingly, and he treated me the same way.

PRESIDING JUDGE: You were shown a photograph; is this it? (Shows photograph.)

NIKOLAEV: Yes, yes. I told him I was a Red Professor and a member of an underground organisation.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you name the organisation?

NIKOLAEV: Well, an opposition current; that we were conducting a struggle within the Party, within the VKP.

Then in this conversation I explained that I wanted to send material abroad, anti-Soviet material about the internal situation in the Soviet Union, current Party policy, economic questions and so on, with harsh criticism of all these things. After that I turned to discussing the establishment of contact with TROTSKY. PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you state definitely that you wished to contact TROTSKY specifically?

NIKOLAEV: Yes, as a former opposition leader we needed to establish contact with him for the further dissemination of our materials. On the organisation's instructions, we did not think that the consulate — a bourgeois country, a fascist state — could fully reflect, being such a neighbour of the Soviet Union, reflect our indignation, our criticism. We wanted to use the consulate's apparatus to obtain such funds.

PRESIDING JUDGE: When you told the consul you wanted to contact TROTSKY, how did he respond to this proposal?

NIKOLAEV: For him it was the same as the material. I also asked for money for the material. I could not suggest how contact should be established, but he simply agreed to all my explanations.

PRESIDING JUDGE: You intended to contact TROTSKY through the consul and transmit material — to whom? To TROTSKY?

NIKOLAEV: No, to contact him, to transmit something initially, but the material…

PRESIDING JUDGE: I am asking how you spoke about TROTSKY; did the entire conversation revolve around TROTSKY? Did you not name the organisation you wished to contact abroad?

NIKOLAEV: I said: an organisation of an opposition character working within the Party…

PRESIDING JUDGE: No, not yours; the organisation abroad. Did you name only TROTSKY or another organisation?

NIKOLAEV: Only TROTSKY.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Was the consul not surprised that you wished to contact TROTSKY precisely through him, a consul?

NIKOLAEV: He was surprised that I had come to him, surprised at the whole conversation, but still looked approvingly.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Are you describing your second conversation with the consul now?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you have the materials with you then?

NIKOLAEV: No, I might have been arrested; I thought of doing it later, but had no material then. I needed to sound out the ground.

PRESIDING JUDGE: How did the consul respond to your proposal?

NIKOLAEV: He agreed that I should call on him and talk again, and that he would help.

PRESIDING JUDGE: That was your second visit; when was the third conversation?

NIKOLAEV: All the conversations were in September. I did not reach the fourth meeting because I became suspicious.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What happened in the third conversation?

NIKOLAEV: We talked about support. He did not touch on TROTSKY, of course; he had said that during the second conversation. Now we were talking about material support, and I presented a Latvian citizen's passport.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Where did you get the passport?

NIKOLAEV: From relatives on my wife's side. A passport dating from 1917, sent by my wife's grandfather. I presented this passport to gain confidence, since the question was money, and I received money amounting to 5,000 rubles.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What did the consul give you the money for? Because you had supplied material, or did you receive an advance?

NIKOLAEV: I asked for support as a Latvian citizen, saying I had money, that we would receive money, but when the conversation assumed the character of anti-Soviet friendship, he gave money. No material was sent abroad. I knew the address from the passport and already had contact by telephone. I then promised to provide material, and he decided the matter without any doubts. He needed to support me and encouraged me, receiving me at nine or ten, usually two hours before reception hours; but later, when I came a fourth time, it seemed suspicious: I had received the money, but they wanted to catch me. Just then there was a military car; I went up to the first door and came back down.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What has the military car to do with it?

NIKOLAEV: I thought it was a Military Tribunal car; perhaps the consul had informed the Commissariat.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What has the Military Tribunal to do with it?

NIKOLAEV: I thought this was treason against the motherland, and there was also a suspicious man at the main entrance; nevertheless I decided to go ahead, took the number and went upstairs, then came to my senses; there was some noise behind the doors just then, and I came back down. I remember the car's number, I think 106-81, and afterwards I began watching this car to establish whether it had anything to do with me at that moment, meaning surveillance. I saw this car twice there, somewhere near that building. The car stopped, they even unloaded things from it, and I did not intend to appear there again but decided to arrange matters by telephone. Then the matter was postponed; I had no time to deal with it, but I had received the money.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did the consul give you the money immediately after the third meeting?

NIKOLAEV: After the third meeting we talked for half an hour or an hour.

PRESIDING JUDGE: A Latvian consul would not give you five thousand when you are not a Latvian citizen.

NIKOLAEV: Why? He could have drawn me into counter-revolutionary activity from the first time, and after the third conversation he gave me money.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What did you use this money for?

NIKOLAEV: Some time later I handed the money to Vanya KOTOLYNOV, taking a tenth of it for myself. We agreed to take some money for our own needs and some to organise trips to Moscow.

PRESIDING JUDGE: For what purpose?

NIKOLAEV: For the further organisation of terrorist acts in Moscow.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Before visiting the Latvian consul, did you have a conversation with KOTOLYNOV?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Who took the initiative and sent you to the consulate — KOTOLYNOV or you? Who gave whom the idea?

NIKOLAEV: KOTOLYNOV took the initiative in directing me; SHATSKY and I had an agreement in August. SHATSKY spoke of contact with a Comintern worker in Moscow, and I expressed a desire to contact a consulate in Leningrad.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Contact for what, to get in touch with people abroad?

NIKOLAEV: When we discussed the existence of the organisation of former oppositionists and terrorist sentiments, we already discussed this plan too, what exactly we needed. He then said that CHUDOV was an unsuitable candidate and that we needed to commit an act against Stalin. I had contact with RUMYANTSEV and SHATSKY, and I was advised to contact Vanya KOTOLYNOV. This was where the idea arose of obtaining funds and sending articles, since surveillance was taking place, terrorist groups existed, and these sentiments already existed during 1933–1934. Then he proposed, or rather gave me the idea of using a Comintern worker.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Use him how: to obtain funds or to restore contact abroad?

NIKOLAEV: To restore contact.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Were there such workers in the Comintern?

NIKOLAEV: Yes, there was a worker called LOMINADZE, who had been abroad several times; I do not know the others. I was not told about them.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Who raised the consulate question? You, SHATSKY or KOTOLYNOV?

NIKOLAEV: I happened to raise it in conversation with SHATSKY. When I met KOTOLYNOV, we discussed the Comintern and consulate, funds and establishing contacts.

PRESIDING JUDGE: In talking with SHATSKY and KOTOLYNOV, did you have funds or contacts in mind?

NIKOLAEV: Funds.

PRESIDING JUDGE: And contacts?

NIKOLAEV: As yet, the contact question concerned TROTSKY only.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you really want to contact TROTSKY, or did you mention him only for the consul?

NIKOLAEV: In conversation with KOTOLYNOV there emerged a firm and insistent decision to establish contact through TROTSKY with counter-revolutionary elements abroad, firstly; secondly, we thought, and he agreed to write a letter (he is a fellow who writes a lot), that we would receive funds for it.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Who is “he”?

NIKOLAEV: KOTOLYNOV.

PRESIDING JUDGE: What letter did he want to write?

NIKOLAEV: An article of an anti-Soviet character.

PRESIDING JUDGE: To publish in what newspaper?

NIKOLAEV: I do not know; we did not discuss that.

PRESIDING JUDGE: So you wished, on the one hand, to obtain funds for your work and, on the other, actually to contact TROTSKY?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Did you wish to contact any other foreign organisations?

NIKOLAEV: There were no other intentions during that period.

PRESIDING JUDGE: How did you intend to organise contact, this correspondence with TROTSKY?

NIKOLAEV: We raised this question through the consul. From this SHATSKY intended to use former Comintern workers who were oppositionists.

PRESIDING JUDGE: I am speaking of your contact with the consul.

NIKOLAEV: To transmit a letter through the consul.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Personally to TROTSKY or to other persons living abroad?

NIKOLAEV: There were no other persons.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Were there any intermediaries?

NIKOLAEV: There were none.

PRESIDING JUDGE: On the one hand you wanted money, on the other you wanted contact with TROTSKY, and thirdly you wanted to supply information?

NIKOLAEV: Yes.

PRESIDING JUDGE: Information for which organisation, which persons, and for what purposes?

NIKOLAEV: Unfortunately I cannot explain.

PRESIDING JUDGE: But what letter, of what kind?

For example, the Reichstag fire trial proceeds; one of the best speaks with enormous enthusiasm… and beside him trails… in order to carry out… \[44\]; instead of seeing DIMITROV's merits in speaking at this trial, our group undertook to undermine Comintern work and its quality. Here is one thing we did: we undermined confidence in Communist Party policy. In 1934 we experienced difficulties connected with sabotage and new countryside measures; instead of seeing this kulak sabotage, which caused some difficulty in Leningrad, we took the deterioration and charged it to the Communist Party leadership. All our associates knew this and naturally it did not remain just between us. Here is the second method of our work which led to the shot at S. M. Kirov. We criticized not only actions generally but individual people in leadership. I wish to ask the proletarian court for only one thing. I fully acknowledge all my guilt, that I have reached a limit beyond which there is nowhere to go. I ask the proletarian court for one mercy: if it finds it possible to give me the opportunity to make amends for this gravest crime of mine and at least somewhere have the happiness and possibility of calling myself a citizen of the land of the Soviets and prove my devotion and sincerity in the hardest labor, wherever it may be, just as I once proved through my participation in liquidating the Kronstadt mutiny, on the Kornilov front and the Latvian front.

I understand that it is ridiculous now to speak of any mercy, but I find the sole, even slightest, justification and right to ask the proletarian court in feeling that I am not an utterly lost man, that I will be capable honestly, together with the entire proletariat, under the leadership of our great Leninist party, led by Comrade STALIN, who leads our country from one victory to another, at least someday to make amends for a fraction of the immense crime I committed. That alone is what I ask the proletarian court. Lastly, regardless of anything, crush, destroy, leaving nothing of this counterrevolutionary Zinovievist and Kamenevist organization, leave not even its spirit, to enable the land of the Soviets to advance further, so that it… \[68\] star which illuminates the path of the entire world proletariat. That is all I considered necessary to say in my final statement and to ask the court, that I sent a statement to the CC of the AUCP(b) in which I also asked for this.

RUMYANTSEV: Chairman and members of the Supreme Court, I plead guilty. Until the last day I belonged to the counterrevolutionary organization in Leningrad, knew of its existence, and failed to find the courage to expose it in time and thereby prevent that nightmarish murder of our best pupil of LENIN and comrade-in-arms of Comrade STALIN, Sergei Mironovich KIROV. I am grateful to Comrades AGRANOV, MIRONOV, and LYUSHKOV, who helped me break with the counterrevolutionary swamp. It is hard to appear for the first time in one's life before a military proletarian court when one recognizes all the shame of one's appearance, recognizes one's gravest crime, understands that one was raised and educated by Soviet power. I am a Red Army volunteer, a Chekist of 1920–1921, guarded an ammunition depot against explosions, and found myself in the position of an enemy of my motherland, a scoundrel and counterrevolutionary. I am thirty-two; for sixteen of those years, my entire conscious life, I was in the party and Komsomol. I owe my political life, as a result of the education I received, to the “leaders” of the counterrevolutionary group, ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. I do not absolve myself of personal responsibility, since the crime I committed is great and immense. But there was a time when, still a young adolescent, I was educated in the Leningrad Komsomol and did not know the full role; I believed in ZINOVIEV as in a god, gave him my pure heart, recklessly subordinated my consciousness, believed so deeply that in my sincere devotion I did not notice what I was doing. I believed in ZINOVIEV's authority, believed EVDOKIMOV that ZINOVIEV was a true pupil of LENIN, and from this flows the immense crime. That is why I declare here before the court of the Military Collegium that before the Fourteenth Party Congress I set the Komsomol against the party. I organized the discrediting of Politburo members. I organized a Komsomol center, introduced the learning of anti-Soviet songs, “Little Bricks” and others, directed against the party leadership; created doubts and undermined the authority of the party and government concerning any decision. I grossly violated party discipline. I distributed illegal literature among non-party people and Komsomol members. I did all this under the slogans “not caught, not a thief” and “lie to and deceive the leading apparatus.”

In 1927 ZINOVIEV's bloc with TROTSKY was carried out. I went through quite a school during 1920–1921. On 7 November 1927 I returned to Leningrad after Vladivostok and Shanghai, where there had been an uprising in 1927; I participated with GESSEN in drafting an outrageous platform on the Komsomol and then the bloc's platform. I did not believe in building socialism in our country and instilled this in others. That is why, unnoticed by me, at any discussion or assessment, it appeared to everyone around in a different form. \[69\] This explains why, resolutely implementing ZINOVIEV's directive to move to fascist methods of struggle, before the Fifteenth Congress I went into the streets with anti-Soviet slogans. All this led to anti-Soviet unbridled behavior, and I bear direct responsibility for it. Then came the stage concerning the transition of members of the counterrevolutionary organization. You know that the Fifteenth Congress recognized Zinovievists as counterrevolutionaries. During the congress's work the bloc disintegrated, and ZINOVIEV submitted a statement of formal withdrawal. The Trotskyists took the party's path \[70\], while the Zinovievists split into the group of “leaderless ones” — TARKHANOV, BARDIN, NAUMOV, and others I named during the investigation. This group could not abandon its views in a single day, but essentially we wavered between Zinovievists and Trotskyists.

By way of characterization one can indicate that they expressed their attitude toward the Fifteenth Congress in that members of the organization not expelled from the party did not speak condemning counterrevolutionary views, did not vote for the congress decisions, and usually went to the lavatory, to smoke, and so forth, in order not to vote.

After 3 June, serious sentiments in favor of returning to the party arose among the young, but when we negotiated with TARASOV and KOTOLYNOV, with Comrade YAROSLAVSKY, the Zinovievists literally dragged us by the sleeves into the party, and KAMENEV edited our letters and statements, and thereby we introduced all the falsity upon returning to the party.

Arriving from Moscow in Leningrad with this directive, we persuaded the young: renounce propaganda, do not condemn your views, do not stop factional work. That is, for more than eight months after the Fifteenth Party Congress, instead of showing the whole abyss toward which these young people were heading, we not only did not stop but on the contrary persuaded them to adopt a double-dealing position upon returning to the party.

In 1928 ZINOVIEV, after submitting his statement, came to Leningrad. At some point during this period an illegal meeting took place at SHAROV's apartment, to which the “leaderless ones” were invited. I was there. ZINOVIEV then also inaugurated this counterrevolutionary movement in the party. In response to the questions put, he formulated the following tasks for this counterrevolutionary organization: ZINOVIEV said there were two wings in the party, the centrist wing headed by STALIN and the Right, BUKHARINIST wing. One cannot take another path, one must return to the party; the slogan of an active offensive against capitalist elements, he said, we achieved through a hard struggle against the party. Although we paid dearly for that struggle, the slogan of an active offensive against capitalist elements is nevertheless our victory, ZINOVIEV's victory. ZINOVIEV thus assessed the Fifteenth Congress decisions as a definite victory of his struggle, that the slogan of active offensive justified his struggle, and created a basis for the return of double-dealing elements to the party.

ZINOVIEV said that the threat from the Right had passed.

The “leaderless ones” united on a parity basis, fearing a Zinovievist maneuver, since he, proceeding from the fact that the threat from the Right had passed, set the task of organizing the preservation of contacts and so forth. There was lengthy argument about the center's composition. ZINOVIEV insisted that there should be more of their members in the center; the “leaderless ones” would not agree. This rigmarole continued for a long time. Finally this illegal center was approved personally by ZINOVIEV. This center lived until early 1929. I myself went with TARASOV and NAUMOV to KAMENEV's home; he recounted that famous conversation with BUKHARIN, but the most outrageous, most indecent gutter abuse against the party leadership was used by KAMENEV. NAUMOV remarked that the very fact of talking to BUKHARIN constituted a bloc with the Right.

KAMENEV attacked Comrade STALIN with exceptional fury. This badger in a Japanese dressing gown declared: “What kind of leader is STALIN? He has not a single scholarly work on the elaboration of socialist economy and proletarian revolution. He collects BUKHARIN's ideas and proposals, ZINOVIEV's, and mine.” Such is the point these rogues reached.

Having nothing to propose in opposition to the party's general line, party policy, and the country's economic policy, this nest was left with only hatred. The only slogan was “For a Leninist Politburo with ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV.”

Is it not a disgrace, a horror, what I spoke of at the beginning, when KUKHAREV — an old Bolshevik who in 1917 led the best detachment against the Winter Palace — declares: “Let the heart of the proletarian revolution stop, so that in a year's time ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV may stand at the head of our party.”

One must reach such degeneration, such White-Guardism, that I… \[71\] the proletarian court and Military Tribunal to seize this counterrevolution by the throat. It must be finished. This scum brought our young people too to such a sad outcome. The finest impulses, many years have been broken. Now one understands all one's guilt and all one's crime.

I met Zinovievists and spoke with them. I worked fourteen or fifteen hours a day. I worked days and nights in the Vyborg Soviet, at the House of Culture. … \[72\] people worked here, one feels excellent, one has spirits and enthusiasm, but when one meets a Zinovievist, there is whining, slander, slander against Soviet power, defamation of the party, which like sticky dirt enters one's ears, consciousness, heart; and this trait of double-dealing lived in me until today. It had as a consequence of that education, deeply the domination \[73\]. I made attempts to tear them out definitively. I acknowledge all my gravest crime before the party and Soviet Government. I did not belong to the terrorist group and knew nothing of its existence. Believe me, had I known that KOTOLYNOV's hand was organizing murder, I myself would have put a bullet into him and handed him over to the OGPU. Notes came to me and I submitted them to the OGPU; this is known, that some process was going on within me. I do not know NIKOLAEV, did not speak with ZVEZDOV, do not know YUSKIN, did not speak or meet with SOKOLOV. I reduced all my meetings to a minimum. I tried to withdraw from this but did not withdraw. I am guilty of this, I acknowledge all this guilt; this is still a remnant of my last, and I shall free myself here in court, since my conscience is clear: I did not participate in terror or defend it. One must be a ferocious enemy who hates Soviet power, or a beast, to commit such a deed in the proletarian state, unseen in the world, surpassing the deaths of Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg. And the danger lies all the more in the fact that a communist fired; an open enemy is a hundred times better than a hidden one, while a communist is a thousand times more dangerous.

I understand and acknowledge this, and this counterrevolutionary Zinovievist group must be eliminated with a red-hot iron. I cursed the day of my life when I took the path of struggle against the party. I broke with this counterrevolutionary nest and swamp; I ask you here, Supreme Collegium and supreme judges, if you find even the slightest possibility of preserving my life in the Soviet Union and returning to bright days and a new joyful, so radiant, flourishing revolutionary country. I swear I will go to any labor and sector of work if given the opportunity. With self-sacrificing devotion I shall atone for my gravest crime.

MANDELSHTAM: Comrades! Of course, the most terrible, most shameful thing is to find oneself in the dock in such a pitiful role. All this, of course, proletarian revolutionary human dignity is dearer than life, comrades, to anyone who had or has any conviction; I do not wish to repeat myself, deliver political lectures here, make excursions into the past, because through the materials of the preliminary investigation in the case of those sitting in the dock here today and the case of those who will yet sit here, all this is sufficiently correct. I confirm all the materials set out here by defendant RUMYANTSEV; of course, our guilty ones are those at whom all the defendants pointed the finger. The proletarian state, the party, and Soviet power will call them to account for this, and today they call us to account. So, comrades, and all defendants, if you have indeed disarmed, then manage to rise to the position of the guard, to the position of Soviet power, to the position of the proletarian court and say: no mercy, shoot everyone to the last man, shoot everyone. Comrades! I am not a counterrevolutionary terrorist. It is, of course, for the court to believe me or not, but in the past I was a sufficiently serious political figure and managed to reach the point where such a blow on the head made me understand the whole vileness of my position, understand into what abyss I had slid. Therefore your answer, the just answer, the answer of the only worthy proletarian court, the answer which will be applauded by the entire Leningrad proletariat and those among the defendants who find the courage, although it concerns them personally, the sole answer can be execution by shooting for all without exception.

Comrades! I ask you for only one mercy, just humanly, because politically you will do your work by execution, but humanly I ask you for one thing: comrades, I am an old fighter, it is hard for me to die like a dog, so I ask you, comrades, to allow me to take this shot in the chest and not in the back of the head, as is usual. I ask you for this, dear comrades. Then I ask you before death, but I believe and do not want to lie in the last minute before death: if you find it possible, give me permission to see my wife and write a letter to the leader of our party. Such are my last requests.

Long live your severe, merciless sentence against all of us who slid into the vile and repulsive counterrevolutionary shell.

CHAIRMAN: Defendant ZVEZDOV, when concluding your final statement you said several phrases very nervously. You said approximately that together with ANTONOV you reported on an assignment which KOTOLYNOV received \[74\].

Please clarify.

ZVEZDOV: I wished to confirm the indictment you presented to me, in that a whole series of assignments originating from KOTOLYNOV were given to us; and second, I considered it entirely necessary to emphasize here that, for example, on meeting NIKOLAEV in the Istmol building, I had a conversation with him which allowed one to judge that NIKOLAEV was preparing to commit his crime. I immediately passed this to SOKOLOV, and communicated it to ANTONOV, so I draw the conclusion that KOTOLYNOV met NIKOLAEV, and it was precisely this communication of mine that facilitated their encounter.

Further, I believe that the behavior at the very beginning of the trial was, of course, behavior in no way or degree worthy of a man who participated in a crime. Of course, it was not by chance that I knew KOTOLYNOV was preparing a terrorist act.

CHAIRMAN: What assignments did you receive from KOTOLYNOV?

ZVEZDOV: The assignments concerned collecting information at the Smolny. I had to collect information about the Smolny throughout 1934. By the nature of my work I was often at the Smolny. I needed to ascertain whether I might encounter any persons who had previously participated in opposition work and were now working at the Smolny. I reported one such name. Another assignment was to keep informed of the work of the city Party Committee itself. And third, I needed to report what measures were being carried out, particularly among students. I reported in a distorted presentation that…

CHAIRMAN: To whom did you report?

ZVEZDOV: To ANTONOV and KOTOLYNOV, that the second round of all-Union socialist emulation was being prepared, with such-and-such people participating directly, that this was being carried out on KIROV's instructions, and that this fact had to be used to discredit socialist emulation accordingly.

I also reported on unrest which occurred during 1934 in Ostrov District. And then some more conversations of approximately this kind about practical questions which I now find somewhat difficult to recall.

CHAIRMAN: Regarding the terrorist act, did you receive information from KOTOLYNOV or not?

ZVEZDOV: Regarding the terrorist act, to him — rather, not to him but to ANTONOV — first of all I reported the meeting with NIKOLAEV and that he had sentiments of a sort that would allow him to act; and I passed this information to ANTONOV.

CHAIRMAN: Why did you pass it to ANTONOV?

ZVEZDOV: So that he would pass it to KOTOLYNOV.

KOTOLYNOV: Chairman, may I?

CHAIRMAN: Perhaps ANTONOV will add something?

ANTONOV: ZVEZDOV could contact KOTOLYNOV directly, just like me; he knew him perfectly well. I received no assignments from KOTOLYNOV. After the conversation in September, I did not see him again. I declare this most sincerely.

CHAIRMAN: That information ZVEZDOV spoke of, which he passed on to be communicated to KOTOLYNOV, did you receive it? About the terrorist act being prepared?

ANTONOV: He spoke of sentiments.

CHAIRMAN: For what purpose?

ZVEZDOV: To pass it to KOTOLYNOV.

ANTONOV: In any event, I did not see KOTOLYNOV after that.

CHAIRMAN: But did you receive information for KOTOLYNOV?

ANTONOV: There was no such thing. There was simply a conversation, and he spoke of NIKOLAEV's sentiments; but there was no conversation about specifically passing this information to KOTOLYNOV.

KOTOLYNOV: Comrades, from the time they began speaking to me about NIKOLAEV and so forth, I simply did not believe it; I thought they were probing, searching for the true path. I spoke about this to both MIRONOV and DMITRIEV.

Comrades, in recent days everyone told me during the investigation: you are lying, all the threads lead to you, and you are delaying the investigation. And I told them, disarmed to the end. It is easier for me to say “yes” than to say “no.” And, comrades, for ten days I was in such a state of tension that death is not the most terrible thing for me, but… \[75\] the most malicious enemy of Soviet power.

What do I wish to say? I am literally on my knees before the court, and swear that I heard nothing from ANTONOV, ZVEZDOV, or NIKOLAEV about a terrorist act. In my statement I did not even say anything in defense. I said that I demand severe punishment despite my life having taken a very distorted course.

CHAIRMAN: The court retires to deliberate upon the sentence.

At 6:40 a.m. on 29 December 1934, the court returned from deliberation. The chairman pronounced the sentence.

The session was declared closed.
