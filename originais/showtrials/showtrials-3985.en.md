INTERROGATION RECORD

OF EVDOKIMOV, Grigory Yeremeyevich, January 2, 1935.

Question: What other persons connected with ZINOVIEV do you know?

Answer: Beginning in 1931, when visiting ZINOVIEV, I saw MAGYAR, a Comintern employee, at his home. I did not happen to be present during MAGYAR's conversations with ZINOVIEV. Circumstances were such that I always arrived at ZINOVIEV's at the end of his conversation with MAGYAR. I know that MAGYAR informed ZINOVIEV about the work of the Comintern and sent foreign newspapers to ZINOVIEV in exile in Kustanai. MAGYAR also met GORSHENIN. I personally spoke with MAGYAR only once, during a meeting of the Executive Committee of the Comintern in the spring of 1934, in passing, on the street. He briefly informed me about the work of the German Communist Party, and we agreed to meet at GORSHENIN's for more detailed conversations.

We did not manage to meet MAGYAR at GORSHENIN's home.

Question: The investigation has information that on November 6, 1934, you were at SHAROV's apartment with other Zinovievists. Tell us who was at SHAROV's with you and what the nature of the gathering was.

Answer: On November 6, 1934, I and others visited SHAROV at his invitation on the occasion of his fiftieth birthday. A very mixed company assembled. Among those present were SHAROV's comrades from his past revolutionary work—STRIZHEVSKY and others—and his closest Zinovievist friends: myself, BAKAYEV, KOSTINA and GORSHENIN. I remember an entire family from Leningrad at SHAROV's: a mother, daughter and the daughter's son. I do not know the surname of this family that came from Leningrad, nor do I know whether any of them had belonged to the Zinovievists in the past. In general, I did not know many of SHAROV's guests who were there on November 6, 1934, at all. Nobody spoke about politics that evening, since the circumstances ruled out such conversations.

Question: What can you say about the Zinovievists' conversations in connection with the suicide of BOGDAN, ZINOVIEV's former secretary, in the autumn of 1933?

Answer: At the time of BOGDAN's suicide, I was on leave. I learned of it also from GORSHENIN some time after returning from leave. All the Zinovievists with whom I spoke about it considered that BOGDAN's suicide resulted from his personal qualities and the cell bureau's improper treatment of him. We considered that he had done something foolish. It was also said that the general position of Zinovievists in the party affected BOGDAN painfully. After her husband's suicide, I visited BOGDAN's wife twice with GORSHENIN. As far as I remember, she had some document BOGDAN had left after his death, I think an unfinished statement to the cell bureau in which he accused it of treating him unjustly. I know that BOGDAN's wife intended to hand this document over to the cell bureau and that she visited ZINOVIEV and spoke to him about BOGDAN's state of mind before the suicide. She told ZINOVIEV that before his death BOGDAN had very much wanted to see him and that, possibly, if they had met, BOGDAN's fate would not have ended this way.

Question: What additional information can you give about your connections with Zinovievists living in Leningrad?

Answer: In the spring of 1933, Yakov TSEITLIN visited me, trying to obtain the specific political information usually circulated in our circles. I suggested that TSEITLIN read the Soviet press, and he left me embittered. After that, I met KUKLIN, who told me that TSEITLIN had visited him with someone else and that he, KUKLIN, had informed TSEITLIN about certain questions of party policy. I do not remember the details of my conversation with KUKLIN about his talk with TSEITLIN, but I remember my general impression from it: KUKLIN's conversation with TSEITLIN was anti-party in character.

EVDOKIMOV.

INTERROGATED BY RUTKOVSKY.

Certified correct: Churbanova
