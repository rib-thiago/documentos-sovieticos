TESTIMONY

OF GERTIK Artem Moiseevich, dated January 14, 1935.

I joined the Zinovievist opposition on the eve of the Fourteenth Congress. I took an active part in it and never withdrew throughout the entire period.

When a Zinovievist center was formed in Moscow after the Fourteenth Congress, I did not belong to it. It consisted exclusively of former members of the Central Committee and the Central Control Commission.

After the bloc with TROTSKY, in addition to the Zinovievist center, a joint center was also organized on a parity basis. Five or seven people from each side, with SMILGA as the eleventh or fifteenth.

I was invited to meetings of this center only when the corresponding number of members of the Zinovievist center was not present in Moscow.

In addition, I participated in expanded meetings of the Zinovievist center.

At the beginning of 1926 I was transferred to work in Moscow, and during 1926-1927, up to the Fifteenth Congress, my apartment was a center where Zinovievists who were in Moscow or came from the provinces gathered. Meetings were also held there. ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV attended, especially before their eviction from the Kremlin.

Soon after the Fifteenth Congress I was transferred to Tashkent, where I spent 1928-1929. Many Zinovievists visited my apartment, where my wife continued to live; meetings were held there, and some — for example BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, PERIMOV, PEKAR-ORLOV, and KORSHUNOV, who lived in Moscow — stayed at my apartment.

I came to Moscow from Tashkent only once, in the summer of 1928, when I was reinstated in the Party.

Before leaving for Tashkent again I traveled with FEDOROV to Kaluga to see ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV. My visit dates from the period of the first negotiations with the Rights (BUKHARIN), and the following directives were given in Kaluga then: we shall not conclude a bloc with the Rights, but shall use the negotiations to secure more favorable terms when negotiations with STALIN begin.

At that time ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV had no doubt that negotiations would begin soon, because in their opinion the economic situation was deteriorating (KAMENEV kept a special chart of grain procurements, which clearly showed that procurements in 1928 were going worse than in 1927), the struggle against the Rights was intensifying, and consequently ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV would soon be brought into responsible work, which would be a transitional stage toward their inclusion in the Central Committee and subsequently the Politburo.

After Kaluga I was in Leningrad, where a meeting of ten to twelve people was convened, I do not now remember by whom, LEVIN or MYASNIKOV, at which I gave information about my trip to Kaluga and about the need to preserve the Leningrad group in view of ZINOVIEV's impending early appointment to responsible work.

Then I went to Tashkent, where I also gave a report on my trip to Kaluga.

I returned permanently from Tashkent to Moscow in November 1929. I immediately established contact with ZINOVIEV and the other Zinovievists who were in Moscow at that time.

Most comrades arriving in Moscow from the provinces continued to visit my Moscow apartment, and Moscow Zinovievists gathered at my home too. This continued until the end of January 1930, when I moved to another apartment: two small rooms in a shared apartment, which did not offer the same conveniences for illegal work as the old apartment. At the new apartment I continued to be visited by the following out-of-town people: GORDON, until 1932; ELKOVICH and GESSEN — until early 1931 they usually stayed with me, then moved to GORSHENIN's and visited me only rarely; KORSHUNOV, from Leningrad, who also usually stayed with BRAVO after 1931; I do not now remember whether he visited me in 1932; Nikolai DMITRIEV also called; besides myself, I know that he also visited GORSHENIN and SAFAROV. MANDELSHTAM visited me once — I do not remember whether in 1930 or 1931 — brought by PERIMOV; after that I did not see him again. I last saw RUMYANTSEV in late 1929 or early 1930. He visited me at my old apartment on Arbat Square. I did not meet him again after that visit either. TSVIBAK and BASHKIROV also visited me when they came to Moscow. BASHKIROV usually stayed with KHARITONOV. LEVIN began visiting me in late 1931, when he moved to work at the LSPO and began traveling to Moscow; he also called in 1932.

Before 1932 I was rarely in Leningrad. As far as I can now recall, I went once in 1930 and saw none of the Zinovievists. I was there once or twice in 1931; I do not now remember whether I met any Zinovievists then besides TSVIBAK. I remember only that I first visited LEVIN's and BASHKIROV's apartments in 1932. In 1932 I visited LEVIN twice — I related the details in my previous testimony; I visited BASHKIROV twice, ANISHEV once or twice, and TSVIBAK several times.

Early in 1932 TSVIBAK was ill and in a psychiatric hospital. I visited him there and happened to meet KOTOLYNOV there. KOTOLYNOV told me about his studies, was enthusiastic about his specialty (metallurgical chemist), and predicted a great future for it; as far as I can now recall, there were no political anti-Party conversations with him, since the setting was unsuitable, KOTOLYNOV himself appeared ill, and TSVIBAK, who was undoubtedly ill, was there too.

After returning from exile I was in Leningrad once in 1933 and twice in 1934. During all these visits I visited only TSVIBAK, once in 1933, and had no political conversations with him, since two non-Party professors of some sort were with him at the time. After returning from exile I never met any other people from Leningrad, either in Leningrad or Moscow.

All my meetings with comrades, especially non-Muscovites, pursued a single aim: as far as possible to retain them as cadres of the Zinovievist organization, maintain in them hostile sentiments toward the present Party leadership, and strengthen their conviction that if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV had been brought into the leadership, many negative phenomena could have been avoided.

What was the aim of preserving the Zinovievist cadres? The aim was twofold: on the one hand, as the late LASHEVICH put it, “the leaders must not be left exposed,” and if ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV were brought into the leadership, it was necessary that certain cadres should surround them on whom they could immediately rely; on the other hand, we placed great hopes on disagreements in the Party leadership, believed that matters could lead to a discussion, and then we would immediately join in, having a ready command cadre.

The method by which we sought to retain cadres was tendentious information about the domestic and external situation, of an openly anti-Party character, which essentially amounted to saying that the Party leadership was not coping with its task. We had no program of our own that we could oppose to the Party's general line. We could not defend the notorious platform of the bloc: first, life itself had already proved its inadequacy; second, it was generally a compromise platform in which the Zinovievists had made great concessions to the Trotskyists. We could present no serious objections to the Party's general line in its fundamentals, and relied chiefly on the assertion that decrees and resolutions, mostly not bad in themselves, were put into practice badly, without planning or advance preparation, and at enormous cost.

Concerning the letter “Dizzy with Success,” we believed that it had come too late and that the Party leadership had undoubtedly already known about the bungling taking place locally. A rumor circulated among us that the secretary of the Moscow committee, BAUMAN, also believed that the Party leadership had not taken timely measures, and had been removed and sent to Tashkent for this.

In 1931 we said that peasants were starving and fleeing the kolkhozes by entire villages, abandoning them to their fate. GORSHENIN cited data on this and figures on the decrease in the number of kolkhozes. Many rumors circulated among us then about unrest and discontent among workers over food. In general, concerning the food situation, ZINOVIEV gave the following directive: there had been neither war nor crop failure, but because of poor leadership the food situation had deteriorated as much as if both had occurred together.

GORSHENIN cited many negative facts and figures of all kinds about the economic situation, evidently drawing them from Gosplan. I remember that PERIMOV also said concerning the construction of meat-packing plants that, both in size and in number, far more were being built than the slaughter of livestock during the first and second five-year plans would require, thereby immobilizing a large amount of capital. It was also said that large sums would be immobilized in the construction of Magnitogorsk and Kuznetsk, since there would not be enough ore for both plants to operate fully, and so on.

All this information was usually accompanied by conclusions that, had ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV been brought into the leadership, most of these mistakes would not have occurred.

Many rumors circulated among us about disagreements among members of the Party leadership. We never verified these rumors and spread them on trust.

A considerable amount of information came from ZINOVIEV, who was visited by many people, and partly from EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, GORSHENIN, and myself. It was disseminated among all members of the Zinovievist organization, as I have already said, usually without any verification.

The principal members of the Zinovievist organization — the center, among whom I must also include myself — met throughout the period quite regularly, at least once a month, sometimes twice, in groups of three to five. Until 1930 they gathered at my home, and then, as the other members moved to Moscow and acquired apartments, they began to gather more or less evenly at other apartments too.

Out-of-town people arriving in Moscow, as well as Muscovites who did not themselves visit ZINOVIEV, also gradually became distributed among individual comrades. Thus I was visited chiefly by ZILBERMAN, GORDON, and, until 1931, BRAVO; afterward BRAVO began visiting EVDOKIMOV, with whom he worked, more frequently. A circle of regular visitors also formed around GORSHENIN, BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, and others.

All my anti-Party counterrevolutionary work in this spirit in Moscow continued, as I have already said, from 1930 to 1932, until my arrest.

After exile I returned to Moscow with the most sincere intentions of breaking with everything old and beginning to work honestly in the Party; but my system was already so poisoned by many years of counterrevolutionary work deceiving the Party that my good intentions remained merely intentions. All my comrades in the group received me in Moscow with open arms, showed unusual attention to me, and every single one considered my behavior at the GPU and my testimony entirely correct. Thus BAKAEV, to whom I related my case and my testimony in detail, said: “You testified correctly. We met, and apart from conversations there was nothing else.” And to my decision not to meet members of the organization anymore, he replied: “That is pointless. Nobody will believe you anyway, you have nowhere to go, nobody but us will see you, and to the GPU

it makes absolutely no difference whether you meet EVDOKIMOV, BAKAEV, or GORSHENIN. What matters to them is that we should not meet ZINOVIEV, that he should be isolated.”

Thus the old ties and old relations gradually resumed.

However, in my profound conviction, our anti-Party, hostile attitudes toward the Party leadership softened considerably over the last year: first, because the political and economic situation of the USSR changed greatly for the better (plans for ferrous metallurgy, coal, and grain procurements were fulfilled completely), and even GORSHENIN, as far as I remember, no longer cited negative facts and figures; second, ZINOVIEV's appointment as a member of the editorial staff of “Bolshevik” fully satisfied us, since we regarded it as transitional work toward the Comintern.

Personally, after returning from exile I did not meet most comrades in the organization at all, including ZINOVIEV, and met the others, except BAKAEV and GORSHENIN, very rarely. Thus I had not seen EVDOKIMOV since May, GERTSBERG since July; during the whole period I visited KUKLIN and SHAROV once each. Nobody visited me at my apartment, since after returning I had no apartment of my own: I lived with my daughter.

A. GERTIK.

INTERROGATED BY —

ASSISTANT HEAD OF THE SPO OF THE GUGB — RUTKOVSKY
