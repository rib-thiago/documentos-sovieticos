INTERROGATION RECORD

OF SHVARTS Grigory Ilyich

31 December 1934.

SHVARTS Grigory Ilyich, born 1903, native of Mikhailovka village, Dnepropetrovsk Region; resident in Moscow, 8/12 Sushchevskaya Street, apartment 18; Jewish, citizen of the USSR, passport issued in Moscow by a police station. Deputy head of the national economic personnel sector of USSR Gosplan; father a merchant, died 1927; student before the revolution, employee afterward. Married. Wife Klavdia Iosifovna, party member, economist at Khlopkoproekt. Two children: daughter aged eleven, son four. Mother Berta Lvovna in Evpatoria; brother David SHVARTS (Mikhail Permsky), party member, works at military chemical courses in Moscow; sister Revekka SHVARTS-SOKOL, physician in Kharkov. Incomplete higher education, Plekhanov Institute of National Economy, Moscow. Party member since 1919. Expelled in 1928 for membership in the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc. Reinstated several months later in 1928. Received for the same reason a severe reprimand with warning from the Moscow Control Commission, annulled in 1930 by the Zamoskvorechye Control Commission. No convictions. Arrested for three days in December 1928 on suspicion of Trotskyist activity, then released. Registered in the Moscow–Oktyabrsky District. Did not serve in the Red Army or White Army.

QUESTION: The investigation has information that you returned to the party while retaining the Trotskyist-Zinovievist bloc's positions and belonged to a group of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization. What can you testify?

ANSWER: Yes, I acknowledge this. After returning to the party I effectively remained connected with the Zinovievist group alongside which I waged open counterrevolutionary struggle against the party in 1926–1927. It included ROZOVSKY \[1\], BULAKH V. S., ROGOV V. I., TARASOV I. I., FAIVILOVICH L. and MINAEV S. P. Until arrest I was most closely connected with BULAKH V. S. and ROZOVSKY S. V.

Preserving organizational connections, a common political orientation expressed in our double-dealing role within the party, and our common desire for ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV to return to party leadership: all this united us Zinovievists into cohesive groups joined in an organization of a definitely counterrevolutionary and treacherous character.

At the end of 1928, already a party member, I and BULAKH were arrested in Moscow for Trotskyist connections. We were released after three days, and on our release day, 31 December 1928, I, BULAKH and ROZOVSKY attended New Year celebrations with KAMENEV and ZINOVIEV. About thirty others attended. I remember only KHARITONOV and his wife. I do not remember the conversations, but the gathering's general character was counterrevolutionary. Mutual support, connections and information on other Zinovievists' circumstances, and particular interest in ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV's fortunes remained in our group until recently.

For example, TARASOV I. I. apparently settled in ROZOVSKY's apartment after returning to Moscow. I met him there very frequently. What did we discuss? We were interested in former “Zinovievists'” fortunes and each person's party and public standing.

In summer 1930 MINAEV S. P., returned to Moscow from Kursk, lived in my apartment. Our conversations had the same character. MINAEV apparently visited Leningrad then and informed us whom he had seen there. Later he told us he had moved first into trade-union and then party work at the Moscow Electrical Combine. We regarded “Zinovievists'” transfer into party work very positively, seeing it as occupying definite positions in the party. For that reason, I think, ROZOVSKY and BULAKH were so eager to work in the party apparatus (they worked two or three years in the former Zamoskvorechye District Control Commission and Workers' and Peasants' Inspectorate).

Meetings with ROGOV and FAIVILOVICH had the same character. We discussed whether “Zinovievists” were admitted to propaganda work and teaching political and economic sciences. Those unable to transfer entirely into party work were advised to undertake propaganda as a party assignment.

Our entire group's tactics inside the party consisted generally in gaining trust and a definite position by every means while retaining all former connections and “Zinovievist” cadres, double-dealing and creating another counterrevolutionary organization within the party, thereby betraying party unity's interests.

Correctly recorded from my words and read by me —

SHVARTS G. M.

INTERROGATED BY — KOGAN.

Certified correct: A. Svetlova
