INTERROGATION RECORD

Conducted by RUTKOVSKY on December 25, 1934.

PERIMOV Aleksei Viktorovich (supplementary), born in 1897, member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) since 1915. Expelled from the party in 1928 for participation in the opposition, readmitted in 1928. Representative of the People's Commissariat for the Food Industry for construction of glass-container factories.

Question: The investigation has established that you retained attitudes hostile to the party until the last days. Do you confirm this?

Answer: I considered party policy basically correct, but disagreed with the Fourteenth Party Congress's assessment of the Zinovievists' ideological and political positions on the peasant question. I also held the opinion that ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV could and should be employed more extensively in party work.

Question: What functions did you perform in the Zinovievist organization's illegal antiparty activities from the Fifteenth Party Congress until recently?

Answer: Early in 1928, together with YAROSLAV and GERTIK, I sent a letter from Tashkent to ZINOVIEV on behalf of the Tashkent group of Zinovievists. In this letter we noted the significant leftward turn in the Party Central Committee's policy and the need for the Zinovievist group to draw closer to the party without dissolving into it.

There in Tashkent, in the initial period after the Fifteenth Congress, I maintained connections with exiled Trotskyists for mutual information; in particular, I received from them a leaflet setting out ZINOVIEV's views on current aspects of party policy.

I gave this leaflet to GERTIK, who forwarded it to ZINOVIEV in Moscow. I finally broke connections with the Trotskyists in 1928. Early in 1930 I brought a letter from REINGOLD in Tashkent to KAMENEV. I do not know its contents; it may have been personal.

Subsequently I was not organizationally connected with any Zinovievists and received assignments from none of them, but participated in antiparty conversations with them, which gave the Zinovievists grounds to consider me one of their own.

Of political conversations with ZINOVIEV, I remember the following:

During one of my visits to Moscow from Tashkent, in the winter of 1929, I believe, I talked with ZINOVIEV about events in Afghanistan. ZINOVIEV considered that one cause of Amanullah Khan's failures was STARK's incorrect advice, given under erroneous directives from Comrade STALIN. In 1930, I believe, when discussing the food situation in Kazakhstan, ZINOVIEV remarked: “What GOLOSHCHEKIN, who sits as secretary at STALIN's will, costs the country.”

Later, around the spring of 1932, during my conversation with ZINOVIEV about German events, he considered that the Comintern was making insufficient use of revolutionary possibilities in Germany for fear of damaging state relations between the USSR and Germany, owing to the party leadership's incorrect policy deriving from the fundamental positions of building socialism in one country. ZINOVIEV regarded events in the North Caucasus in 1932 as a consequence of the party's previously insufficient struggle against kulak sentiments existing within it.

After the liquidation of the Labor Peasant Party, headed by KONDRATIEV, CHAYANOV [2] and others, ZINOVIEV said that as early as 1926 he had warned the party of the danger of Ustryalovism in the country, writing a special article on this subject in “Bolshevik.”

After ZINOVIEV returned from Kustanai, I saw him only once, in late March 1934. I spoke little with him. He told me about his moods in connection with his speech, brought back [2] from the Seventeenth Party Congress. From this meeting and conversation I gained the impression that ZINOVIEV had sincerely reconciled with the party and wanted to work harmoniously with it.

In criticizing party policy and the party leadership, ZINOVIEV did not counterpose any positive platform of his own to party policy in any conversation with me.

Question: In the record of December 11 of this year, you testified that BAKAEV and GORSHENIN retained dissatisfaction with the party leadership. Describe in detail what this consisted of.

Answer: I must stipulate that BAKAEV's antiparty attitudes were equally characteristic of me personally. All my conversations with him amounted to petty criticism of party policy on current questions. They can be characterized as philistine grumbling and gossip.

GORSHENIN was another matter. His antiparty attitudes were more deeply formed; when criticizing party policy, he always supported his criticism with figures, proving that our construction cost the country terribly dearly and that the party did not know how to manage the country's economy rationally.

Concerning our conversations at GORSHENIN's apartment in February 1933, I can additionally report that their antiparty essence consisted in accusing the party of excessive cruelty toward the Zinovievist Artem GERTIK, arrested, as it then seemed to us, without sufficient grounds, at an extremely difficult moment for him: his wife's death. In addition, KOSTINA told those present about her conversation with Comrade YAROSLAVSKY concerning her expulsion from the party and GERTIK's expulsion and arrest. She informed us that, according to YAROSLAVSKY, GERTIK had been expelled from the party for services rendered to the Zinovievist faction.

From everything KOSTINA told us, it was clear that in her conversation with Comrade YAROSLAVSKY she had denied the existence of the Zinovievist faction. Comrade YAROSLAVSKY did not insist on its existence, but considered that connections among former Zinovievists had been preserved and warned that this must be stopped. —

PERIMOV.

INTERROGATED BY RUTKOVSKY.
