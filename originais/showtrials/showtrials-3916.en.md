INTERROGATION RECORD

Of the accused ANISHEV Anatoly Isayevich, December 28, 1934.

In addition to my testimony of December 27 of this year.

1\. The main conclusion drawn after the Fifteenth Congress at joint meetings of the “Leningraders” and the “Trotskyists” was that the opposition had not been defeated ideologically. We in the localities (Samarkand–Tashkent) also made this assessment. See, for example, my theses for the meeting of the Samarkand group; the members of the center in Moscow also defended this proposition.

There followed a division along two tactical lines: one was to continue openly to “hold the banner,” “without losing face”; the other was to submit statements of repentance, return (chiefly, strictly speaking, the Trotskyists) to the Party, earn trust in order to preserve cadres, and wait for an opportunity to return to the leadership and pursue their line (Zinoviev, the “Leningrad group”).

Both sides equally preserved their distinct identity. Both sides equally continued to preserve the counterrevolutionary organization: some within the Party, others outside it. I belonged to and was connected with the Zinovievist group.

During my work in Central Asia (until the summer of 1931), I met several times with members of the Tashkent group (YAROSLAV, PERIMOV, and others) and, among the members of the center, with GERTIK. In both places, instructions were given about “honest” work in the Party, the need to work harmoniously and earn trust, and at the same time an assessment of the situation was offered to the effect that the Party was moving toward our positions, that STALIN, maneuvering, was compelled to retreat (the struggle against the Right), but that STALIN's maneuvers could ruin the Party and that cadres had to be preserved, the leaders ZINOVIEV and KAMENEV especially protected, and the situation not made more difficult by ill-timed statements criticizing the Party leadership.

I can say nothing about organizational contacts during this period, since I lived rather isolated in Central Asia. In 1930, only three remained in Tashkent (myself, DRONOVA, and RITMAN), inseparably connected with one another and constituting a separate counterrevolutionary group, working in the Party but preserving ourselves as a group that met constantly and discussed Party policy from its counterrevolutionary positions.

2\. My return to Moscow, and especially to Leningrad (late 1932), meant for me a strengthening of contacts with the Leningrad and especially the Moscow groups. My connection with NATANSON meant, in particular, drawing closer through her to the Moscow center of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization (BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, and also SAFAROV).

I found all these persons in Moscow connected with one another just as they had been before the Fifteenth Congress.

During the period from 1932 to the end of 1933, our work also amounted to the following:

a) continue to gain the Party's trust and sit tight;

b) cautiously minimize the Party's achievements and exaggerate difficulties and failures;

c) cautiously discredit the Party leadership;

d) maintain contact with members of the organization, preserve a common internal line and assessment of the political situation, and preserve cadres.

I shall show how this was carried out through the following facts known to me:

1\. Around the spring of 1932, GERTIK came to Leningrad to establish contact between the Moscow center and the Leningrad groups. GERTIK visited NATANSON and me, BASHKIROV, and LEVIN (I believe Mikhail LEVIN). The conversation at our home contained the following instructions: that the situation in the country, owing to STALIN's crude maneuvers, as he tried with his Left line to knock the weapon out of the opposition's hands (collectivization), was extremely difficult; that administrative methods in collectivization could lead to ruin, and so on. Information was given about uprisings in the villages and famine in Ukraine. Then the problem of leaders was discussed in the following terms: why does the revolutionary movement in the West, which is on the rise, not produce genuine revolutionary leaders and struggle through a series of failures? GERTIK's answer amounted to saying that the allegedly incorrect leadership of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and the Comintern was to blame.

This subject was generally a constant theme, the fundamental directive orientation of the Moscow counterrevolutionary center. The names of Comrade THÄLMANN in the West and Comrade KIROV here were cited as illustrations.

NATANSON and I put two questions to GERTIK: could we now counterpose our own positive program of action, and could we counterpose other leaders, our own, to STALIN?

To both questions, GERTIK answered that if ZINOVIEV, KAMENEV, and the group of “subleaders” (BAKAEV, KUKLIN, EVDOKIMOV, and so on) were brought into the leadership, there would be both a program and a correct Party policy. In doing so, he spoke of Comrade STALIN in a counterrevolutionary, hostile, slanderous spirit.

GERTIK gave instructions in the same spirit on his next visit as well.

2\. Among other directive visits to Leningrad, I shall note that of ELKOVICH, who, although he worked in Smolensk, was undoubtedly in Leningrad as a representative of the Moscow center shortly after the exposure of the RYUTIN–ZINOVIEV case.

ELKOVICH, while affirming the need to vote and even, if necessary, speak against ZINOVIEV, characterized the entire RYUTIN–ZINOVIEV case as a fraud organized by the Central Committee in order to divert the Party's attention from failures on the agricultural front and throughout the economic front, failures supposedly resulting from the Central Committee's incorrect leadership.

From ELKOVICH's conversations, it was clear that the purpose of his visit was to dispel the negative impression that ZINOVIEV's connection with the Right had made on many of us and to prepare the ground for recognizing the possible necessity of such blocs…

3\. In the summer of 1932, I visited BAKAEV, where KUKLIN and ELKOVICH were also present and, I believe, EVDOKIMOV called briefly. The conversations were strictly of a “tea-time” character, but dealt more cautiously with the same subjects. I had the impression that I had interrupted more serious conversations. A party at Comrade FEDOROV's was of the same character; BAKAEV, KOSTINA, GERTIK, NATANSON, and I were there. Since I worked in agriculture, conversations with me dealt chiefly with the situation in the countryside, and I reported what I knew about all sorts of shortcomings in the Party's agricultural policy and practice.

4\. SAFAROV's position in the organization seems somewhat apart to me. He apparently did not meet with ZINOVIEV, although it seems possible to me that his break was a tactical maneuver, like that of the Leftists among the Social Democrats.

I visited SAFAROV several times (and at BAKAEV's I was always asked about my conversations with SAFAROV). At one meeting with SAFAROV, around the spring of 1932, he asked me about the situation in Leningrad and expressed dissatisfaction that, living in Leningrad, I did not know about strikes and stoppages at Leningrad factories. I do not remember the details, but according to his information the strikes and stoppages had a mass character, and his dissatisfaction with my lack of information clearly implied the need for us to keep abreast of such events rather than feeding only on “official” newspaper information.

More often, conversations with SAFAROV concerned the Comintern, and SAFAROV's entire presentation of Comintern affairs always amounted to the conclusion that there were no leaders and that the leadership provided by the Comintern and the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) was weak.

Concerning SAFAROV, I shall note that the last time I visited him, after NATANSON's arrest, he asked me not to arrange meetings with him by telephone, as we had done before, but to meet while avoiding telephone conversations.

5\. BAKAEV, EVDOKIMOV, KUKLIN, SAFAROV, and KOSTINA all displayed an openly counterrevolutionary attitude toward the arrests of GERTIK and NATANSON and toward KOSTINA's expulsion from the Party for her statement on the ZINOVIEV–RYUTIN case. I do not remember the details, but what was common to us all was that this was a reprisal by the Stalinist guard, that everything against GERTIK and NATANSON had been fabricated.

We all berated KOSTINA for her foolish statement, correct in substance; foolish because, in any event, she had not and could not have told the whole truth, but had created difficulties both for herself and for all of us.

This entire atmosphere, all these sentiments and information, openly counterrevolutionary, all this was what nourished me politically throughout this time.

I had fewer connections with the Leningrad members of the counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization, not counting NATANSON, but both NATANSON and I served as a link between Leningrad and Moscow (I frequently visited Moscow for work).

TSEITLIN, LUKIN, KOTOLYNOV, ALEKSANDROV, ANTONEVICH, and someone else from the Polytechnic Institute called on us. I visited RUMYANTSEV several times. In the street, I met and conversed with MANDELSHTAM and BOGOMOLNY.

All the meetings and conversations, with varying shades, were a continuation of meetings and conversations and a transmission of the Moscow center's directives, however much we altered details of the assessment of the political situation in our own way. We in Leningrad constituted a cohesive counterrevolutionary organization. Here, as in Moscow, the forms of this organization were diffuse, in accordance with the tactics of quietly undermining, the tactics of double-dealing. We had no need for any formal registration of membership: we already knew one another; and I, like each of us, could meet and work with the others as members of the same organization without formal registration. No one appointed me to maintain contact with the Moscow center, but everyone knew that if I went to Moscow, I would visit GERTIK or BAKAEV, and so on.

Of greatest value to the counterrevolutionary organization was the position occupied by RUMYANTSEV. He was closer than the others to the Party bodies (the district committee) and, through his work, better informed than the others about the situation in Leningrad. I too visited him to obtain information.

I remember, for example, visiting RUMYANTSEV during the 1933 loan-subscription campaign. He told me that the loan was proceeding with great difficulty at factories in the Vyborg district, that the workers were dissatisfied, that the Party organizations were poorly connected with the workers, and that the district committee did not know the factories. He related that the secretaries had no authority among established workers, that there were old established workers, Party members, who avoided active work and promotion because of distrust of the leadership, and non-Party workers with whom he had old connections. We both assessed this to mean that, if the leadership in Leningrad were occupied not by people as alien to Leningrad as those now there, but by old Leningraders, that is, members of our organization, the situation would be completely different. This theme of bad leaders in Leningrad, their weak connection with the masses, and the contrast with what had existed “under us” and what could exist again if our people were returned to the leadership, was repeated most often. Discrediting and cultivating a hostile attitude toward the Leningrad leaders, especially Comrade KIROV, was a continuation of the same line toward the overall Party leadership, toward Comrade STALIN.

In particular, GERTIK, as I remember, and I related, when visiting Smolny, how empty and alien Smolny had now become; NATANSON, I, and, as I recall, TSEITLIN and LUKIN, after some festive demonstration, spoke of how there was now no connection between the ranks and the reviewing stands, and so on.

Being ourselves entirely alien to proletarian Leningrad, entirely cut off from proletarian Leningrad, we continued — even I, who had never in any sense been an old Leningrader — to consider ourselves the sole representatives of the Leningrad proletariat, called upon to break the evil fate of STALIN–KIROV, who, in our conception, were destroying Leningrad's revolutionary role and thereby the proletarian support of the entire Party.

Hence the assessment of the entire situation in the Party and country on the premise that we were not being given the opportunity to work where, in our view, we belonged, that is, in leadership on a local scale.

This theme was central at meetings at NATANSON's and my home with TSEITLIN and LUKIN. From TSEITLIN's work as manager of supplies at the Committee of the North to the little articles with which ZINOVIEV was compelled to “get by,” and on to generalizations about Party leadership. I do not now remember the details of one or another conversation, but the essence was always the same.

We were and remained members of a single counterrevolutionary Zinovievist organization and carried on the same counterrevolutionary work in Leningrad as everywhere else.

Our attitude toward NATANSON's arrest was the same as in Moscow.

I, in particular, after NATANSON's arrest, naturally went not to the Party organizations, not to the GPU, but to RUMYANTSEV, so that he would use his district committee connections to clarify the situation and my tactics in connection with NATANSON's arrest.

RUMYANTSEV unsuccessfully tried to discuss this in Party circles, but also learned of other arrests of members of our organization. On my tactics, RUMYANTSEV first “consulted” MANDELSHTAM, that is, essentially requested directives, after which he told me that I must sit and wait, which I did until NATANSON's banishment.

On external contacts.

I know of NATANSON's contacts with the representative of nationalist counterrevolution ABDURAKHMANOV \[1\]. This connection was a continuation of the connection with nationalists that we established in Central Asia. I was not present at NATANSON's meetings with ABDURAKHMANOV, but knew about them.

I know of NATANSON's contacts with Vladimir BOGUSHEVSKY \[2\], which, I think, were far from limited to the continuation of a simple acquaintance made at a health resort. NATANSON told me that these meetings were valuable for the information she obtained from BOGUSHEVSKY on the economic situation.

I think that SAFAROV's excursions to Central Asia were not simply sightseeing trips. I do not remember details or names, but SAFAROV spoke of meetings with some former Central Asian leaders.

On the murder of Comrade KIROV

From everything I have testified, from all that work of discrediting and fostering a spirit of hatred toward the Party leaders, particularly Comrade KIROV, it is entirely clear that politically NIKOLAEV's shot is connected with all our counterrevolutionary work. I shall not speak of my personal role in the counterrevolutionary organization: it is clear from what I have testified. I did not expose this counterrevolutionary organization to the Party in time, did not help the Party prevent what happened, and myself actively participated in all the counterrevolutionary work; I therefore bear political responsibility for everything, including the murder of Comrade KIROV.

Written in my own hand.

ANISHEV

Interrogated by: head of the 1st Section of the SPO of the UGB — LULOV.

Certified true: Polunina.

\[1\] This refers to Abdrakhmanov Yusup Abdrakhmanovich (1901–1938), a statesman and political figure of Kyrgyzstan, chairman of the Council of People's Commissars of the Kirghiz ASSR, member of the Central Asian Bureau of the Central Committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) and of the Central Executive Committee of the Union of SSRs. His diaries, published in 1991, contain entries about events in 1928–1932, as well as about his love affair with M. Ya. Natanson, whom he met during her exile in Tashkent. Yu. Abdrakhmanov was expelled from the Party on October 14, 1933, and arrested on April 4, 1937. He was sentenced to death by a traveling session of the Military Collegium of the USSR Supreme Court on November 6, 1937, and shot on November 7, 1937.

\[2\] Bogushevsky Vladimir Sergeyevich, born in 1895 in Yuryev, Livonia province. Member of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), delegate to its Seventeenth Congress. In 1925–1933, editor of the newspapers “Torgovye izvestiya” and “Za industrializatsiyu.” Head of the planning and economic department of the GAZ plant. At the time of arrest, lived at: Moscow, 1st Koptelsky Lane, house 9, apartment 40. Arrested on July 25, 1938. Sentenced to death by the Military Collegium of the USSR Supreme Court for participation in a counterrevolutionary terrorist organization and shot on March 31, 1939.
